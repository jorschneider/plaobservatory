# When access itself became the bargain: Geneva, London and export controls in 2025

**Developmental research memo, 15 September 2026.** This is a bounded reconstruction of two decisions in April–July 2025, with July trade volumes reported in August used as an implementation check. It is not a reconstruction of the later October controls or a statement of the rules in force in September 2026. The accompanying [source register](trade-2025-export-controls-register.json) records exact reading scope, original URLs, local captures and SHA-256 hashes. Source IDs below refer to that register. Existing trade and Phase One implementation registers were searched before acquisition; they did not contain this Geneva–London sequence.

## Finding and proposed use

This episode warrants a substantive section in Chapter 6. It supplies something the purchase-target story does not: evidence that access could improve while the Chinese administrative instrument capable of restricting that access remained in place. The usable concession was an export permission—or accelerated processing leading toward one—rather than necessarily the repeal of the underlying control. Across the Pacific, two company statements identify the removal of particular US restrictions and distinguish restoration under way from access actually restored. These are observable administrative acts, not merely an exchange of favorable summit descriptions. [EC25-06–08, EC25-18]

The case also changes whose implementation matters. Beijing described US restrictions as violations of the Geneva understanding; Washington described Chinese export delays as failures to deliver what it had agreed. Each side could obstruct transactions the other valued. A further negotiation specified reciprocal administrative relief. Calling this simply Chinese refusal to honor a signed bargain would suppress the disputed scope of the original undertaking, the US countermeasures, and the subsequent accommodation. Calling it a complete Chinese victory would suppress retained controls and the limited character of the disclosed relief. [EC25-02, EC25-15, EC25-10]

The strongest bounded conclusion is that control of difficult-to-replace industrial inputs gave Chinese negotiators a material issue on which the United States sought relief and accepted reciprocal relaxation. Public statements by both governments connect these objects of exchange; company records show part of the US relaxation reaching customers. The sources do not identify the Chinese instruction governing an individual license, the exact internal reservation positions, or what Washington would have done without rare-earth restrictions. A chapter can make a consequential argument without pretending to possess those records.

## Decision one: preserve the control, negotiate access through it

### What the April instrument did

On 4 April 2025, MOFCOM and the General Administration of Customs issued Announcement 18, stating State Council approval and taking effect immediately. It covered specified items associated with seven elements: samarium, gadolinium, terbium, dysprosium, lutetium, scandium and yttrium. Particular permanent-magnet materials were included. This was neither a prohibition of every rare-earth export nor, on its face, a rule confined to US destinations.

The operative distinction appears at the end of the notice. Exporters had to seek permission from the State Council's competent commercial authority; customs could question classification, and goods would not be released during that questioning. A firm therefore needed more than an assurance that trade was politically welcome. It needed a product classification accepted by customs and an applicable export license. The notice invoked security and nonproliferation; it did not disclose an instruction to deny US applications until a particular American concession arrived. [EC25-01: original Chinese notice, opening and final three substantive paragraphs; Hong Kong government reproduction, pp. 1–4]

### What Geneva promised—and left unspecified

The 12 May joint statement, following meetings on 10–11 May, required specified actions by 14 May. Its tariff provisions should be kept separate from its non-tariff provision: the parties suspended 24 percentage points of the relevant April tariff rates for an initial 90 days, retained 10, and removed specified subsequent escalations. China also undertook to suspend or remove non-tariff countermeasures taken against the United States since 2 April. The statement did not identify Announcement 18, list rare-earth products, publish an application queue, or set a deadline for each license. It established further talks under He Lifeng, Scott Bessent and Jamieson Greer. The Chinese ministry's same-day explanation likewise described reciprocal tariff adjustments and continued consultation. [EC25-02–03]

This wording matters because formal coverage and practical availability were different questions. A nationally framed control could remain on the books while officials facilitated eligible US transactions. Conversely, formally maintaining a licensing process could frustrate the commercial effect of a promise to remove a countermeasure if applications did not progress. The released joint statement alone cannot decide what the delegations privately understood about that distinction.

Reuters supplied a valuable, narrower piece of process evidence on 5 June. One person briefed on Geneva told its reporters that China had privately accepted the rare-earth controls as non-tariff countermeasures. That report gives reason to investigate the original scope dispute rather than assume it was only an American misunderstanding. But it remains one unidentified account, not a jointly released clarification or a negotiating minute. The same article discusses application burdens and reported increases in administrative staffing; it expressly leaves current approval authority uncertain. It is evidence against treating every delay as a demonstrated tactical choice. [EC25-25]

The Chinese public account was different in emphasis. On 29 May, when asked when the April controls would be suspended or removed, MOFCOM restated their dual-use rationale and referred the questioner to the governing law; it supplied no repeal condition. On 2 June it said China had implemented Geneva and accused the United States of subsequently introducing discriminatory restrictions, naming AI-chip guidance, EDA sales restrictions and Chinese student visas. These are evidence of the position China advanced and the remedies it demanded, not an independent adjudication of either party's compliance. [EC25-23: CNBC and Phoenix TV questions; EC25-15]

### Reopening the operational terms

The official Chinese readout of the 5 June Xi–Trump call says Xi called for reversal of negative US measures and that the leaders agreed their teams would continue implementing Geneva and meet again promptly. It does not disclose a rare-earth licensing schedule. This is a documented connection between leader-level intervention and further talks, not a private instruction granting He Lifeng unrestricted authority to bargain. [EC25-16: substantive readout]

London on 9–10 June produced agreement in principle on an implementation framework. The Chinese meeting account lists Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick alongside Bessent and Greer. That records a changed delegation composition; it does not by itself establish why Lutnick was added or which official prevailed. MOFCOM's 27 June account then describes continued team contact after London and further confirmation of details following approval—“经批准.” It pairs lawful consideration of eligible controlled-item export applications with corresponding US cancellation of a series of restrictions. The public account does not identify the approving Chinese body or release the detailed framework. Its sequence is nevertheless more informative than a single end-of-meeting announcement: agreement in principle, further communication, approval, confirmation and implementation remained distinguishable steps. [EC25-05–06]

The US account also connects the exchange. Reuters' 26–27 June report attributes to Bessent the explanation that mineral flows had not resumed as agreed and that Washington had imposed countermeasures. It records an administration official describing an additional understanding on expedited shipments. The important convergence is the reciprocal object of the accommodation, not identical explanations of who broke the earlier promise. These comments do not authenticate an undisclosed annex or establish that every affected company would receive permission. [EC25-10]

## Decision two: remove particular American restrictions and restore services

The most concrete company-level comparison is in EDA, the software and associated technology used to design semiconductors.

| Stage | Synopsys | Siemens |
|---|---|---|
| Restriction identified by the company | BIS letter received 29 May 2025 | BIS letter received 23 May 2025 |
| Subsequent change | On 2 July, received a BIS letter rescinding the restrictions immediately | By its 3 July statement, notified that the May restrictions no longer applied |
| Operational statement | Working to restore access to the recently restricted products | Had restored access and resumed sales and support for software/technology under ECCNs 3D991 and 3E991, subject to applicable rules |

These are original company statements, not copies of the BIS letters. They support the occurrence and the different implementation stages the companies reported; they do not disclose the full regulatory instructions or prove restoration to every customer. In particular, Siemens' classification-specific statement cannot be enlarged into a claim that Washington removed all semiconductor restrictions or granted China access to the most advanced AI chips. [EC25-08, EC25-18]

On 4 July MOFCOM said the teams were implementing the London framework, China was reviewing eligible license applications, and the United States had acted to remove restrictions and notified China. The reporter's question named EDA software, ethane and aircraft engines; the ministry's answer did not individually certify every named product. The EDA company records therefore do work that the political reply cannot: they identify particular recipients of US relief. This memo does not promote the question's other examples into independently verified cargo deliveries. [EC25-07]

Nor was relief necessarily comprehensive on the Chinese side. Reuters' 15 June original reporting, based on two people briefed on the London outcomes, says the fast track concerned non-military applications and describes six-month licenses and a prospective channel for trusted US firms. Its report of an apparent Chinese attempt to connect military-use rare-earth access with advanced AI-chip restrictions is significant process evidence, but the attempted linkage is not an accepted bargain. The same article records Bessent's rejection of a quid pro quo. No license instrument or agreed text establishing a universal six-month term was recovered here. [EC25-17]

The safe inference is correspondingly precise: the public framework was followed by specific US rescissions, and one provider reported service resumption. The underlying reciprocal understanding is supported by contemporaneous official explanations. The evidence is insufficient to assign a value to the exchange, determine which side conceded more, or treat later changes concerning particular AI chips as part of the same completed package. Those would require a separate dated reconstruction.

## What reached firms, and why the global scope matters

China had publicly acknowledged approving some compliant applications on 7 June, before London. Later licenses cannot all be credited to the 9–10 June talks, and a before/after comparison using London as a sharp treatment date would be misleading. [EC25-04]

The European automotive suppliers' association CLEPA reported on 4 June that some European production lines and plants had stopped, with only roughly a quarter of hundreds of applications apparently approved. It also complained of inconsistent procedures and demands for commercially sensitive information. This is an affected industry's account and advocacy statement, not a representative audit of the Chinese queue. It establishes concrete reported costs and concerns beyond the bilateral US relationship. [EC25-19: p. 1]

By 27 June, CLEPA's Nils Poel told Reuters issuance had accelerated, while routes involving US end users or third countries such as India remained slower. Volkswagen reported stable supply and Stellantis relief from its immediate concerns. In the same report, Dexter's CEO described only five approvals from 180 applications, all for non-defence sectors. These observations support uneven improvement, not a uniform reopening. The reporting does not isolate whether each difference arose from end-use review, incomplete paperwork, staff constraints or political prioritization. [EC25-09]

MOFCOM's 10 July briefing supplies a useful primary check on the parallel European track: it said a green channel had been established to accelerate approval for European enterprises. A US–China accommodation was therefore operating within a Chinese export-licensing regime covering all destinations and alongside other diplomatic arrangements. It would be wrong to represent London as a bilateral switch controlling all world shipments or to attribute relief for European firms solely to American bargaining. [EC25-24: Global Times question and export-control paragraph]

A later shipment measure prevents the analysis from stopping at promises. Reuters' report of Chinese customs data, updated on 20 August, gives July rare-earth magnet exports of 5,577 metric tons globally, including 619 to the United States and 1,116 to Germany; US shipments were above their July 2024 level. This is evidence that physical flows recovered substantially. It is not an independently downloaded customs microdataset, does not identify particular controlled elements or military end uses, and cannot establish fulfillment of all applications or replenishment of all inventories. The denominator is magnet exports, not every controlled rare-earth item. [EC25-11]

## Mechanism: what the case establishes and what competes with it

The evidence sustains three connected propositions.

First, bargaining concerned administratively delivered access. The Chinese authority could approve an eligible export without dismantling the regulatory basis for scrutinizing the next transaction. This makes implementation an object of negotiation in its own right. A commitment to restore commerce needed translation into decisions distributed across firms, products, end users and customs procedures. The software example illustrates the same distinction on the US side: rescission and usable service were separate observable events.

Second, threatened disruption had a material basis outside the negotiating room. The IEA's October 2025 analysis estimates China's 2024 share at about 91 percent of refining of the four magnet rare earths it measures; its accompanying production chart also distinguishes mining, refining and magnet manufacturing. The exact denominator matters: this is not a claim that China mined 91 percent of all rare earths. Industrial dependence helps explain why licenses became valuable bargaining objects. It cannot identify the instruction behind a particular delay. [EC25-14: first rare-earth section and chart note]

Third, the exchange was interactive. Chinese restrictions were followed by American restrictions whose removal China sought; continued talks produced administrative accommodations. An analyst who codes only Chinese delay and subsequent Chinese approvals will miss both the American instrument and the Chinese benefit. The design should trace the two decision chains together, not use a tariff headline as a proxy for leverage.

The competing explanations are substantive. Screening dual-use transactions can impose real informational work. The immediate extension of licensing to newly covered items can overwhelm processing capacity. Applicants may struggle with classification or decline to provide requested information. Officials may also use the same process to exercise pressure. These explanations can coexist. The chronology and public pairing establish an exchange over access, while the present records do not identify the mixture behind individual delays. Resolving that mixture would require application dates, completeness determinations, interagency referrals, decision reasons and shipment releases—not simply interviews asserting that China could have moved faster.

The strongest contrary evidence also limits claims of Chinese control over the outcome. Approvals preceded London; some customers subsequently reported relief; shipments recovered; and the reported attempt to obtain advanced-chip relaxation did not establish an accepted exchange. China could impose costs, but this record does not show that it could convert every dependency into its preferred concession. Costs to Chinese suppliers and incentives for buyers to diversify also make short-run leverage different from durable advantage.

## What is new relative to the book, and what is not

Rare-earth export restriction was not invented in 2025. Bown's May 2025 working paper places the current measures in a longer history that includes the 2010 Japan episode and later diversification. This memo has not reconstructed that episode or adjudicated the contested mechanics of an informal Japan-specific interruption. The WTO's DS431 case summary establishes a narrower formal precedent: litigation over export duties, quotas and trading restrictions on rare earths and other materials, with China reporting removal of the condemned measures in May 2015. Those proceedings must not be conflated with a ruling on every allegation concerning Japan in 2010. [EC25-13: section 3.2, pp. 10–14; EC25-26: measures and implementation sections]

The more defensible change is the combination of industrial position, the specific contemporary control architecture and transaction-level relief exchanged with Washington. The IEA describes increased Chinese concentration in magnet manufacturing over the preceding two decades. The April notice demonstrates the legal and administrative machinery used in this episode. Neither fact requires an assertion that Chinese officials newly discovered coercion, secrecy or linkage. [EC25-14; EC25-01]

Bohman, Wong and Ferguson's June 2025 brief offers a useful interpretation: formal and informal instruments can be combined, with a legal basis established before pressure is calibrated through application. Their comparative sanctions dataset gives that argument a wider basis than this one case. But the brief's private-process claims largely follow identified news reports; its third-country coercion account is partly hypothetical. It is not an additional witness to the London room. Its timeline also mislabels the April rare-earth round as 14 March and the Geneva deal as 6 May; both errors are visible in the downloaded PDF. Use the primary notices and joint statement for dates. [EC25-12: pp. 3–9 and notes 17, 25–41]

For the [Solomon/Freeman baseline](../../book/context-claims.json), this is a productive extension rather than a national-character verdict:

- **S-C18:** the case shows why interests and material position must come before claims about interpersonal tactics. It does not estimate tactics' marginal effect.
- **S-P04 and S-P13:** [agenda linkage and costly pressure](../../book/process-claims.json) remain useful questions. Here the instruments are identifiable, while the reported advanced-chip linkage remains short of demonstrated success.
- **S-P25:** continued implementation bargaining is established. The full proposition also concerns Chinese demands for counterpart adherence while seeking adjustment for itself. The reciprocal framework and mutual compliance accusations do not, without the missing private terms, establish that asymmetry. Do not upgrade the proposition merely because further bargaining occurred.
- **F04:** talks abroad and subsequent approval are compatible with centrally bounded delegation. They do not prove either unchanged micromanagement or newly autonomous negotiators.

## Integration and further tests

In [Chapter 6](../chapters/06-after-the-signature.md), place this episode after the discussion of contested enforcement and before the 2026 mechanism. The contrast with finance and agricultural approvals is substantive: administrative authority can deliver the promised opportunity and retain the capacity to condition later access. This is a stronger bridge than another paragraph saying agreements require follow-through.

A compact chapter section can use the April notice, Geneva clause, June reciprocal framework, July company comparison and shipment check. Keep the full source criticism here and in notes. Chapter 5 needs at most a forward reference explaining that the 2020 trade settlement does not exhaust the later repertoire; a second 2025 narrative there would duplicate Chapter 6.

For practitioners, the finding yields questions tied to observable performance: which authority can issue the permission, which product and end user it covers, how long it lasts, what customs still requires, and whether the firm can use it. A further question concerns reciprocity: what exact administrative action must the counterpart undertake, and when does it become effective? These are bargaining-design implications from the case, not instructions for complying with current export-control law.

The highest-value missing records are the detailed Geneva and London understandings; the Chinese approvals underlying the June framework; the relevant BIS letters; and a small matched set of applications, licenses and shipment clearances. Such material could distinguish bargaining-induced acceleration from routine queue improvement and determine whether the parties knowingly accepted different descriptions of the same arrangement. Until then, the positive argument is already usable: significant relief was exchanged through administrative instruments, and recovery of access did not itself mean surrender of the authority to restrict it.
