# EP-3: bargaining over the letter, the authority and the exchange

Research and complete reading: 16 September 2026. Nine publicly released State Department cables, 63 physical PDF pages, read as extracted text; consequential pages visually checked as specified in the [register](ep3-drafting-cables-register.json). All 46 catalogue entries in case F-2010-07070 were inventoried, but only these nine documents were acquired and read. The catalogue is not a complete record of the crisis. Original scans and text are preserved outside Git with hashes. No intermediate full draft of the Prueher letter has been recovered.

## What the new record changes

The earlier chapter reconstructed the public outcome and used later participant interviews to approach the negotiation. These cables establish a contemporary, dated series of exchanges over the settlement. They show contested wording, asserted clearance, consultation with capitals, a separate document certifying American authority, and the practical exchange required to turn a letter into a crew departure. They also show an implementation document being renegotiated after the Chinese official presenting it said there was no time for changes.

This is a substantial improvement in the evidence, with a specific boundary. The records are American reports of conversations with Chinese officials. Their authors sometimes supply openly conjectural comments about Chinese leaders' motives or timetable. Those comments cannot become unqualified statements of what happened inside Beijing. An interlocutor saying that a draft has high-level clearance is evidence of the assurance given to the American negotiator; it is not possession of the clearance itself.

## The dispute before the draft

On April 1 in Washington, Marc Grossman emphasized immediate communication with the crew, access, protection of the aircraft and eventual return. His report says Powell had tried unsuccessfully to contact Qian Qichen. Yang Jiechi said he was instructed to repeat Zhou Wenzhong's earlier points and promised to report Grossman's demands. The unsuccessful senior contact and the continuing ambassadorial encounter belong in the same account. Neither proves why the senior contact failed. The cable was transmitted April 2 UTC and reports an April 1 Washington meeting; the dates must not be collapsed. [EPD01, paras 2–14]

The April 2 Prueher–Zhou report contains more than an abstract dispute over responsibility. Zhou gave an account of the aircraft movements and damage, claimed evidence from the surviving Chinese pilot and examination of the EP-3, and advanced legal arguments for detention and investigation. Prueher disputed both the mechanics and the legal conclusions and demanded access to his own crew. The account refutes any simple contrast in which Americans alone discuss physical causes while Chinese officials discuss only relations or consequences. It does not establish the truth of either account of the flight. [EPD02, paras 3–20]

On April 4, Tang insisted on an explanation and apology before China could consider releasing the crew. He invoked public anger and American statements; Prueher challenged the role of Chinese official media in inflaming that anger. These are opposing representations about the public constraint, not independent measures of its intensity. Prueher tried to make his aviation expertise bear on the Chinese account. Tang continued to assert that facts and evidence supported China, while describing resolution as a diplomatic matter. Both wanted a prompt outcome; that common preference did not establish its terms. [EPD03, paras 5–7,9–18,20–34]

An additional continuity matters for the chapter's later comparison. Asked about daily contacts and use of the Military Maritime Consultative Agreement, Tang said meetings depended on need and questioned their purpose if the Americans began by denying what China called facts. Political conditions on discussion were already visible in this 2001 encounter. The later 2022–24 evidence should therefore establish the treatment of particular institutions, not introduce conditional access as an invention of the Xi period. [EPD03, para 19, physical p.10]

## April 7–9: authority did not eliminate further bargaining

At 9:30 a.m. on April 7, Zhou described China's response to the previous evening's US draft as formally cleared at a high level. Prueher objected especially to reduced clarity about when the crew and aircraft would return. A new US-cleared text followed at noon. Zhou said a proposed special envoy was optional and should not be linked to release. He promised formal oral assurances from Tang; Lu Shumin and Zheng Zeguang, in side conversations, said release would follow Tang's response immediately. The cable does not reproduce the intermediate texts, and “immediate” release in these discussions is not a record of the eventual departure time. [EPD04, paras 1–7]

At the April 8 meetings, the American decision not to send a special envoy generated a more concrete solution to the authority question. Zhou requested a brief letter from Powell to Qian confirming Prueher's full authority. Prueher agreed. Its delivery was subsequently set for the meeting with Tang, with authority to be confirmed earlier. This was separate from the Prueher-to-Tang settlement letter. [EPD05, paras 2–4]

Zhou continued to press for *apologize*, identifying it as the leaders' position. At 8 p.m., after consulting Powell, Prueher rejected the word and delivered a revised draft incorporating other Chinese proposals. Zhou said he would report immediately. He also said the follow-on delegation and agenda required consultation with the navy and General Staff; leadership approval of the whole package preceded discussion of departure details. He referred to approval by traveling leaders. These are unusually specific contemporary descriptions of the constraints presented to the American negotiator, but the report cannot establish which constraint caused any particular day's delay. The embassy's own comment calls the leadership's internal reasons a presumption. [EPD05, paras 1–9]

On April 9, Zhou conveyed Chinese leaders' objections to American discussion of consequences for the Olympics, trade status, WTO entry and Taiwan arms. Prueher replied that prolonging the detention made the political damage worse. Zhou again sought an apology, now specifically for entry without permission, along with other strengthening changes. Prueher warned that the existing draft might itself become harder to sustain and suggested adding Chinese interception tactics to the future agenda. After another consultation with Powell, he accepted one minor change and rejected the others. The report does not identify that minor change. Zhou acknowledged improvement from the first American draft, maintained that the current version was not entirely satisfactory, and promised a response after a decision. [EPD06, paras 2–5]

The three reports permit a stronger conclusion than “approval mattered.” Both negotiators carried proposals to superiors, and both invoked the limits of their governments' tolerance. Claimed high-level clearance on April 7 did not make the Chinese wording immutable. Nor did American willingness to revise mean willingness to accept the disputed apology. The observable sequence is selective movement within defended limits. Which private consideration ultimately secured approval remains unresolved.

Washington also tried to keep its offer coherent. On April 10, when Gao Yanping raised the incident and congressional pressure with James Keith, Keith declined further substantive argument, explicitly deferring to Prueher's authorized discussions. He told Gao not to interpret that restraint as acquiescence in China's points. This is evidence of an American effort to preserve one bargaining channel, even as other representations continued. It is not proof that every US official followed the same discipline. [EPD07, paras 1–10, especially 5]

## April 11: the exchange was still being negotiated

The report of three preparatory meetings on April 11 was transmitted April 13. At 9:30 a.m., Lu confirmed that Tang would receive Prueher and sought an advance photocopy of the signed letter. The American deputy chief of mission objected that giving a signed copy could amount to delivering the letter. At 3 p.m., James Moriarty supplied an unsigned copy and resisted Zheng's renewed request for the signed version. Zheng framed the request as protection against misunderstanding at the ministerial meeting. The Americans were protecting the relationship between delivery and the assurance they expected to receive. The cable establishes both rationales; it does not establish a Chinese plan to misuse the copy. [EPD09, paras 2–3,10]

Moriarty also insisted on enough information about Tang's release assurance to conclude the bargain. He warned that Prueher could leave the meeting without agreement. Zheng declined to give Tang's exact words. At a further meeting at 4:50 p.m., he specified that the charter aircraft could arrive within twenty hours of delivery, perhaps fourteen or fifteen, and explained that the necessary procedures concerned identifying the crew and signing a transfer memorandum. This reduced the uncertainty while leaving the minister's exact language undisclosed. The embassy's description of Zheng as under pressure is the reporter's observation, not access to Zheng's instructions. [EPD09, paras 11–14]

The transfer memorandum was itself negotiable. Lu had called it simple and said there was no time to change it. The deputy chief of mission nevertheless withheld agreement. After lunch, he and Zheng revised the text by phone: they inserted *verbal* in the description of the missing landing clearance and removed thanks for China's appropriate treatment of the crew. Those are reported changes, not a recovered full before-and-after pair of documents. The episode is still a direct counterexample to treating an official's claim of drafting finality as sufficient evidence that revision is impossible. [EPD09, paras 6–7, physical p.4]

At 5:30 p.m., Prueher gave Tang Powell's letter to Qian citing presidential authorization. Before handing over his own letter, he asked Tang to confirm that the crew could leave. Tang asked for the signed original first; Zhou said the assurance was in Tang's prepared response. Prueher stated his understanding that the crew would be free and handed over the letter. The sequence is more precise than either “an unconditional American apology” or “China released the crew before delivery.” [EPD08, paras 2–4]

Tang then read a prepared statement that retained China's account of American responsibility while permitting departure after necessary procedures. The cable records Tang using *daoqian* in Chinese where the interpreter used *very sorry* in English. This language difference was observed inside the meeting, not only in subsequent public presentation. Prueher explicitly dissented from China's account. When he asked about the procedures, Tang anticipated departure the next day; Zheng then confirmed permission for the charter's 6 a.m. arrival on April 12. The cable also reproduces the final Prueher letter. [EPD08, paras 5–14]

## Consequences for the manuscript and the remaining archive

The chapter should now distinguish a sequence of reported draft negotiations from the still-missing full intermediate texts; a documented delivery of Powell's authority letter from its still-unacquired complete wording; and contemporary Chinese statements about clearance from an actual internal authorization record. The previously noted secondary-source identification problem needs the same precision: Powell–Qian correspondence existed, but the reproduced settlement letter is Prueher–Tang.

The sources strengthen four practical observations: test the concrete act a proposed formulation purchases; make authority verifiable; protect the timing of delivery and reciprocal assurance; and inspect implementation documents for wording that changes the bargain. None requires assuming an immutable Chinese national negotiating personality. Several are demonstrated by what the Americans did. The Chinese side's continued defense of its position coexisted with movement in the form of apology, the handling of authority, the release arrangements and transfer wording.

Still unacquired are the April 6 initial text, the complete April 7–9 revisions, the full authority letter, the transfer memorandum versions, and Chinese internal approvals or a corresponding Chinese meeting record. The 46-entry catalogue also identifies promising later aircraft-return cables, including the April 25 joint-terms instructions and April 25–26 disagreements over a proposed document. These are metadata leads only in this pass. The new State collection does not establish acquisition of any of the separate Bush Library finding aid's underlying records.
