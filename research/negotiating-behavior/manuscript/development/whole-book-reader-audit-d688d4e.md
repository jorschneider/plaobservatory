The manuscript now has a defensible answer. Political limits have long coexisted with negotiable accommodations; the practical difficulty is to discover the available exchange and judge its remaining exposure. What changed is partly China's capacity to make demands and supply benefits consequential: using WTO procedures against powerful partners, assembling legal expertise, and controlling inputs on which foreign production depends. The account of Xi-era supervision is narrower than a claim of universal rigidity, as it should be. The missing May 2019 draft still prevents a satisfying causal account of that reversal, but the book no longer pretends otherwise.

The best passages make this argument through decisions: Woodcock checks what Deng has accepted; Lamy seeks confirmation of a limit; Prueher withholds a signed copy and then accepts an imperfect assurance; Gackle and Cooper want different responses to the same failure. These scenes earn the reader's attention. The remaining obstacles are chiefly how the book assembles and explains them. It still sometimes feels like several accomplished case studies surrounded by successive interpretations, rather than one historical argument acquiring force. I would make the following three changes before adding another case.

## 1. Let the conclusion answer the historical question before revisiting the craft

**Anchors:** conclusion, “The price of a limited bargain” through “Performance and the cost of complaint” (blocks 005–028), followed by “What the update requires” (029–039).

The conclusion's first three substantive sections use roughly **1,660 words** to revisit lessons the reader has just encountered. The explicit historical synthesis arrives after them. This order weakens the answer to the book's title: what survives and what changes initially sounds like “understand the agreement and its risks,” with the distinctive historical finding delayed. The material does not generally exceed its sources; its arrangement hides what the evidence has accomplished.

**Concrete revision:** move the historical assessment forward, immediately after the opening London example. Lead with the distinction already established by blocks 030–039: sophisticated conditional bargaining was present in the 1970s; China's later legal capacity and industrial position changed the available instruments and the cost of refusal. State the Xi-era institutional finding at its actual strength. The historical section should be an argument, not another chronological tour.

Then retain only two sustained practical consequences: **a completed benefit versus continuing permission**, and **authority sufficient to act without complete certainty**. The latter deserves the Prueher ending. Enforcement can be incorporated through the Gackle–Cooper choice and the complainant's exposure, rather than a third review of every performance case.

Cuts that would make room without removing a material limit:

- Remove the separate Lamy recap beginning “Lamy's response to the ownership ceiling” (008); the specific encounter has already carried Chapter 2. Keep its example in a note if required by the shortened comparison.
- Cut the accession-delegation recap beginning “Both sides' authority mattered” (013) and the separate Kissinger recap (019). Neither develops the much stronger authority argument supplied by the newly recovered Prueher sequence.
- Reduce the finance/seed/purchase recap (021–023) to one contrast, preserving the point that delivery in one sector neither excuses nor disappears because of failure in another.
- Compress the 1995–Phase One clause recitation (033) to its historical implication and exact difference. Chapter 5 has already taught the mechanism.

An opening to the reordered assessment could say: “The update begins with a change in China's position, not the discovery that its diplomats bargain over details. The 1970s records already contain legal objections, conditional offers and precise exchanges. Later Chinese negotiators acquired additional ways to make refusal costly and concessions valuable: treaty litigation supported by a professional network, and decisions about supplies needed by foreign industry.” This is a synthesis of the existing cases, not a claim that every sector or counterpart followed the same trajectory. About **600–800 words** of recap could disappear while the conclusion became more decisive.

## 2. Finish the enforcement story before moving to China's power over supplies

**Anchors:** Chapter 6 opening (002–005); “Buying a number” (034–040); “Permissions in exchange for permissions” (041–062); “The cost of enforcing an agreement” and “Negotiating the next mechanism” (063–071).

The opening asks a strong question: why do two intended beneficiaries of the same agreement disagree about enforcing it? Finance, biotechnology and purchases establish what was delivered and what remained contested. But just when the chapter should return to Gackle and Cooper, it begins a separate **approximately 1,760-word** export-control story, running from spring 2025 into September 2026. The hearing finally returns afterward. The reader has to retain the opening dilemma through a second conflict, a different reciprocal bargain and several summits.

This also understates the licensing case's importance. It is the book's clearest demonstration of a change in China's bargaining position, yet structurally appears as an interlude in an argument about enforcing Phase One. The chapter then ends on proposed Boards and the older enforcement choice, leaving its most consequential historical development behind.

**Concrete reorder, without a new chapter or new evidence:** opening → finance → seeds → purchases → enforcement hearing and proposed Boards → supply permissions. Finish the Gackle–Cooper question while its commercial stakes are still fresh. Compress the Boards material into that unresolved choice. Then turn explicitly from the costs of enforcing a Chinese promise to the costs of obtaining relief from a Chinese restriction. End with the latter's choice between immediate licenses and a more durable opening.

The necessary bridge is short: “Washington was deciding how much pressure to apply over promises China had not fulfilled. In another negotiation, it needed Chinese decisions that could restore supplies to American industry. The government able to impose a cost in one dispute was also seeking relief in another.” This does not identify a cross-dispute quid pro quo; keep the existing sentence separating the licensing dispute from the Phase One shortfall. The change makes the book's progression visible: China is not merely the recipient of foreign enforcement demands. Its own administrative decisions can give foreigners an urgent reason to negotiate.

For the actual move, keep blocks 064–067 with the Gackle–Cooper return, compress 068 into their competing demands, and join the Boards paragraph 070 to that account of prospective business. Block 071 presently repeats the hearing's lesson and ends with another version of the book's general “decisions remain” refrain. Replace it with the bridge above. Preserve the licensing sequence's contrast between restored shipments, surviving controls and suppliers' own hesitation; moving the section does not justify describing every delay as leverage. Its final paragraph can now end the chapter on the price of immediate relief, rather than promise a return to the hearing.

If an explicit bridge to Chapter 7 is needed, keep it to one sentence after that choice: “India presented a different calculation: how much progress at the border should be exchanged for movement in the wider relationship.” The next chapter supplies the date and ministerial encounter. Avoid another preview of India, the Russian islands and both pipelines together; that would repeat the introductory catalogue. An equally good option is to stop on the unresolved choice over supply permissions and let Chapter 7's concrete opening make the change of counterpart. The transition should not imply that the Indian case followed the 2026 licensing events chronologically.

## 3. Give Chinese domestic purposes a place in the synthesis, not only Chinese limits

**Anchors:** Chapter 2, Zhu's reform defense (007–008); Chapter 3, Xie's account of choosing the target (049–051); Chapter 7, CNPC's pricing problem (054–056); introduction “What changed at the table” and conclusion “What the update requires.”

The stories themselves are less one-sided than their accumulated lessons. Zhu explains why exposure to competition can serve reform. Xie describes a target intended to promote domestic restructuring. The gas material shows a Chinese buyer wanting supplies while facing a domestic cost-recovery problem. Yet the synthesis repeatedly casts the Chinese official as the holder of a limit, permission or instruction that a foreigner must navigate. The consequence is subtle: Beijing's positive purposes become local color, while defended authority becomes the general explanation.

**Concrete replacement:** replace one of the general statements about seeking an available exchange—preferably conclusion block 031, rather than adding another paragraph—with a compact comparison of these three purposes. Preserve attribution to public explanations and the limit on causal inference. For example:

“Chinese participants also explained bargains through domestic projects. Zhu defended WTO competition as a means of reform; Xie described the emissions target as a way to push economic restructuring. In gas, the Chinese company's account placed the foreign purchase beside domestic prices, costs and taxes. These explanations were not private instructions, but they gave counterparts something more useful to test than a presumed aversion to concession: whether a proposed exchange helped an undertaking Beijing already valued, and which domestic decision still stood in its way.”

This is continuity with Solomon's interest-centered explanation, not a new trait under Xi. It gives the practical reader a positive inquiry to conduct and lets Chinese participants help explain the book, rather than mainly furnish boundaries for its American reader. The checked notes already support the bounded statements; a stronger claim that one of these domestic projects caused a particular concession would require further evidence. Do not add that claim.
