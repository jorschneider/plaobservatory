## Findings and specific refinements

The central reconstruction is sustained by the passages checked. The normalization narrative follows the difference between an offer, an instruction, a text accepted by both sides and a unilateral position that remained opposed. The claims narrative gives the failed proposal enough substance to explain why senior engagement was useful without treating it as sufficient. The main fixes are precision and baseline balance, rather than a reversal of either account.

1. **Arrival of instructions is established; timing of their revision is not.** XIII/168 paragraph 2 says that changes reached Woodcock at the last minute. It does not independently date Washington’s decision to revise them. The original opening sentence assigned the last-minute timing to the revision itself. The parent has replaced it with “Changes to his instructions had arrived at the last minute,” which matches the record. The nine o’clock evening meeting and his late arrival are explicit in the same paragraph. No invented physical setting or dialogue appears in the opening.

2. **An undertaking is demonstrable; its marginal cost is not established.** The draft initially called the sales pause an accommodation with a tangible American cost. Deng did seek a concrete undertaking. But Woodcock also suggested in XIII/167 paragraph 9 that the administrative transition to unofficial relations might itself prevent new sales until the end of the year. That is his forecast, not proof that a pause was costless; equally, this selected evidence does not measure an additional American sacrifice. The parent’s verified replacement, “requiring a concrete American commitment,” avoids both inferences while preserving the bargaining content.

3. **Correct the note 137 locator.** Chapter note 18 currently reads “Solomon, 148 and note 137, 152.” The June 14, 1974 PRC message is cited in note 137 on **printed p. 148**, not p. 152. This was checked in the extracted text and visually on PDF page 166 of the supplied book; the page image is `/tmp/solomon-p148-review.png`. Use “Solomon, 148, note 137.” This fixes a locator; it does not make the underlying Chinese message available.

4. **Retain Freeman’s continuity claim alongside his account of adaptation.** The bridge accurately describes his budget, communication, authority and counterpart-specialization explanations. It currently gives less prominence to his accompanying conclusion: p. 184 says there was no substantial evidence of a change in the basic approach, while p. 195 endorses the continuing utility of distinctive and repetitive patterns and relative predictability. One sentence would preserve the baseline’s actual tension: “He nevertheless regarded those adaptations as compatible with a persistent basic approach and continued to endorse Solomon’s confidence in recognizable negotiating patterns.” Cite pp. 184 and 195. This is a balance refinement, not evidence that Freeman’s continuity proposition has now been tested successfully.

5. **One optional narrative cut.** After the December 14 clarification, delete “That still did not mean the Chinese had endorsed it.” The preceding paragraph has already warned against this inference, and the December 15 objection immediately demonstrates the difference. Keep the objection itself and the promise not to misrepresent China’s position. Most other repetitions perform different work: the initial problem, the negotiating choice and the practical inference should not all be collapsed into one disclaimer.

## Consequential claims checked

**Deng’s two conditions are not invented.** XIII/166 paragraphs 26–35 explicitly connect acceptance of the term “terminate” to both the absence of arms sales during the year and omission of Article 10 from the statement. Paragraph 35 states the conjunction. The chapter also keeps troop withdrawal within four months distinct from the one-year treaty notice and adjustment of other arrangements. Carter’s annotations, rather than an imagined instruction transcript, support the account of his reaction. XIII/167 remains an assessment by the same envoy, not an independent witness or a Chinese internal decision record.

**The December 4–15 chronology remains intelligible.** The brief December 14 opening is followed by an explicit return to December 4, December 11 and December 13; the prose announces its return to December 14. XIII/159 paragraphs 13–17 support the peaceful-reunification distinction, bilingual draft and invitation to meet Deng. XIII/163 supports the proposed senior visit, January target and denied Soviet linkage. XIII/166 paragraph 3 establishes Deng’s knowledge of Brzezinski’s exchange, but the chapter does not turn that knowledge into proof that the message caused his scheduling or instructions. There is no need to add another causal disclaimer to the body.

**Agreement to normalize is not consent to future arms sales.** XIII/168 paragraphs 3–4 directly support the wording change distinguishing shared principles from separate positions. Paragraphs 9–10 distinguish the assurance for 1979 from a press answer and permit previously committed deliveries. XIII/169 explicitly anticipates Chinese objections; its account of their likely political consequences is an American expectation. XIII/170 describes Deng’s emphatic refusal, his exploration of managing the announcement and his willingness to proceed. XIII/172 requires the United States to acknowledge the absence of Chinese endorsement. The chapter keeps these positions apart. It also avoids converting the published “defensive ABM” anomaly into a weapons claim.

**Zhou’s money is a proposal within an unfinished settlement.** XVIII/59, printed pp. 411–416, supports the dispute over designated nationals, private bond litigation, the $17 million proposal, the preference for direct payment and promised American legal consultation. Kissinger’s forty-cent estimate is presented as what he thought Congress could accept, not an actual vote or a binding congressional threshold. XIII/29 later reports an actual congressional rejection of a different country’s settlement at 42 cents; the draft does not contradict that inconvenient evidence by treating forty cents as a universal rule. Adding the comparison is optional, because the present attribution already carries the necessary limit.

**Solomon’s advice is separated from implemented action.** XVIII/65 supports his concern about the durability of the relationship, his acknowledgement of some Chinese grounds for suspicion, and the proposed message, senior trade visitor and broader contacts. Notes 7–8 distinguish Kissinger’s approvals from the deferred itinerary and requirement to clear a State démarche. The chapter does not turn these recommendations into completed visits. XVIII/71 independently documents American communication and clearance bottlenecks, including the retracted space-needs authorization. It is not presented as evidence of the cause of the claims failure. XIII/29 supports the later American account of offer withdrawal and approved deferral; XVIII/125 supports the continuing Chinese objection to a settlement requiring observance of American law. The missing June 1974 message remains missing.

**Solomon’s strongest claims have not been quietly softened.** Printed pp. 149–150 describe senior intervention as the most reliable sign and a highly consistent endgame pattern. The chapter preserves that comparative prediction and its face-based explanation before identifying what the selected cases cannot establish. Printed pp. 175–176 support the stated diagnostic claim that rigidity or polemicizing usually indicates leadership conflict. The draft challenges inference from the observable conduct alone without purporting to estimate or refute the claimed frequency. Its account of partial agreements is also faithful to p. 148; it does not mislabel that observation as a discovery of the update or as a complete test of S-P25’s additional asymmetry claim.
