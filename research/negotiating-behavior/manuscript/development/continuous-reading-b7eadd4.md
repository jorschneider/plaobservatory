## What this book now argues

The manuscript has moved beyond a collection of cautions about interpreting Chinese behavior. It argues that Chinese officials can preserve a political or legal position while negotiating valuable changes in the actions it permits. The foreign negotiator must decide whether to change the defended rule, seek accommodation within it, or refuse the available exchange. Relationships and senior intervention matter through the particular decisions they make possible. The historical development concerns China's legal capabilities, international position, administrative instruments and the dependencies through which a refusal acquires a price.

That is a defensible argument with substantial practical content. Woodcock and Deng, Lin and Habib, the EP-3 exchanges, the opposing enforcement witnesses, the island users and the Chinese gas buyer now provide real decisions and conflicting interests. The successful financial opening is essential contrary evidence to a story of permissions perpetually withheld. The Indian and Russian material also makes foreign choices part of the explanation.

The book is strongest when it follows those decisions. It is weaker when it pauses to prove that it has not committed an interpretive error, or ends an episode by telling the reader once again to identify authority and value the benefit. Several such endings flatten a particular discovery into advice applicable to any negotiation. The manuscript is now a serious comparative argument with several strong narrative sequences. It has not yet made its most consequential historical findings as memorable as its recurring methodological distinctions. Five changes would have the highest return.

## 1. Put the 1970s evidence inside the historical verdict

**Anchors:** introduction lines 19, 33 and 35–45; conclusion “What the update requires,” especially lines 57–71; Chapter 1, Lin–Habib exchange at lines 79–89.

The conclusion's claim that technical preparation and conditional offers were present “during accession” is true but undersells the book's strongest baseline evidence. Readers have just been shown how legal categories, private litigation, public presentation and a disputed accompanying assurance mattered in 1973–78. The new records change how a reader should understand Solomon's period itself. They should not disappear when the conclusion lists modern developments.

State the continuity positively before enumerating change. Lin's explanation and Habib's disagreement show a dispute about the conditions under which an earlier concession remained available. Normalization shows a different outcome: the parties deliberately preserved disagreement about arms sales while proceeding. Neither requires a new Chinese preference for ambiguity or a discovery that implementation matters. The older political logic already operated through precise distinctions.

Then make the later historical argument about **the routes by which particular demands could be pursued**. China could use WTO membership and a developed professional network to demand an answer from Washington. Foreign firms' dependence on Chinese processing made export approvals valuable bargaining objects. The availability of external review also changed again when ordinary WTO appeals became unavailable. This is a history of changing opportunities and constraints, not a monotonic march toward either legalism or Chinese dominance.

Possible replacement for the opening of the historical assessment:

> The records of the opening already contain the problem. Zhou's proposed payment depended on a settlement whose legal terms remained disputed; Deng accepted normalization while explicitly rejecting future American arms sales to Taiwan. Political agreement did not abolish those distinctions. In the later cases, the routes for pursuing interests changed. Membership and accumulated legal expertise allowed China to require an American defense before WTO adjudicators. China's position in supplying foreign industry made an export permission something Washington urgently sought. The weakening of ordinary WTO appeals then changed the available route again. The historical question is which institution or dependency makes an otherwise familiar demand effective, and what the other side can still refuse.

This formulation needs the existing Chapter 1 and Chapter 3/6 references, not a new claim about frequency. Keep the Xi discussion, but distinguish observed supervision and contact conditions from an unestablished change in concession latitude. The formal reforms do not deserve the same causal wording as the documented use of a WTO procedure. Xie's account of target selection and CNPC's domestic-price problem should remain visible examples of Chinese purposes, so that the historical argument is not told only as a sequence of foreign difficulties.

## 2. Replace the ideal of a definite answer with a decision about sufficient assurance

**Anchors:** introduction line 33, “obtain a definite answer”; conclusion “Authority that can be used,” lines 25–35; Chapter 4, April 7–11 sequence, lines 19–47.

The archival chapter is more sophisticated than the old synthesis. High-level clearance did not fix China's April 7 text permanently. An American instruction could permit discussion while reserving approval to sign. The transfer memorandum changed despite a claim that no time remained. Most importantly, Prueher handed over the settlement letter without having been given Tang's exact response in advance. He had obtained practical assurances and stated his understanding, but he still acted before uncertainty disappeared.

The practical question is therefore not only who can decide. It is what the approval covers, what remains negotiable, and which uncertainty must be resolved before the next irreversible act. Do not turn this into an injunction always to demand more documents: Prueher's exchange was completed partly through oral assurances.

Possible synthesis:

> Prueher did not obtain every answer before acting. He resisted delivery of an advance signed copy, sought the terms of release and secured practical departure assurances. Tang's exact words still came after the American letter was handed over. The ambassador had to judge whether the remaining exposure was acceptable. Authority made that judgment consequential; it did not remove the need to make it.

This is a practical inference from the documented sequence. It does not establish that the same protections would suffice in another case, or that wording alone caused release. It also improves the treatment of Solomon: the issue is how to use an observed signal, while the stronger comparative prediction remains unresolved. The introduction needs only a short statement of this point; the conclusion can carry the episode and tradeoff.

## 3. Let the crisis chapter finish its best story, then shorten the weaker recaps

**Anchors:** Chapter 4 lines 43–61 and 81–115, with lines 133–147 as a secondary target.

The enlarged EP-3 reconstruction earns its space. The signed-copy problem and the changed transfer memorandum turn abstract authority into decisions with stakes. At b7eadd4, however, the narrative moved from permission and an expected flight to later bargaining without plainly showing the twenty-four Americans leaving. The eventual result was assumed in the aircraft section and final comparison. A short, sourced return sentence completes the story; an invented airport scene would not.

The 2023 half repeatedly spends words ruling out exaggerated interpretations no reader has yet been invited to hold. This creates an imbalance in narrative quality rather than merely length: the first half follows a difficult exchange, while parts of the second verify the meaning of successive public readouts.

Concrete cuts:

- At line 89, remove “The American postponement is a necessary counterpart observation.” Begin directly with the difference between the contacts Washington wanted to preserve and the visit it postponed.
- At line 97, retain the Munich meeting and the opposed positions, but remove the sentence explaining that it rules out a chronology of all senior communication stopping until June. The encounter already demonstrates that.
- Reduce line 115's inventory of Taiwan and technology positions to one sentence, with the detail in note 21/22. The necessary point is that June engagement did not settle the underlying disputes. The chapter has already established it through an actual requested outcome Blinken failed to obtain.
- Preserve Li's removal, vacancy and the distinction between a summit undertaking and later implementation. Avoid giving every intermediate statement a fresh paragraph explaining that it supplies no complete account of private causation.

Keep the 2024 successful contact/refused meeting contrast. Keep the distinction between counternarcotics delisting and military contact: removing it would create a false exchange. A reduction of roughly 200–300 words in explanatory recaps would let the new archival episode remain the chapter's center without losing contrary evidence.

## 4. Give the WTO story a cleaner sequence and fewer unexplained numbers

**Anchors:** Chapter 3 lines 21–63; especially rate paragraphs 33 and 43, the 2016 coda at 47 and the backward turn at 51.

The chapter makes an important finding: China could win a more demanding legal test and then face a more demanding request for evidence under it. Commerce's acceptance of a partial anti-dumping adjustment against American producers' objections is a concrete result. The dispute still matters when no clean estimate of commercial relief is available.

The several rates make the reader work harder without supplying that estimate. The subsidy-rate increase is unexplained because the calculation attachment is missing. The next comparison deliberately juxtaposes different measures. A later paragraph returns to 64.28 percent but cannot explain the underlying remand reasoning. These are carefully qualified facts; their combined narrative effect is a succession of numbers whose meaning the book immediately limits.

Move the detailed rates and entry conditions into their existing notes. In the body retain: the subsidy finding survived; Commerce recognized a partial anti-dumping adjustment; American producers opposed it; China wanted more; and the sacks adjustment was later withdrawn in domestic litigation. Do not imply that a determination rate is taxes actually paid.

Consider ordering the sequence as: 2011 rulings → May 2012 agreement on further procedures → renewed administrative inquiries and July determination → September disagreement over compliance → brief 2016 coda → current appellate availability. The May arrangement would then be preparation made before the disputed implementation, not an option introduced after jumping forward four years. The authority of that arrangement becomes easier to understand.

The coda should remain brief unless the underlying remand reasoning is recovered and read. The existing note expressly says that reasoning has not been reconstructed. If the reversal becomes a principal lesson about the weakness of legal remedies, that missing record is a substantive research need. It is unnecessary for the narrower finding that the administrative adjustment did not survive the subsequent proceeding.

## 5. Stop giving the same practical lesson at three different scales

**Anchors:** introduction lines 17, 33, 53 and 57; Chapter 3 lines 129–135; Chapter 4 lines 119–129 and conclusion lines 33–35; Chapter 7 lines 137–141.

Repetition is now the main obstacle to pace. The problem is not that a central idea recurs; it is that it returns in nearly the same instructional form after evidence that deserves a more particular conclusion. Repeated directions to identify the decision, use the relationship and distinguish stages make distinct Chinese actors sound like illustrations of one lesson.

Recommended small cuts and replacements:

- Remove the introduction's four-question catalogue at line 53. Lines 17 and 33 already establish the reader's decision; the source paragraph and closing promise can follow the counterpart discussion. This would shorten framing without sacrificing the historical argument.
- After Chapter 3's substantive comparison at line 129, cut most of “What a specialist can change.” Retain a single transition into the crisis: technical expertise could clarify what was possible, while the decision about what to accept still belonged elsewhere. Avoid retelling both halves a third time.
- Keep the Kissinger encounter and its limits in Chapter 4. In the conclusion, condense lines 33–35 to the one unresolved question that matters: what message or proposal should an intermediary carry to whom? The four-function list almost repeats Chapter 4 line 129. Access as a resource is established; the book need not keep re-proving that it is not an instruction.
- End Chapter 7 with “Knowing the strength of their relationship did not dispense with knowing the business” at line 139. The following senior-intervention recap repeats the earlier chapters and the conclusion. The gas ending gives this chapter its own memorable finding: political value can sustain a relationship while geography, domestic prices and risk allocation keep its next project unfinished.

These cuts would protect the useful exceptions. Do not remove the profitable securities business, Indian acceptance of local progress without immediate political normalization, the temporary island permissions, or the Chinese buyer's domestic pricing constraints. They prevent an artificially uniform story. The aim is to let their differences change the reader's judgment rather than end them all with the same list of questions.
