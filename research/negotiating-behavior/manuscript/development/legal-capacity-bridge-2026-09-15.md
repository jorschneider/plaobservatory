# From accession to usable legal capacity

15 September 2026. Bounded return to the already acquired and previously fully read Shaffer–Gao article, Gao chapter and published Jiang speech. No new acquisition, interview or chapter edit. This note supplies an insertion and a conclusion recommendation, not an additional independent sample or a new verdict on Chinese legal compliance.

## Editorial finding

The most useful bridge is a documented investment in people and working relationships. It explains how WTO membership became something officials and lawyers could use. Litigation counts alone cannot supply that explanation, and the story need not adopt the article's broad framing about an allegedly anti-legalist tradition. Our accession chapter has already demonstrated detailed Chinese legal and commercial bargaining before membership.

The evidence also supplies a practical distinction: the specialist who can formulate a challenge or explain a loss does not necessarily control the ministry whose conduct is at issue. The article describes efforts to bring that ministry into the process. The officials' accounts are evidence of their practice and rationale, not an independently measured rate of successful persuasion.

Recommended placement: after Chapter 3's introductory three paragraphs and before “The work a victory leaves behind,” or after the paragraph introducing China's April 2009 legal arguments. The first location makes the chapter's explanation of acquired expertise explicit; the second preserves a faster answer to the Aifudi opening. Avoid adding a second miniature literature review later in the chapter. The insertion looks forward through the first decade of WTO participation; it does not claim that the 2011 secondment program produced the April 2009 submission or the March 2011 DS379 ruling.

## Proposed insertion

In February 2002, China's leaders gathered provincial and ministerial officials to study the organization they had joined. Jiang Zemin warned that inexperience would bring losses. He wanted specialists in trade law, negotiations and anti-dumping investigations, and demanded urgent recruitment and training. The published speech linked that investment to a second task: changing how Chinese governments managed the economy. WTO rules constrained members' policies, he told them; officials would have to reduce direct intervention and learn different ways of supporting industry. Expertise was part of a political program for improving China's competitive position.[^capacity1]

The work extended beyond the government payroll. Gregory Shaffer and Henry Gao's research describes how the commerce ministry paired Chinese firms with experienced foreign counsel. Chinese lawyers obtained access to earlier submissions and legal databases, helped establish the facts of Chinese measures and commented on drafts. Third-party participation in other countries' disputes supplied another apprenticeship: lawyers could study the arguments before China had to defend an equivalent measure itself. From 2011, the ministry also brought young law professors into its legal department for one-year assignments, giving them work on actual disputes.[^capacity2]

The relationships mattered inside government too. Foreign counsel needed information from the agencies responsible for a challenged measure. Commerce officials arranged meetings and brought those agencies into WTO hearings. Interviewees described this participation as a way to prepare ministries to accept an adverse finding. Expertise could help explain why a policy was vulnerable without giving the lawyers power to order its removal.[^capacity3]

Membership thus gave a growing professional network work to do, and that network made membership more useful. An objection to an American restriction could become a developed legal claim rather than remain a request for accommodation. The counterparty now had to reckon with the treaty argument and the procedure available to test it. DS379 shows how consequential that change could be—and how much bargaining could remain after China won.

## Proposed conclusion recommendation

One concrete change was China's acquisition of the people and organizations needed to use an external legal procedure against powerful trading partners. Membership supplied the rights; officials, domestic and foreign lawyers, and academic specialists developed ways to turn a commercial objection into a claim. Their work also connected international litigation to the Chinese agencies whose measures had to be defended or altered. This helps explain why expertise mattered without assuming that it displaced political authority. For the foreign negotiator, a Chinese legal argument could carry a consequence beyond the bilateral meeting: review under rules that both governments had accepted. The strength of that alternative now depends on the counterpart's procedural commitments, as the contrast between the US appeal in DS543 and China–EU appeal arbitration shows. The practical change is a different set of options and constraints at the table, not a guarantee that a ruling will remove a commercial barrier.

Use this as a replacement for the conclusion's generic claim that later cases widen the field of negotiation. It synthesizes the capacity bridge, the existing DS379 reconstruction and the separately verified [current appeals update](wto-current-appeal-2026-09-15.md). It should not be added on top of another full retelling of the litigation. A footnote can cite the bridge's sources and APP-01–08; no additional authority claim is needed.

## What Gao adds—and what should remain attributed

Gao's later chapter helps distinguish increased capability from a wholesale national embrace of legal constraint. At pp. 48–50 he expressly presents accession as serving Chinese development and strategic purposes, drawing heavily on the same Jiang speech. At pp. 51–58 he interprets later negotiation and dispute experience as changing the government's view of the institution. Those are different kinds of claims. The former can be checked against the published leadership text; the latter assembles public positions and episodes into an account of national attitudes.

The insertion therefore uses the later work to sharpen the interpretation of the early political program, rather than treating it as a second independent demonstration of capacity growth. It does not adopt the counterfactual that the accession-era crises would terminate negotiations “today” (48), national resentment as the established cause of a 2008 refusal (51), an undifferentiated A+ compliance verdict (50), or disappointment inferred from the DS516 suspension as recovered internal motivation (58). Those claims would need additional evidence. The previously recorded chronology error on the Appellate Body at p. 61 is also not imported; the current appeals memo uses official records.
