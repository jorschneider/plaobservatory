# 2023 military communications: sanctions, the vacant ministry and the remaining decision

Bounded research supplement, September 15, 2026. Read with [Chapter 4](../chapters/04-negotiating-in-crisis.md), the [existing 2023 case](../../cases/us-china-crisis-2023.md) and this [new source register](crisis-2023-authority-development-register.json). No chapter, case or shared apparatus edited.

## Result

The sanctions issue can be made more concrete than it is in the current chapter. China publicly asked for sanctions to be withdrawn in a response to a question about Li Shangfu and a proposed meeting. By August, the Defense Ministry explicitly included sanctions on Central Military Commission leaders among the obstacles to military relations. Washington, meanwhile, publicly distinguished retaining sanctions from the possibility of conducting official meetings. These were competing views of what would make contact acceptable, not simply an unresolved technical question about whether a meeting was legally possible. [CAD02, CAD08, CAD11, CAD03]

The evidence does **not** establish that removing Li on October 24 caused the November agreement or that replacing a sanctioned minister restored the wider communication system. The strongest limiting observation is more specific: **the leaders agreed on broader military communication while the defense-minister post was still vacant.** On November 16 the Pentagon said Austin would meet his counterpart when that person was named, while describing the other communication commitments as agreed but still requiring practical follow-up. A personnel obstacle to one proposed encounter and authority to restore several institutional mechanisms were distinguishable problems. [CAD04, CAD09; existing C23-17–19]

Li’s departure ended his role as the serving minister whom Austin was seeking to meet. That makes removal of a person-specific impediment plausible. It does not establish why Xi accepted the broader package, whether relief for Li had been a necessary condition, or whether an appointment was necessary for every military channel. No private offer/counteroffer record or Chinese implementing instruction was recovered. The useful update is a sharper account of the problem and its observable change, not a newly proven causal explanation.

## What the public exchanges actually say

### May: an invitation and an argument about acceptable contact

The specific American proposal was a bilateral Austin–Li meeting during the Shangri-La Dialogue in Singapore. Ryder’s account, preserved in the existing May 30 VOA report, places the invitation in early May and its rejection later that month. That is more definite than saying the United States generally wanted dialogue. [C23-12, complete report reread]

Before the refusal became public, Biden created some ambiguity about Washington’s position. At his May 21 Hiroshima press conference, a Bloomberg journalist asked about easing sanctions, expressly including those on China’s defense minister. Biden initially said no. After a lengthy discussion of the separate budget question, the journalist returned specifically to Li; Biden said the matter was under negotiation and explained that he had initially understood the question as concerning material supplied to the Chinese defense department. The complete exchange prevents presenting the first no or the later suggestion of negotiation as an uncomplicated, final instruction. It names no approved concession or recipient. [CAD01, Bloomberg question, full answer and follow-up]

On May 22, Matthew Miller was directly asked whether State was considering lifting Li’s sanctions, then challenged with Biden’s answer, then asked whether lifting them was being entertained for negotiation purposes. He denied it in each exchange. This is an observable public rejection of the proposed concession, not evidence that every internal discussion had stopped. The acquired source is a reproduction of the State briefing; its direct archive download returned an error page. [CAD02, complete sanctions exchange]

The Chinese answer that day deserves inclusion alongside Miller’s. A Bloomberg question to Mao Ning explicitly mentioned Biden’s comments, sanctions on the Chinese defense minister and Austin’s desired meeting. Mao criticized communicating while sanctioning Chinese officials, institutions and enterprises, and called for immediate withdrawal of sanctions to remove obstacles and create conditions. Her answer broadened the issue beyond one minister. It did not say that lifting Li’s designation alone would guarantee the Singapore meeting. [CAD11, complete Bloomberg exchange]

Contemporaneous VOA reporting also records the American position that the sanctions did not prevent official meetings. The distinction matters: Washington was saying a meeting could occur without delisting; Beijing was questioning whether contact under those political conditions was acceptable. The source here is attributed reporting of US officials’ position, not this memo’s independent legal determination. [CAD08]

On May 30, Reuters asked Mao whether sanctions were the reason the meeting had been refused. She called for respect for Chinese concerns and corrected American conduct. Bloomberg then explicitly asked whether she was confirming that Beijing had told Washington that the sanctions were the reason. She repeated that Washington knew the reason for the difficulties and should take practical action. The reporter’s proposed formula is not the same as an unqualified official confirmation of it. [CAD05, both complete exchanges]

CNN’s contemporary report separately quotes embassy spokesman Liu Pengyu calling for sanctions to be lifted and obstacles removed. The report itself notes that this statement did not specifically name Li’s sanctions. It strengthens evidence of a public request, while preserving the same limit on its exact scope. Its Pentagon chronology overlaps C23-12 and should not be counted as an independent witness to the underlying Chinese refusal. [CAD10]

### August: sanctions become an explicit component of a wider stated obstacle

At the August 31 Defense Ministry briefing, Wu Qian addressed the failed Singapore meeting and denied that all military contact had stopped. He said the two sides maintained communication through military-diplomatic channels, then listed American conduct he blamed for the difficulties: containment, Taiwan-related interference and arms support, military activity near China, and refusal to remove sanctions on CMC leaders. He demanded concrete American action and rejected normal exchanges while Chinese interests were being harmed. [CAD03, complete question and answer]

This is stronger source evidence than treating sanctions as a detail mentioned only by journalists. It also cuts against reducing the public Chinese position to the identity of the defense minister. Sanctions appeared within a bundle of political complaints. The statement does not disclose which concerns were necessary conditions, which were negotiating demands, or which could remain unresolved while a particular mechanism resumed.

The current chapter can therefore say **sanctions were a stated Chinese obstacle**. It should retain the qualification that the acquired public texts do not supply a single, guaranteed exchange of delisting for access. Those propositions are compatible. The absence of a narrow formula is not evidence that sanctions were irrelevant.

### October: dismissal did not itself announce a new communications policy

The October 24 presidential decree removes Li as state councilor and defense minister. It does not explain the reason or identify a replacement. Claims that he was dismissed to facilitate an American meeting cannot be derived from that act. The scope of this inquiry does not require importing later disciplinary findings to supply a diplomatic motive. [CAD04, item 1 and complete decree]

Two days later, Wu publicly identified Cynthia Carras as the coming US Defense Department representative at the Xiangshan Forum and said corresponding Chinese officials would exchange views with her. Asked whether her rank showed insufficient American sincerity, he said rank was not the most important consideration; substantive content mattered. This is useful process evidence: officials could arrange a working-level encounter while the ministerial seat was vacant. The statement is prospective, however, and not evidence that every planned discussion had already happened. [CAD06, first US-attendance question and subsequent three-part question]

Wu simultaneously continued to demand respect for sovereignty, dignity and core interests and practical changes by Washington. Asked about American complaints that China rejected military exchanges, he again said communication had not been interrupted and called for an improved political atmosphere. The dismissal therefore did not produce a publicly announced, unconditional reopening on October 26. Nor can the mere repetition of demands establish that none of the negotiating instructions had changed. [CAD06, military-report/communication exchange]

Axios reported that Wang had signaled readiness to reopen military communication during his Washington visit. [CAD07] This remains anonymous contemporary process reporting, not a disclosed Chinese instruction.

### November: agreement, a vacancy and unfinished arrangements

The paired November summit accounts already registered in the case name the mechanisms to be restored. The new Pentagon source clarifies how American officials described the next step. In her November 16 opening, Sabrina Singh welcomed the high-level communication and theater-call commitments, said Austin would meet his counterpart **“when that person is named,”** and acknowledged work still required with the Chinese military to turn the principles into actions and schedule meetings and calls. [CAD09, opening China paragraphs]

That formulation rules out a simple narrative in which a new Chinese defense minister first took office and then secured the November package. The package preceded the appointment being awaited. It also leaves the existing chapter’s November 30 boundary intact: agreed restoration, organizing work and usable recurring contacts were not the same accomplishment.

It does not prove that Li’s removal had no effect. A vacancy could remove an awkward interlocutor from one strand of the negotiation without being the reason for agreement in another. To distinguish that possibility from a decisive personnel mechanism would require an account of the proposals and instructions linking the two. None of the acquired sources provides one.

## What the existing participant study adds—and does not

The relevant Carnegie pages were reread before extending the search. On printed page 111, Chivvis and Hsia link Li’s sanctions to his unwillingness to meet American counterparts. Note 687 cites Amy Hawkins’s July 18, 2023 *Guardian* report. That particular statement is not additional corroboration from the study’s retrospective official interviews. The study’s military-resumption narrative on page 113 points in note 705 to November summit pool reports. Neither citation supplies the missing Chinese decision sequence. [Existing C23-21, printed pp. 110–113 and notes 673–718; selected reading only]

The study does provide a more specific reported bargain for counternarcotics: removal of the Institute of Forensic Science from the Entity List in exchange for cooperation. Chapter 4 already distinguishes this from military communication. That separation should remain. A recovered exchange in one field cannot identify the undisclosed price in another merely because both featured at the same summit.

## Recommended developmental use

The evidence supports two short additions rather than another chronology-heavy section:

- After the current May meeting-refusal paragraph, give the paired position: Mao’s May 22 sanctions-removal request; Miller’s denial that delisting was being considered; the American view that official contact was possible anyway. Add August’s explicit sanctions reference if necessary in a note. This lets the reader see a concrete disputed step without claiming a fully specified bargain.
- Immediately before or within the November section, date Li’s October 24 removal and state that the post remained vacant when the broader agreement was announced. Use Singh’s awaited-counterpart formulation to distinguish ministerial access from the other mechanisms. Do not say removal caused the restoration.

A possible connective paragraph:

> Li’s removal on October 24 changed the identity problem without settling the larger one. The decree did not name a replacement or explain the dismissal. Two days later, the Defense Ministry announced a planned exchange with a visiting American official while continuing to demand respect for Chinese interests. When Biden and Xi agreed in November to restore named military channels, China still had no new defense minister. The Pentagon said Austin would meet his counterpart when one was named; work on the broader arrangements could nevertheless proceed. A sanctioned interlocutor, political permission to resume mechanisms and the practical work of scheduling contact were related obstacles, but they were not the same obstacle. [CAD04, CAD06, CAD09; C23-17–19]

For a practitioner, the justified question is which contact can be authorized now and which awaits a different decision—not whether replacing one official will automatically reopen the system. The record shows a political objection to contact under sanctions, continued activity at other levels, and an eventual broader authorization that still required implementation. It does not reveal the full set of concessions or internal reasons that produced that authorization.
