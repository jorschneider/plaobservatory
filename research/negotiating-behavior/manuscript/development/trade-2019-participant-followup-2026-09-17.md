## What the new evidence adds

The chapter can replace an undifferentiated absence of knowledge about the spring breakdown with two more precise findings: the Chinese chief negotiator publicly disputed the purchase baseline as well as the treatment of the draft, and the reporters who reconstructed the negotiations later described a specific episode of senior resistance. These findings have different evidentiary status. Neither is the recovered agreement, an internal instruction, or minutes of an approval meeting.

**Liu He, in his own public account.** Phoenix’s longer publication of his May 10 remarks adds detail missing from the short Xinhua item already used. He said the leaders’ Argentina meeting had produced a preliminary understanding concerning purchase quantities, but the quantity was now disputed. He gave no amount. He also paired insistence on principles with negotiating flexibility and continued work on wording. The two claims belong together; a quotation of the first alone would make his posture appear less negotiable than this publication records. [T19P01, lines 39–43](https://news.ifeng.com/c/7ma1xgATP3g)

That account should sit beside, rather than silently replace, the December 1 American statement already cited in the chapter: the United States had publicly described a substantial purchase amount still to be agreed. A preliminary understanding and a final agreed number are not interchangeable. Nor can either statement establish which negotiating team subsequently moved the quantity. The useful historical finding is a contested baseline that survived several months of negotiation—not discovery of the missing number.

**A reported approval sequence.** In the first answer of the September 2020 *China Leadership Monitor* interview, Lingling Wei says Liu kept Xi informed but three other Standing Committee members resisted the provisional deal at an April meeting, particularly specified legal changes on deadlines backed by tariffs. This is original reporting recounted by its author. The interview identifies neither those members nor the underlying informants, and supplies no internal document. Its accompanying claims about Xi’s political vulnerability and economic calculations are interpretations, not separately exhibited evidence. [T19P04, first question and answer](https://www.prcleader.org/post/clm-insights-interview-with-bob-davis-and-lingling-wei)

This changes the treatment of uncertainty. It is too strong to say that nothing has been reported about approval. It remains justified to say that the available sources do not let this manuscript independently establish the internal sequence or Liu’s exact authority. Keeping Xi informed, obtaining permission to explore a provision, and obtaining final approval are distinct possibilities. The reporting does not by itself tell us where any boundary lay at a particular negotiating session.

**The authors’ own qualification matters.** In the Chicago discussion, Bob Davis explicitly calls the agreement preliminary and describes it as reported to the authors. His comparison with China’s WTO accession also resists treating legal amendment itself as a timeless prohibition. The surrounding exchange is a counterfactual prompted by Chang-Tai Hsieh. Its speculations about trust, tariffs and the pandemic are not evidence of effects that actually occurred. [T19P05, physical pages 11–13, especially page 12](https://bfi.uchicago.edu/wp-content/uploads/Superpower-Showdown_-Discussion-of-New-Book-by-Bob-Davis-and-Lingling-Wei_Transcript.pdf)

The Chicago and CLM interviews should therefore be one reporting chain in the argument, not two independent confirmations. The full *Superpower Showdown* has not been read in this follow-up. Its notes could offer further attribution; that possibility does not authorize filling in the missing source base from the interviews.

**A contemporary public expectation about approval.** Asked on May 24 whether Liu and Lighthizer had exhausted their level of negotiation, Ambassador Cui Tiankai said he still expected the teams to finish and submit results to the two leaders. In another answer he said a G20 leader meeting had not yet been formally discussed through official channels. These are prospective public positions in the embassy’s complete Chinese translation of his Bloomberg interview. They neither verify an April meeting nor supply a constitutional rule that every trade provision required personal leader approval. [T19P03, questions on negotiating level and the G20](https://www.mfa.gov.cn/gjhdq_676201/gj_676203/bmz_679954/1206_680528/1206x2_680548/201905/t20190525_9364780.shtml)

## What should change in the chapter

Use the new material to clarify the dispute before moving to the smaller agreement. It does not establish a fresh causal account of the September–October decision. The chapter already possesses stronger contemporaneous material for that later sequence, including the report of narrowing the package and the October 11 public exchange. Repeating those outcomes here would not close the spring process gap.

The most useful addition would replace part of the existing missing-draft discussion, not create another subsection. Suggested text, offered for integration rather than inserted into the chapter:

## Practical implication, with limits

The useful distinction is between negotiating text and establishing the status of that text. A team may possess a detailed document and remain uncertain about whether the other side regards a purchase number as provisional, settled, or conditional on the package. Those are different risks from ambiguity in the eventual contractual language. A version history and an explicit exchange about which terms remain subject to approval can reveal disagreement earlier. They cannot compel truthful disclosure, prevent a leader from changing position, or remove the political cost of publishing a concession.

The chapter should not turn that observation into advice to bypass the negotiator and obtain a private guarantee from Xi. Such access is rarely available; even the reporting examined here distinguishes leader awareness from the final acceptance of a package. Nor should it generalize that China cannot accept changing domestic law. The manuscript’s accession chapter and Phase One text already make that proposition untenable. The narrower issue is the acceptability of a particular combination of specificity, deadlines, retained tariffs and reciprocity.

There is also a distinction between an actor’s claim about dignity and an outside explanation of why a negotiation failed. Liu’s remarks show what he defended publicly. They cannot establish how much weight that concern carried against price, quantities, enforcement or domestic opposition behind closed doors. The practical lesson is to test which provisions remain negotiable while taking stated constraints seriously, rather than treating either the rhetoric or the signed outcome as a complete account of the bargaining process.
