# Domestic enforcement before Xi: the 1995 IP bargain and Phase One

Research date: 15 September 2026. Bounded original-document comparison, not a history of the entire 1992–96 dispute or a claim that either agreement was fully implemented. Source metadata and actual reading scopes are in [the accompanying register](ip-enforcement-pre-xi-register.json). Raw texts remain outside Git. No manuscript chapter was edited in this assignment.

## Finding that changes the historical argument

Detailed domestic obligations, monitoring, private complaints and renewed unilateral tariff pressure did **not** arrive with Phase One. The published 1995 IP exchange and annex already specified Chinese administrative organization, investigative powers, deadlines, information flows and sectoral enforcement; the United States retained a domestic-law route back to Section 301 action. The distinction supported by this comparison is the **particular bilateral complaint and remedy procedure accepted in 2020**, not a transition from political agreements to administratively specific ones.

This matters to the proposed update of Solomon. Freeman's epilogue already identified foreign demands to change Chinese domestic policies, including intellectual property, and Solomon's bibliography named the two hearings examined here. The older book's broad account cannot fairly be contrasted with only its normalization episodes while leaving its contemporary trade setting out of the baseline. Nor do these texts establish that Chinese delegates became more or less willing to accept intrusive obligations under Xi: there is no matched measure of the price, coverage, rejected alternatives or implementation in the two bargains.

The usable historical claim is narrower and stronger. Americans were already negotiating how China's domestic machinery should work in 1995. By 2020, the parties had written a different procedure for handling interstate allegations of breach, including confidentiality, escalation, unilateral remedies and a contested-remedy exit. Legal detail and discretionary power coexist in both periods.

## The earlier bargain, at the level of acts required

The Commerce-hosted English text contains the exchanged letters and complete action plan. The letters require regular, reciprocal enforcement statistics and consultations: quarterly consultations in the first year, semiannual consultations in the following two, and prompt consultations on either government's request. The American side supplies specified enforcement statistics and technical assistance; these are not exclusively one-way Chinese reporting obligations. The annex allocates work among a State Council working conference, local coordinating bodies and enforcement task forces. It addresses local protectionism, investigative access to premises and records, seizure and destruction, criminal referrals, license revocation, public reporting, customs procedures and staff training. These are stated duties, not independent proof that the bodies could or did perform them. [IPPRE-01, letters PDF 1–3; annex I.A–G, PDF 4–13; II, PDF 15–16.]

The bargain also preserves control. The letter's market-access provisions allow measures to protect public morals or order, subject to stated constraints, and require publication and processing rules for audiovisual censorship. They do not abolish censorship. The administrative enforcement route likewise preserves judicial relief rather than replacing the Chinese courts with an external tribunal. A protected sphere of Chinese decision-making therefore is not itself a new discovery in the 2020s. [IPPRE-01, letter PDF 2; annex I.B.6, PDF 6.]

## An implementation dilemma already visible in 1995–96

On 2 March 1995, Toby Roth asked Charlene Barshefsky whether Beijing could enforce the agreement, then distinguished ability from will. She expressed confidence in the government's ability while acknowledging that its will would have to be tested. Her answer joined continued pressure to vigilance, technical assistance and Americans on the ground. This is a contemporary participant's proposed enforcement strategy, not confirmation of state capacity or a retrospectively established motive. [IPPRE-03, printed 11–12 / PDF 19–20.]

The following year's hearing and notice keep the aftermath from disappearing. On 7 March 1996, Mickey Kantor reported eighteen bilateral meetings in less than eleven months, acknowledged some retail enforcement and institutional progress, and charged inadequate action against larger production and distribution operations and inadequate market access. On 17 May, the operative USTR notice recorded a finding of unsatisfactory implementation and proposed prohibitive tariffs. It also announced an actual, narrower, temporary restriction on entries of certain textile and apparel products. The proposed tariff list must not be described as if all its duties had already taken effect. These are US assessments and actions; this bounded pass does not independently measure piracy or reconstruct the final June 1996 settlement. [IPPRE-04, printed 5–12 / PDF 13–20; IPPRE-06, 61 FR 25000–02.]

Thus extensive follow-up and renewed pressure were not inventions of the Phase One era. What this episode cannot establish is that the two settlements performed equally well, or that one was more coercive in effect.

## Suggested manuscript insertion

Place before Chapter 5 explains Phase One's enforcement machinery, or immediately after the hearing discussion of the enforcement problem. Preserve the later explanation of Lighthizer's company-identity concern; this paragraph prevents it being made a claim about the first appearance of confidentiality.

> Lighthizer was not the first American negotiator to ask how a Chinese promise could be enforced inside China. In March 1995, after a new intellectual-property agreement, Congressman Toby Roth pressed Charlene Barshefsky on whether Beijing had the ability to enforce it—and then whether it had the will. She was confident about the first and proposed to test the second through continued vigilance, technical assistance and pressure. The agreement already reached into domestic administration: enforcement task forces, published contacts for right holders, investigative powers and a fifteen-day deadline to accept a petition or explain its rejection. Both governments undertook to exchange enforcement statistics and consult regularly. Washington withdrew its scheduled tariff action while retaining the possibility of further Section 301 action if implementation disappointed it.[^a]
>
> Phase One therefore did not introduce detailed obligations backed by American pressure. It specified a different route from a government complaint to the next coercive move. An interstate appeal could omit the identity of the company involved; designated officials and then senior representatives were to attempt a settlement. After unresolved consultations, the complaining government could take a proportionate remedial measure. The respondent's judgment of good or bad faith then mattered: the text prohibited a counter-response to action it considered in good faith and designated withdrawal as the remedy for action it considered in bad faith. This was a negotiated procedure for managing unresolved disagreement, with no neutral adjudicator inserted into that procedure. Its operation still depended on what each government was prepared to tolerate.[^b]

[^a]: IPPRE-03, printed 11–12; IPPRE-01, letter PDF 3 and annex I.B.5–6, PDF 6; IPPRE-05, 60 FR 12582–83. The petition deadline concerns acceptance or refusal, not completion of enforcement.

[^b]: POA-EN, Articles 7.4.1, 7.4.4, 7.6 and Annex 7-A; general termination in Article 8.3. Existing WTO rights are affirmed; this passage describes the agreement's bilateral procedure rather than every legal route available.

## Provenance and limits that should travel with the evidence

- The English original used here is a Commerce-hosted PDF reproduction of a treaty-collection text, not an executed signature facsimile. Its date is 26 February 1995. MOFCOM hosts a Chinese reproduction dated 11 March, crediting PKU Law. The apparent mismatch is resolved by the contemporary May 1996 Federal Register notice: agreement on 26 February, formal signature on 11 March. Do not count these dates as conflicting evidence or the two reproductions as independent witnesses to negotiations.
- All 16 English PDF pages were read via fresh OCR after the embedded text proved unreliable; selected pages were checked visually. The Chinese letters and first two substantive annex sections were read, not the entire Chinese annex. The Chinese reproduction abbreviates the US reply's repeated quotation. No original diplomatic signing archive was acquired.
- The 1995 and 1996 hearings were read only in the specified selected exchanges and openings, not cover to cover. Their confident predictions and allegations remain attributed. The March 1996 transcript uses inconsistent descriptions of the earlier sanctions at different points; the operative 1995 notice governs this memo's chronology. The 1995 order was rescinded effective on its scheduled tariff start date.
- The 1996 source records renewed enforcement pressure, not a final verdict of total noncompliance. There is no new comparative estimate of implementation, bargaining power or the causal effect of either agreement. The finding is sufficient to remove an unsupported historical novelty claim without replacing it with an equally broad continuity claim.
