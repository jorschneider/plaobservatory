# Network power: a useful distinction, not a universal label

Henry Farrell and Abraham L. Newman, “Weaponized Interdependence: How Global Economic Networks Shape State Coercion,” *International Security*44(1),2019,42–79. [Published article](https://doi.org/10.1162/isec_a_00351); [public university-hosted copy](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/sites.tufts.edu/dist/2/7314/files/2019/08/Farrell-and-Newman-2019-Weaponized-Interdependence.pdf); [reading register](network-power-selected-register.json).

Selected reading: complete printed52–58 and75–79, including notes. This reads the principal mechanism, scope conditions and concluding limits, not both full empirical narratives. It must not increase the complete scholarly-reading count.

The useful distinction is between economic weight and the institutional capacity to control a difficult-to-avoid part of an international network. The authors distinguish this mechanism from market size and bilateral dependence. Their argument requires both position and usable authority; domestic constraints can prevent a state from exploiting an apparent advantage. They also expect targets and firms to adapt, so an immediate capacity to impose costs is not an assurance of enduring power.

For the2025 chapter, this makes the April control notice and actual administrative relief essential evidence. A concentration statistic alone would not establish the bargaining mechanism. Conversely, issuing licenses does not establish that China controlled the whole outcome. The article's finance/internet cases are not empirical verification of rare-earth bargaining, and the manuscript should avoid treating “weaponized interdependence” as a synonym for any pressured commercial relationship.

This is a conceptual check on the research design. No claim from an unread case section has been incorporated. Further reading should examine the manufacturing-network distinction and sector-specific substitution, rather than simply attach a familiar theoretical label to the new episode.
