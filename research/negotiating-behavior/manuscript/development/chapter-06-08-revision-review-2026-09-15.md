## Chapter 6

The new order resolves the principal structural issue in the audit. The hearing now completes the Phase One movement before the chapter introduces a separate dispute about permission for future transactions. That transition expressly prevents the licensing bargain from appearing to settle the old purchase shortfall. The final licensing paragraphs then explain the price of relief under retained authority rather than merely repeat that implementation matters.

The shortened Board coda has not lost essential context. CH6-01 describes discussion of structure, functions and operating arrangements, an exploratory tariff-reduction framework of $30 billion on each side, consultation of interested parties, and product arrangements still to be agreed and implemented. POI18 describes guiding objectives for expanded agricultural trade and market-based company decisions; its answer does not affirm every premise in the reporter's purchase question. POI21–22 establish solicitation and a contemplated mechanism, not a completed tariff schedule. The revised coda preserves these distinctions and its July endpoint. Its advice concerns acts officials can specify, information they can seek and costs their own governments can assess. It does not require access to a Chinese instruction record.

## Conclusion

The revisions make two important corrections to the argument. They put removable ownership restrictions and earlier rare-earth measures beside retained limits, so the reader is no longer encouraged to treat every defended instrument as a permanent political boundary. They also give refusal a concrete place in the analysis: a government can reject relief that is too narrow or brief for the concession it must make. This remains a conditional judgment, not a calculated verdict on the undisclosed London terms.

The authority section is also more useful. Presenting a specified proposal for clearance and conditioning an extension on a response are feasible actions under uncertainty. The text properly explains that these actions do not diagnose whether delay reflects unwillingness or limited authority.

One residual sentence should follow that discipline. In the penultimate paragraph, **“Know what China wants enough to exchange”** restates the reader's informational problem as an instruction. Replace it with **“Test a specific reciprocal offer, and establish what one's own government can sustain,”** or omit the sentence, because the preceding sections already supply the practical moves. This is a refinement, not an empirical correction.

The continuous-reading audit's current-WTO point remains outstanding in this version: note 10 warns against assuming unchanged appellate machinery, but the body still does not distinguish currently available routes by counterpart. A bounded update elsewhere in Chapter 3 can supply the substance; the conclusion then needs only a brief dated reference. This review has not researched or established the current legal position. The Paris paragraph and military-channel example remain framed as historical design/results and do not themselves claim current operational availability.

The conclusion's separate Board paragraph partly repeats the now-effective Chapter 6 coda. Cutting it is an optional economy, not a factual correction. The remaining argument coherently distinguishes the choice to accept a limited benefit, authority to deliver it, evidence of performance and the willingness to enforce it.
