## Narrative and argument

The replacement begins with Jaishankar’s response to a concrete press question after his three-hour March 25, 2022 meeting with Wang Yi: did discussing the wider relationship mean relations had already become normal? It follows the choices disclosed in his answer. He could continue discussion, seek relief for students and press trade concerns while withholding the broader characterization Beijing wanted. His account expressly separates the BRICS invitation from the border discussion; the draft does not invent a summit-for-withdrawal bargain.

China’s own full meeting account now appears opposite that briefing. It supports Wang’s argument against letting the boundary define wider ties, but also records Jaishankar’s demand to finish disengagement and agreement about restoring peace. This is useful contrary evidence against a simplified portrait in which China declined every connection between the border and the relationship. Different priorities remained compatible with some shared language and work. The two official accounts do not supply a transcript of the room or evidence that either party privately accepted all the other’s conditions.

The narrative moves through the existing agreements and divisions of responsibility only when they help explain the available choices. The 2005 agreement contains both protection of the wider relationship from boundary differences and obligations to respect the line and maintain peace. The 2012 mechanism’s border-management role differs from the Special Representatives’ responsibility for the boundary question. Chadha’s account of an Indian commander’s bounded mandate shows why hierarchy belongs in the counterpart’s story as well as China’s.

PP15 then becomes the central bargain, oriented geographically as one confrontation point in eastern Ladakh in the western sector, without adding unsupported directional or map detail. The July announcement promised continued work; the September explanation supplied a start time, deadline, reciprocal withdrawals, dismantling and verification. The October release reported completion. The draft preserves those different documentary stages. It does not infer a vague operative arrangement from a short communiqué, nor claim that the Indian explanation is the authenticated protocol.

Sun Weidong’s September speech and Bagchi’s October reply turn the local result into a new decision: how much wider cooperation should follow? Their disagreement is over the sufficiency and political consequence of progress; neither text, by itself, establishes nonperformance of PP15. The ambassador’s own words are less categorical than the Indian questioner’s paraphrase that the dispute was over. The draft uses his words and Bagchi’s actual reply, rather than treating that paraphrase as a Chinese admission.

The 2024–May 2026 coda is shorter than the current chapter’s chronology. It preserves the arrangement-before-summit sequence, India’s official patrolling and grazing claims, remaining temporary local restraints, and disengagement/de-escalation distinction. Jaishankar’s December 2024 statement already permits calibrated engagement after disengagement, before all subsequent military work is done; the paired 2025 releases make that coexistence explicit. This avoids treating Indian conditionality as either meaningless from the outset or an immutable all-or-nothing rule.
