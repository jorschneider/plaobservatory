# Formal tools, discretionary relief: a complete reading of Bohman, Wong and Ferguson

**Developmental research, 15 September 2026.** This is a critical reading for manuscript revision, not approval of a finished chapter. Source **OL25-01** is Viking Bohman, Audrye Wong and Victor A. Ferguson, “The Old Logic Behind China’s New Economic Weapons,” *The Washington Quarterly* 48(3), Fall 2025, pp. 25–45, published online 29 September 2025, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/0163660X.2025.2554476). The published article is available as a [journal/university-hosted open-access PDF](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/blogs.gwu.edu/dist/1/2181/files/2025/09/TWQ_48_3_BohmanWongFerguson.pdf). All prose, figures, table, and 48 notes have been read. The article adds a complete scholarly journal reading; its June 2025 policy brief remains a related work by the same authors, not independent corroboration.

The most useful contribution is to separate three questions too easily collapsed in a story about China's growing economic power: whether a restriction has an explicit political or security basis, whether its enforcement is predictable, and whether the target knows what would obtain relief. A legal instrument can strengthen Beijing's authority over domestic actors while leaving substantial discretion over foreign access. That distinction gives the [Geneva–London reconstruction](trade-2025-export-controls-evidence.md) a stronger analytical foundation. The article does not establish that a particular licensing delay was centrally ordered to extract a concession, or that the presence of discretion makes every administrative decision a bargaining move.

## What the research actually measures

The authors assemble 233 individual economic restrictions from 2010 through **10 August 2025**. These are measures, not entire sanctions episodes or separate negotiations. Formal measures come from official Chinese websites; informal measures are identified through reporting, timing and assessments that a government-initiated or encouraged restriction plausibly served political or security purposes. The authors expressly disclaim exhaustiveness, especially in less closely monitored earlier years (p. 28). Their formal/informal distinction concerns acknowledged political or security purposes, not simply the presence of an official document: a published food-safety action can enter the informal category when its coercive purpose is inferred rather than declared (pp. 26–28; Table 1, p. 35).

The descriptive result deserves attention. In their series, formal restrictions outnumber informal ones from 2023. They classify 46 of 75 formal measures, or 61 percent, as countersanctions; 69 percent of formal measures target the United States. “Countersanction” means direct response to another state's sanctions. Their contrasting category, primary sanctions, covers restrictions initiated to pursue broader political or security objectives. They report a slight decline in the number of primary sanctions since 2022, while proactive formal measures concentrate on Taiwan (pp. 29–32). This is a challenge to a simple story of uniformly increasing initiative, rather than a finding that Chinese policy has become benign. A retaliatory measure can impose large costs, seek a major concession or deter third parties. Its classification does not establish legal proportionality or strategic passivity.

Several limits belong beside any use of those numbers. Officially announced restrictions and inferred informal ones have different detection processes. More reporting can increase observed counts without an equivalent increase in underlying activity. One crisis can generate many measures. A restriction on a defense company with little Chinese exposure and a bottleneck in an industrial supply chain each contribute to a count without carrying equal economic weight. The 2025 observations cover a partial year. Most importantly, the dataset records restrictions rather than accepted demands, implementation or negotiating success. The linked China Sanctions Monitor was not downloaded or independently recoded for this reading; the counts above remain the authors' reported results. Figures 1–3 were visually checked, not treated as a substitute for row-level replication.

## The mechanisms and their evidence

**Formalization can help the center secure domestic compliance.** Local governments and firms may protect profitable foreign relationships or evade restrictions. Clear rules can make orders easier to monitor and violations easier to punish. The article connects this principal–agent problem to variation during the THAAD dispute, reports of gallium smuggling in 2024, and the 2025 campaign against strategic-mineral smuggling (pp. 32–33, notes 25–29). This usefully complicates an account in which the Chinese state automatically delivers whatever its negotiators promise. But an enforcement campaign demonstrates a concern and an attempted remedy; it does not itself measure improved compliance. The THAAD point relies on Kacie Miura's work, cited here but not newly read in this task. The article does not reproduce internal instructions or a matched before-and-after enforcement study.

**Formalization may also make threats more legible to foreign targets.** Recognizable legal tools can clarify state involvement and make restrictive action appear more credible. The authors suggest that officials accustomed to formal sanctions may take these tools more seriously (p. 33). Yet the argument is conditional. There is no systematic measurement here of counterpart beliefs before and after designation. The Geneva statement's undertaking concerning non-tariff countermeasures is consistent with their importance in bargaining; it cannot alone identify which restriction caused which concession (p. 36 and note 32). Our case's paired official implementation statements and corporate records are therefore doing a different evidentiary job from the article's broader interpretation.

**Formalization need not eliminate selective enforcement.** The authors stress that the April rare-earth measures require licenses rather than prohibit all exports. They pair these with uncertainty over Unreliable Entity List consequences, reported exceptions to other sanctions, and differential access to licenses (pp. 36–38). This supplies a plausible mechanism: the state can calibrate relief without repealing the underlying authority. It can also respond to domestic economic costs. Their suggestion that friendly foreign actors might be rewarded is a hypothesis, not something demonstrated merely by Volkswagen suppliers obtaining approvals. Crucially, they preserve rival explanations for slow rare-earth exports—intentional pressure and insufficient administrative capacity—rather than resolving them from the delay alone (pp. 37–38, note 42).

**The triggering offense and the condition for relief are different objects.** An announcement can explain why punishment began without saying what action would secure delisting. The authors develop this point through Taiwan-related designations and the 2025 removal of sanctions on five members of the European Parliament (pp. 38–39). Ambiguity can preserve room to retreat, but it can also frustrate compellence if targets doubt concessions will yield relief. Alternatively, punishment may aim to deter future behavior or influence third parties. These explanations make different predictions. The article does not settle their relative importance. It is strongest when it exposes those competing functions, rather than when it calls ambiguous relief criteria a systematic national distinction.

## What changes in the 2025 chapter

The article supports a substantive addition to Chapter 6: **implementation can preserve the capacity to negotiate further even while delivering genuine relief**. The April licensing authority and the June undertaking to approve eligible exports are compatible. The operative distinction is between abolishing a restriction and administering permissions under it. That was already visible in our primary sources; the article places it in a wider debate about Chinese economic statecraft.

The case should retain the sequence established in [the 2025 register](trade-2025-export-controls-register.json): the April 4 instrument (EC25-01), the May 12 joint statement (EC25-02), approvals reported before London (EC25-04), the June 27 approved implementation understanding (EC25-06), and the July corporate evidence of US restrictions being lifted and access being restored or restoration beginning (EC25-08 and EC25-18). European industry pressure, unequal approvals and the European green channel (EC25-19, EC25-09 and EC25-24) prevent a bilateral bargain from being mistaken for the disposition of all global applications. These records establish the administrative form and some delivery. They do not identify the fate of every application or quantify the bargain's effect relative to other pressures.

The article also checks an inflated novelty claim. Its own series begins in 2010, well after Solomon's core cases. It cannot establish a continuous transition from his 1967–1984 baseline. The selected historical material already in our dossier shows that critical-mineral restrictions were not invented in 2025. What the present case brings into view is a combination of industrial dependence, explicit legal authority, and permissions capable of differentiated delivery. Whether that combination is more effective than earlier instruments requires additional comparisons. It is enough for this chapter to explain what the mechanism allows and what happened in this episode.

This is not an additional test proving Solomon's full S-P25 proposition. Continuing to bargain after an agreement is visible; the conjunction involving asymmetrical insistence on counterpart adherence and freedom to adjust one's own commitments still requires version-specific evidence. Nor does an opaque queue establish deliberate delay or a leader's private order. The new literature should sharpen those distinctions rather than turn them into cultural explanations. The [proposition assessment](../appendices/01-proposition-assessment.md) remains the baseline for verdicts.

## Where the article needs qualification

The contrast with Western or democratic sanctions systems is broader than the comparative evidence displayed. The authors acknowledge that democracies can also leave demands unclear (p. 38), but their characterization of comparatively predictable enforcement does not rest on a matched sample of US, EU and Chinese cases. Our US company notices describe changes in BIS restrictions through letters whose full contents we have not acquired. That is itself a reason to compare particular procedures, review rights and decisions, rather than import a categorical China–democracy contrast. This qualification neither makes the systems identical nor erases differences in legal remedies; it asks the chapter to specify the feature actually observed.

The article's source chain also limits its independence. Note 30 explicitly cites the same authors' June 18 brief, already fully read as EC25-12. Notes 32 and 35 return to the Geneva statement and April instrument already acquired as EC25-02 and EC25-01. Note 42 cites the Reuters account already read as EC25-25. Those passages extend interpretation; they do not add separate witnesses to Geneva, licensing deliberations or staffing. Chinese legal scholarship in notes 18–24 supplies evidence of published arguments about legitimacy and retaliation. It is not a released mandate and has not been independently read here. No original private interviews or negotiation records are identified as the evidentiary basis for the article's account of the London bargain.

Four bibliographic checks found errors that must not migrate into the book. Note 43, visually checked on printed p. 44, dates three MFA documents to 2025 although their original headers and operative dates are **5 December 2024**, **7 April 2023**, and **31 July 2024** (OL25-02–04). The last concerns **Jim McGovern**, whereas the corresponding prose discusses **Michael McCaul**. It therefore cannot serve as the direct citation for that example. Note 47 dates the linked BNS/LRT report to 5 January 2025; the article header gives **5 January 2022** (OL25-05). These checks concern selected citations, not an audit of every reference or proof that the dataset has the same errors. Conversely, Table 1 correctly places the rare-earth measures on April 4; the earlier policy brief's erroneous March 14 label should not be attributed to this journal version.

## Tests that would strengthen the explanation

For domestic coordination, seek records showing how an order changed behavior among agencies and firms that had resisted it, including sanctions for evasion and the timing of compliance. For threat credibility, seek the counterpart's contemporaneous assessment and a changed offer tied to that assessment. For selective licensing as bargaining, match item, end user, application completeness, review stage and approval date across recipients; aggregate exports alone cannot separate political selection from backlog and technical review. For ambiguity over relief, compare what negotiators privately understood would suffice with the published conditions, and test whether relief followed the specified action.

The strongest current formulation is thus bounded: the 2025 bargain shows how access could be restored through administrative decisions while the authority to restrict remained. Bohman, Wong and Ferguson explain why formalization and retained discretion can coexist, and why a clear grievance does not necessarily provide a usable path to relief. Their article supplies mechanisms to test and a broader descriptive comparison. The dated primary record must still carry the account of what the two governments agreed and delivered.
