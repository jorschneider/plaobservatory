## What the revision now does

The introduction begins with a consequential offer: the American departure letters in November 1999. Chapter 1 follows Woodcock and Deng through the last normalization decisions, then places Solomon himself inside the claims/assets problem as an adviser proposing action. Accession follows the different packages that American and European negotiators could obtain and authorize. The trade chapters move from a disputed promise to operating permission, a corporate complaint, an enforcement decision and the 2025 exchange of relief under retained export controls.

The crisis chapter now sets Prueher’s account beside a newly read Chinese participant interview. Its immediate problem is obtaining the crew’s release while responsibility remains disputed. The conclusion first explains the documented historical changes and the domestic purposes Chinese participants described. It then develops the costs of accepting, refusing and enforcing limited bargains. It no longer organizes the reader’s final encounter with the argument around research-status labels.

The central research discipline remains unchanged: identify who sought which decision, what blocked it, what a participant tried, and what the accepted terms changed. An announcement, an original instruction, a later recollection and a subsequent commercial result answer different questions. Prose should preserve those differences where they affect judgment and move source bookkeeping into notes.
