# Aircraft return: a plan, two non-papers and a disputed power supply

Research completed September 16, 2026. This bounded follow-up acquires six publicly released State Department cables covering April 24–30, 2001. All 30 physical PDF pages were read as extracted text; eleven decisive pages were also inspected as images. The [matching register](ep3-aircraft-cables-register.json) gives URLs, hashes, paragraph locators and the exact visual scope. No chapter or earlier register was edited. These sources extend the [crew-release cable reconstruction](ep3-drafting-cables.md), without constituting a complete reading of the 46-document State case.

## What changes

The record is substantially better than two opposing recollections. On April 24, the American embassy reported Zhou Wenzhong's qualified objection to flying the EP-3 out: symbolically difficult, but not the core issue. By April 29–30, the embassy recorded an explicit prohibition alongside permission to inspect the aircraft. The permission to assess its condition did not authorize flight, repair or unrestricted handling. The cables also recover the American draft, its Washington approval conditions, the Chinese non-paper delivered on April 29, and several changes to the inspection arrangements.

They do **not** recover a Chinese flyout authorization or exclude a later proposal or permission in May. They therefore narrow the Prueher–Zhou disagreement without resolving it. More usefully for the chapter, they explain how one part of the return operation advanced while political disagreement and another operational prohibition remained.

## April 24–26: what kind of document?

Prueher told Zhou on April 24 that his instructions prevented further discussion of China's proposal until China answered the American request for an assessment team. Zhou said China was prepared to agree dates for that visit and a target for return. He proposed putting them in a non-political document or concrete plan, to be concluded before the end of April. The document's label did not settle what else it would contain. [EPAIR-01, para 2, physical p.2]

Prueher then raised a technical possibility: assessment might show that flying the aircraft out was easier than returning it by ship. Zhou called a flyout symbolically difficult to accept, while saying this was not the core issue; attention should remain on how and when return would occur. This is a contemporary record of discussion that left an option unaccepted. It cannot be turned into either Chinese consent or proof that a categorical refusal had already ended discussion. [EPAIR-01, para 3, physical p.2, visually checked]

The embassy appended its own draft. It proposed starting the return process with inspection, accomplishing return before the end of May, and considering reasonable and appropriate Chinese costs associated with return. The embassy intended to discuss it informally unless instructed otherwise. This was an American proposal, not an agreed timetable. [EPAIR-01, paras 1,5, physical pp.1–4]

The April 25 Washington instruction supplies firmer evidence of authority than a later interview. Its masthead names Deputy Secretary Richard Armitage as approving official. It authorizes discussion of a signed document on specified conditions. The document was to be politically, rather than legally, binding. Washington excluded compensation for the Chinese aircraft, pilot or search and rescue, an apology, and a side verbal agreement on reconnaissance flights. The post had to coordinate with the Joint Staff and the Office of the Secretary of Defense; Washington had to approve the full proposed text before any commitment to signature. Approval to negotiate and approval to sign were different acts. [EPAIR-02, masthead and paras 3–4, physical pp.1–2, visually checked]

The appended Washington draft described continuing the return process discussed on April 18–19, inspection of the aircraft's condition and available return options, and a target date no later than the end of May. Payment consideration was confined to reasonable and appropriate costs directly associated with return, less deliberate damage to the aircraft. Compared with the embassy's April 24 wording, the text narrowed costs and used a target rather than a promise to accomplish return. The documents establish these textual differences; they do not disclose the internal discussion that produced each one. [EPAIR-02, para 5, physical pp.2–3; EPAIR-01, para 5]

Zhou rejected the emphasis when Prueher presented the Washington draft on April 25. The embassy reports him seeking a document that addressed responsibility and avoiding future incidents, connected the return to the April 18–19 talks, and allowed China to explain what had been achieved. Prueher proposed that each side state its views separately. Zhou suggested a factual record of their discussions as a possible basis for the return plan. [EPAIR-03, paras 2–4, physical pp.1–2]

On April 26, Zheng Zeguang explained China's interest more explicitly. According to the embassy report, he said an informal document could help build consensus within the Chinese government, where views differed. He denied seeking a newly negotiated American formulation on responsibility: each side's existing position could simply be restated. The American political minister-counselor replied that he was authorized to discuss only the draft Prueher had supplied. Zheng would consult his superiors. [EPAIR-04, paras 2–5, physical pp.1–2]

Zheng's statement is evidence of an explanation offered by a Chinese negotiator. It does not independently identify the factions, their positions, or the actual approval meeting. Similarly, the embassy's belief that China's desire to remove the plane by May 31 gave Washington leverage is an American assessment, not a recovered Chinese instruction or proof of why the eventual arrangement was accepted. [EPAIR-03, para 5; EPAIR-04, paras 3,6]

## April 29: a Chinese record instead of a joint text

The April 30 report of the previous evening's meeting reproduces both papers. Zhou presented the Chinese document as a record of agreements, but acknowledged that it stood from Beijing's point of view; he neither expected nor requested an American response. The embassy explicitly distinguished this from its own original intention to produce a text accepted by both sides. It thought China was recasting the American proposal as an American-only paper to place beside a Chinese-only paper. No jointly executed instrument is reproduced. [EPAIR-05, paras 2–3, physical p.2; American text para 10, p.7; Chinese text para 11, pp.8–9]

The Chinese non-paper retained China's account of the collision while expressly recording disagreement over responsibility. It placed China's compensation demand alongside the narrower American willingness to consider costs directly associated with return. Representatives would discuss payment items, amount and method. It described further maritime safety discussions and permission for a five-person inspection team. Its assertion that the sides would work for settlement by the end of May was not a guaranteed delivery date. Separately, Zhou described return around that time as a hope dependent on technical circumstances and the American attitude, and asked that it remain private. [EPAIR-05, paras 6,11, physical pp.3,8–9]

Zhou granted the assessment visit despite what he described as an incomplete American response to China's requests. He also urged minimal publicity. The advance on access therefore did not require a common account of blame or a completed payment bargain. It did require dealing with the practical conditions of the visit. [EPAIR-05, paras 4–5, physical pp.2–3]

## April 29–30: the permission had edges

After Zhou's meeting, General Neal Sealock and an embassy political officer discussed the rules with Foreign Ministry Director General Lu Shumin. China initially allowed five assessors and at most two working days at Lingshui, with PLA control of airfield activities. In response to American requests, China permitted an embassy defense attaché officer to accompany the team and left the precise number of working days for decision at the airfield. It also allowed the team's chartered aircraft to fly directly to Haikou, with visas on arrival. That transport permission concerned the aircraft carrying the team, **not** the EP-3. [EPAIR-05, para 9, physical pp.4–7; EPAIR-06, paras 2–3,5, physical pp.2–6]

The prohibition on altering or removing equipment generated another clarification. Lu allowed checks consistent with determining airworthiness, but repeated that Zhou had said the aircraft could not fly from Lingshui. The later cable renders this explicitly as an absence of permission to fly. The embassy noted the apparent tension, but airworthiness assessment and authorization to depart are not equivalent. The report supports neither permission to make all necessary repairs nor consent to a subsequent flyout. [EPAIR-05, para 9, physical pp.5–6; EPAIR-06, para 3, physical p.4]

The April 30 electrical-power exchange makes the limits unusually visible without requiring a theory of deliberate obstruction. First, the Americans said the team needed to power up the aircraft. The MFA replied that a flyout was impossible and power therefore unnecessary, while initially allowing that the matter might be settled at the airfield. It subsequently prohibited powering up and required static inspection. The Americans explained that they did not propose running the engines and repeatedly pressed for electrical power as part of the assessment. The MFA then returned to discussion on the spot in Haikou. Finally, a more senior ministry official said the ministry lacked understanding of the power issue and that the relevant PLA technical officers had already departed for Haikou. He wanted the visit to proceed and proposed addressing power in Hainan. [EPAIR-06, para 4, physical p.5, visually checked]

The cable does not end with permission to power up. Lockheed Martin contractors and the defense attaché office had located a power cart and would arrange rental **if** approval came. Nor does it identify the senior MFA official or establish who could override the no-flyout position. What it does show is a technical question moving from general diplomatic handling toward officers who could address it locally. The agreed relaxation of days and accompanying personnel is firmer evidence of accommodation than the still-pending power request. [EPAIR-06, paras 3–4]

## The recollections and the remaining gap

Prueher's published 2019 interview recalls permission for a repaired aircraft to fly out on May 30, then blames an inflexible American approach for the eventual disassembled return. The interview moves between aircraft recovery and the earlier crew flight; it does not provide a dated permission document or identify when the claimed authorization was issued. Zhou's published 2018 account recalls Chinese rejection of repair and flyout and insistence on disassembly. This pass reread those bounded aircraft passages, not the interviews in full. [Existing EP313, local transcript lines 535–557; DEV-CR01, aircraft-return paragraph]

The April 24 qualification cautions against flattening Zhou's recollection into a transcript of an unchanging refusal from the start. The April 29–30 prohibition cautions against treating Prueher's remembered opportunity as already approved during the assessment arrangement. Neither establishes the whole May negotiation. The cables do not identify an accepted flyout offer, its conditions, revocation, or the cause of a reversal. Retain the later May–July sequence already documented in Chapter 4, but replace the suggestion that the contemporary record contains only public milestones.

## Proposed replacement for the aircraft subsection's opening

*Replace the present two paragraphs beginning “That second task exposed…” and “The contemporary record traces…”; retain the June 7/July 3 paragraph. The draft below incorporates the April 29 and May 29 milestones to avoid repeating them. Existing notes 10 and 31 remain useful; the four new definitions follow.*

Getting the aircraft back reopened questions the crew's departure had left unresolved. On April 24, Prueher suggested that an assessment might show flying it out to be easier than shipping it. According to the embassy's cable, Zhou called a flyout symbolically difficult, but said that was not the core issue. How and when the plane would return still had to be settled.[^aircraft-opening]

Washington gave Prueher a tightly bounded proposal the next day. Armitage approved discussion of a document that would be politically rather than legally binding. There could be no apology, compensation for the lost Chinese aircraft or pilot, or side verbal agreement on reconnaissance flights. Washington reserved approval of the complete text before any commitment to sign. The proposed first step was inspection; the end of May was a target for return.[^aircraft-mandate]

Zhou wanted a broader record of responsibility, payments and preventing another incident. A jointly accepted account proved unnecessary for the next step. On April 29 he delivered a Chinese non-paper, acknowledged that it represented Beijing's view, and said he did not require an American response. It recorded disagreement over blame. He also permitted an assessment team to visit the aircraft. The paper preserved China's position while the permission allowed work to begin.[^aircraft-paper]

Permission remained specific. By April 30, the embassy reported that China had allowed an accompanying American defense attaché officer and room to extend the team's working time. But Chinese officials expressly ruled out flying the EP-3 from Lingshui. Even electrical power required argument. The Foreign Ministry first suggested local flexibility, then prohibited powering up. The Americans explained that assessment required power without running the engines. After repeated requests, the ministry returned the question to discussion in Hainan; a senior official said its own officers did not understand the technical issue and the relevant PLA specialists were already there. Approval was still pending when the embassy reported.[^aircraft-power]

Prueher later remembered an opportunity to fly the repaired aircraft out in May that was lost after the American approach changed. Zhou remembered refusing it.[^31] The April cables establish an objection that became explicit, alongside accommodation over inspection. They do not settle whether permission was offered later. On May 29, China announced agreement in principle on removing the disassembled aircraft aboard a commercial An-124. Technical arrangements remained to be made.[^9]
