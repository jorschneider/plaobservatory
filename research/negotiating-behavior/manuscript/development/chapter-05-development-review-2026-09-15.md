## Finding

The chapter now has a narrative problem, purposeful actors and consequential choices. The Oval Office exchange earns its place: Liu can agree with both Americans without their disagreement disappearing. The subsequent question—what makes the promise worth accepting?—organizes the history of failed pauses, disputed clearance, a smaller package and enforcement. The public disagreement is not made the cause of the May rupture. The absence of the May redline remains visible without swallowing the story. The discussion of firms supplying complaints makes the practical advice unusually concrete.

There is one narrow inference to correct and one valuable addition already supported by the research. Beyond these, further wholesale rewriting would risk losing gains already made.

## Correction: tariff retention is not identical to a remedy

In “The missing agreement,” paragraph beginning “Each of Liu's concerns,” the draft says removing every tariff would, “in Lighthizer's view,” make a Chinese commitment harder to enforce. The newly cited testimony establishes his insistence on a power to take proportionate unilateral action. It does not establish this particular judgment about retaining the existing duties. When Neal asks about putting the tariff threat in abeyance on printed p. 14, Lighthizer declines a specific answer and acknowledges China's objective of removing tariffs. The chapter should either cite a separate direct statement supporting the retention claim or leave that view unattributed and narrower.

Suggested replacement for that sentence, without adding a source claim: “Beijing wanted the existing additional tariffs removed; Washington wanted a means of imposing costs if China failed to perform.” The following sentence about allocation of risk can remain. A further distinction between maintaining a duty and retaining a right to reimpose one would be useful if the argument needs it. This does not undermine the documented fact that the final settlement left substantial duties in place.

## Addition: a concrete boundary inside reform

Suggested placement: in “Whose reform?”, after Liu's January 2020 explanation and before the paragraph beginning “Lighthizer had himself told Congress.” Suggested text:

> Lighthizer had described a similarly specific boundary to Congress. When Linda Sánchez asked about obtaining a larger share of Chinese box-office receipts for American studios, he called revenue-sharing a priority. Distribution was more complicated. He said the negotiation was not taking on “challenging control in China.” The terms remained unresolved. This was an American statement of what to seek and what to leave outside the bargain, not a film concession secured in Phase One. [Proposed note: DEV-TR02, printed pp. 57–58.]

Suggested note: House Ways and Means, *U.S.–China Trade*, February 27, 2019, printed pp. 57–58/physical PDF pp. 61–62, DEV-TR02. The reviewer reread both complete pages. Sánchez's descriptions of the 2012 agreement, its asserted 2017 requirement and a 40-percent benchmark are her statements and are not adopted here. Lighthizer confirms the priority, distinguishes revenue-sharing from distribution, excludes a challenge to control and says the issue is unresolved. These pages do not establish the subsequent film bargain or Chinese acceptance.

The paragraph gives a concrete commercial object to the claim that economically valuable terms can be sought within a defended political boundary. It is stronger than another general sentence about respect for the other's system. “Control” should remain Lighthizer's wording; the chapter should not silently expand it into a finding about every form of censorship, Party authority or film distribution.

## Remaining literary issue

The strongest practical payoff currently arrives in the complaint section. Preserve its space. If length must be reduced after adding film, trim the repeated advice about choosing a smaller package or the final general restatement rather than the chronology or the explanation of corporate dependence. The section on purchases should retain its exact distinctions but avoid growing into a second implementation chapter; the next chapter carries the results.

The closing pressure assessment is appropriately bounded. It can establish that tariffs structured the exchange without claiming to isolate the effect of resolve-learning. The book still owes the reader a stronger later account of contemporary Chinese leverage; that should come from the active 2025 research, not a causal claim inserted into the 2019 gap.
