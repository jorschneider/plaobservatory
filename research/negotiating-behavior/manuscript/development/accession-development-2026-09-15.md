## What the chapter now does

The original draft organized much of its explanation around successive source checks. The rewrite follows three problems that officials had to solve: obtaining a package Washington could accept, deciding what another day in Beijing could buy, and giving Europe compensation around an ownership limit it would have to accept. The chapter opens with the March 30 Wu Yi–Barshefsky disagreement about political support. The book’s newly rewritten introduction already uses the November 12 letter, so repeating that opening would spend the reader’s attention twice on the same revelation.

The March exchange supplies specific disagreement. In the Chinese published account, Wu argues that American businesses and farmers can already be satisfied while opponents of China or Clinton’s policy cannot. Barshefsky directs attention to an eroding middle and business representatives who speak differently to different audiences. The analytic question is the value of additional concessions when domestic support is uncertain. The paragraph does not declare which official understood American politics better.

Barshefsky’s remembered warning to Zhu, Summers’s criticism of publicizing concessions without an agreement, his October assurance, and Barshefsky’s explanation for taking Sperling to Beijing put Washington’s capacity to commit inside the negotiation. The chapter does not reconcile the oral histories into a single authoritative White House account. Their different positions are part of the story. The April signed memorandum remains evidence of both extensive progress and consequential unfinished terms.

The November section develops the letter beyond a threat to leave. Its original English expressly puts discussion of China’s equity sensitivities inside a final package satisfying American requirements on safeguards and non-market-economy antidumping. That establishes an offered cross-issue exchange. It does not establish the final private trade or an already authorized numerical equity concession. The successive papers and public outcome summary preserve the three-, twelve-, fifteen- and twenty-year terms as distinct proposals or reported results for different instruments.

Deng supplies the conditions under which the technical team worked: fourteen departments, camp beds, pagers and the need to obtain specific permissions. His dismissal of American departure tactics is left in tension with Barshefsky’s stated purpose of accelerating the internal decision. The red-telephone anecdote is his, with no exact date supplied; it is not fused with the other account into an invented final-day scene. No reconstructed dialogue, room layout, facial expression or private thought was added.

Europe’s negotiation earns its place through a different problem. Lamy’s March meeting with Zhu did not produce a settlement; according to the Chinese account, his own authorization prevented him from accepting the offered ownership ceilings. By May, he could defend accepting some ceilings with compensating gains. On mobile telecommunications he sought authoritative confirmation of the 49 percent limit after reporting a different understanding to ministers. The meeting could help him defend acceptance of a constraint while Zhu approved compensation in principle. It is not narrated as the instant at which Brussels changed its instructions.

## Practical propositions and their limits

The deadline advice concerns a specific choice. A departure can terminate an unproductive round or cut off an imminent authorization. Staying can buy a decision but reveal that the announced clock is flexible. The November 12 letter tied additional time to a specified meeting. The chapter asks what decision an extension can obtain and who can make it; it does not prescribe walkout theater or claim that the letters caused China’s concessions.

The authority advice concerns a specific concession. The financial official who understands a request may not be permitted to accept it; the foreign negotiator’s own president or member governments can be another obstacle. Sending an official associated with a potential domestic obstacle may make an assurance more credible, but the chapter does not claim to measure Chinese reactions to Sperling’s presence. A senior meeting needs an actionable package, and access alone cannot establish that a commitment has cleared either government.

The compensation advice is conditional. Faster timing, additional geographic access, permitted activities and licenses can make acceptance of a retained ceiling worthwhile. They cannot automatically replace control when control is indispensable to the affected firm. Confirmation of a ceiling mattered here because Lamy had a specific mandate problem, not because any senior restatement of a refusal carries commercial value. The final schedule, licensing promises and later multilateral questions keep those benefits attached to their different legal and administrative stages.

Replace with: “During normalization, American officials sought a political accommodation with Beijing. Accession later gave Chinese officials standing to invoke WTO procedures against the United States.” This preserves the comparison without suggesting an existing WTO in the normalization years.
- **Narrow Chapter 2’s final bridge to DS379.** “Rules it had helped negotiate” can suggest that China negotiated the pre-accession SCM Agreement provisions decisive in the public-body and double-remedy litigation. Accession negotiations do not establish that. Suggested replacement: “The next chapter follows a dispute in which China could challenge American conduct under multilateral rules that membership now allowed it to invoke.”
- **Compress one duplicated technical sequence in the introduction.** Its full Deng paragraph repeats the Geneva–ministry–regulator chain, State Council decision and Dai leasing approval developed in Chapter 2. Keep the authority problem there, but leave the specific approvals to the case chapter. For example: “Behind the delegation stood several authorities. Financial negotiator Deng Hongguo later described sector officials seeking permission for particular concessions while the trade ministry assembled the package. An official could understand a request without being authorized to accept it.” Retain the existing source note. The November letters and life-insurance example can remain as the introduction’s concrete opening; they return with additional commercial terms and conflicting participant explanations in Chapter 2.

### A possible introduction paragraph on industrial leverage

The new Chapter 6 evidence supports a concise addition after the paragraph on uneven Phase One performance, before returning to the 2023 political relationship. That location extends the trade argument; a date transition should make clear the subsequent paragraph returns to 2023. No new source acquisition was undertaken for this editorial proposal.

> The 2025 negotiations exposed another source of Chinese bargaining power: permission to supply factories abroad. China’s export controls covered specified rare-earth items, including some magnet materials. After talks in London, Beijing described an arrangement under which it would approve eligible export applications while Washington removed particular restrictions. Software companies subsequently reported relief from American controls, and magnet shipments recovered. China’s licensing authority remained. For a foreign government, obtaining urgently needed supplies could therefore mean accepting continued dependence on future permissions. The bargain concerned access in both directions, and the value of relief depended on which transactions could actually proceed.

Support: Chapter 6 notes 36–37, 42 and 44–46; EC25-01, EC25-06–08, EC25-11, EC25-18 and EC25-26. This wording attributes the public account of the arrangement, does not infer repeal of the April notice, does not equate magnet totals with every controlled end use, and does not claim that Chinese export leverage was invented in 2025. It also avoids treating a reported attempted advanced-chip linkage as an obtained concession.
