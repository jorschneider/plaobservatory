## Result and scope

The strongest addition is the distinction between an exporter obtaining **some licenses** and a customer obtaining the **right material, for the declared use, in sufficient quantity and time**. This distinction was already built into the Chinese procedure before the June London negotiations. The public undertaking to approve eligible applications operated through it. A named Chinese producer and a named Indian customer-side firm make that distinction tangible without pretending their supply chains or applications can be matched.

The evidence supports two narrative improvements, proposed below. It does not recover an actual rare-earth license, an application returned for correction, a complete administrative case file, an internal priority instruction, or the undisclosed London framework. ## 1. A specified transaction, not a general reopening

The 2024 regulation, effective December 1 that year, distinguishes single licenses from general licenses. A single license permits one specified export to one end user within its recorded conditions, with a maximum validity of one year; it expires when the permitted export is completed. A general license can cover repeated specified exports to one or several end users for up to three years. Both existed in law before April 2025. Their legal availability does not establish that a particular rare-earth exporter received either type during June. Changing a licensed item's type, destination, end user or end use requires a new application and a halt in export. The end user must undertake not to alter the use or transfer the item to a third party without permission. These are consequential limits on what a producer can promise a buyer. [LIC25-02, Articles 15–18 and 24.]

The March-dated application guide gives the practical unit: generally one application per contract/order, with explanations for splitting and no combining several contract numbers in one application. It permits up to twelve certificates for phased shipments, keeping their quantities within the approved total. End-use wording must be specific and consistent across documents and translations. Intermediaries require explanation or contracts connecting the parties. Company profiles and an explanation of quantities help officials assess the proposed transaction. [LIC25-01, application fields 20/22/31; self-check sections; FAQs 19–25 and 29/31.]

The statutory decision is not exhausted by checking whether the form is complete. The 2020 Export Control Law lists national security and interests, international obligations and external commitments, export type, sensitivity, destination, end user/use and the exporter's credit record among the decision factors. The regulation also allows referral of exports with major national-security implications to the State Council or State Council and Central Military Commission. This establishes legally stated discretion and different levels of authorization; it does not reveal a June instruction to reject, accelerate or ration a named firm's order. [LIC25-09, Articles 8 and 12–16; LIC25-02, Article 17.]

Product classification itself had operational importance. The ministry's rare-earth FAQ distinguished simple permanent-magnet forms and related assemblies, which remained covered, from further processed electronic components such as motors and products such as speakers or headphones. This is a boundary in the published rules, not proof that a particular rotor, motor or shipment qualified, and not evidence that firms could immediately replace an existing design. The captured licensing-bureau repost is dated **May 6**, although other indexed reproductions identify an April 21 original. I use the acquired May text and do not backdate its acquisition or assert an unrecovered original. [LIC25-03, all three Q&A, especially Q3.]

## 2. A review clock with an entry condition

The 45-working-day period ran from acceptance. The guide says that where an application was returned for additional material, the review time ran from receipt of complete, compliant material. Article 17 excludes identification, expert consultation and site-inspection time; higher-level approvals are outside the ordinary deadline. A promised decision period was therefore not a guaranteed delivery date measured from an importer's first effort to assemble documents. [LIC25-01, FAQ 6; LIC25-02, Article 17.]

This supplements the chapter's existing Reuters June 5 report on administrative burden. It cannot settle the alternative explanations in that article. An application may require substantial verification; political priorities may affect administration; neither follows merely from elapsed time. The record lacks the submission, acceptance, return, resubmission and decision dates needed to distinguish them for a firm.

The document burden should be described accurately. The guide requires specified technical and end-user information, but says firms **may also provide** production-scene or product photographs. Do not convert this into a universal rule requiring factory photographs or proprietary schematics. Specific reported demands, such as the trader's example in EC25-25, must retain their own attribution.

### Six months predates London, with a version-control check

FAQ 33 says single-license certificates were generally valid for six months. This is reproduced in an April 2, 2025 Sichuan trade-promotion-platform copy and a partial indexed April 15 Xinping county-government copy. Consequently the June 15 Reuters report of six-month licenses does not, by itself, identify a newly negotiated six-month restriction. The duration could be a normal license term, a confirmed term of the accommodation, or both. The underlying certificates and framework are still missing. The statutory one-year ceiling and the guide's usual six months are compatible, not conflicting rules. [LIC25-01; LIC25-02, Article 15; reused EC25-17.]

A genuine version issue makes this check necessary: the ministry page still bears March 28, but its present FAQ 44 says classification advice has no validity period, whereas the April reposts say six months. This concerns an **item-classification advice letter**, not the export-license term in FAQ 33. No revision date was recovered. The details used above were checked against the April 2 copy, including the clock, documents, contract structure and six-month license answer. I do not assume every sentence on the current ministry page was present in March. Nor does the pre-existing general-license category establish the date or coverage of later rare-earth general-license relief.

## 3. An exporter with approvals and a manufacturer still facing choices

On June 13, JL Mag's board secretary Lai Xunlong described the company's exports to visiting investors at its Baotou operation. The company said it had begun applications after the controls, had successively obtained permits for destinations including the United States, Europe and Southeast Asia, and had no military business. It still said controlled products would be exported only after obtaining permission. The June 26 record repeats the same position. These company-issued documents add direct corporate testimony to the press accounts; their repetition is not an independent second witness. Neither supplies customer names, license numbers, dates, quantities, expiry terms or proof that all orders were cleared. First-quarter revenue figures concern the period **before** the April controls and must not be presented as post-London delivery evidence. [LIC25-04/05, each physical p2, Q2.]

The contrast is an Indian manufacturer's account, not a matched JL Mag customer. On May 29, Bajaj executive director Rakesh Sharma described a chain running from declarations by its importing vendors through Indian certification and the Chinese embassy, then Chinese provincial authorities and the commerce ministry. His information was that about thirty applications had been forwarded; he did not identify thirty Bajaj applications or claim that MOFCOM had accepted them. Substitution required development, testing and integration, so the immediate effort was to get the process moving. [LIC25-07, printed/physical pp4 and17.]

The near-contemporaneous August 6 call supplies the bounded July outcome and the evolving choice. Sharma said magnet unavailability had begun compromising production in June and described a 50 percent July shortfall in the Chetak discussion. CFO Dinesh Thapar specified June supplies at 60 percent of the electric-two-wheeler plan and 75 percent of the electric-three-wheeler plan—**not** equivalent percentage declines year on year. Management was pursuing official permission alongside other sources and motor alternatives; its forecast of recovery was still a forecast. This does not show that the thirty May applications were all pending in August, explain a Chinese decision, or make Indian approval an obligation of the US agreement. [LIC25-08, pp4–5,7,16–17.]

There were costs on the Chinese side too. Reuters' July 7 original report attributes to four anonymous sources the difficulty of selling customized magnet cargoes domestically while waiting for licenses. It reports producer pressure and an exchange's account of accumulating inventory; neither establishes a state plan to damage domestic suppliers or a quantified industry loss. The material can counter any effortless-leverage narrative, but the named company records above make a stronger short addition to the chapter than another general account of industrial distress. [LIC25-06, complete article; decisive paragraph beginning “In addition, the highly customised nature…”.]

## 4. What the negotiated text does—and does not—explain

The May 12 joint statement requires administrative action on the non-tariff countermeasures taken against the US since April 2 by May 14. It names neither a license class nor a customer, quantity, expiry term or individual processing deadline. The June 27 Chinese account then specifies approval of eligible controlled-item applications under Chinese law and corresponding removal of a series of US restrictions. It reports a reciprocal implementation arrangement, not elimination of application requirements. The primary procedural documents explain the machinery through which that arrangement could reach firms. They do not establish the entire private bargain or adjudicate the subsequent competing claims of breach. [Reused EC25-02 and EC25-06.]

This supports a precise practical judgment: a negotiator seeking restored supply needs to know whether relief attaches to a transaction, a defined set of users, or repeated shipments. A buyer needs to know whether it can perform its production plan under those permissions. It remains an inference from the institutions and corporate testimony, not evidence that either delegation articulated this exact framework internally.

The new evidence does not displace the chapter's existing five-of-180 Dexter example, distinguish administrative congestion from deliberate pressure in that company's applications, or demonstrate uniquely Chinese behavior. Nor should the June arrangement be projected through subsequent changes. The parent is researching October 2025–May 2026 separately.

## Two proposed narrative improvements

**1. Make the unit and clock of permission concrete, before the Geneva paragraph, after the industrial-dependence paragraph.** This supplies the missing premise for the chapter's later advice; retain the detailed scope in the note.

> The application system explains why an agreement to resume trade could leave a factory waiting. The ordinary application was tied to a contract, supported by a particular end user and use. Changing the destination, user or use of an approved export required a new application. Even the advertised review period did not start when a foreign customer first asked for supplies: the ordinary forty-five-working-day clock ran from acceptance, and a returned application awaited complete, compliant material. Time for technical identification, expert consultation or an on-site check could fall outside it. An undertaking to approve eligible transactions could therefore leave firms having to establish which transactions qualified.[^licensing-procedure]

**2. Add the named Chinese producer's continuing choices near the June/July company paragraph.** Keep the Bajaj comparison in this memo unless greater cross-country detail is needed.

> A Chinese supplier's account was equally specific about the limits of the opening. In June, JL Mag's board secretary Lai Xunlong told visiting investors that the magnet producer had successively obtained export permits for markets including America, Europe and Southeast Asia. The company, which said it had no military business, would continue applying for products covered by the controls and ship them after approval. Its June 26 account repeated that position. Its permits opened a route through the controls while further controlled exports still required approval.[^licensing-firms]
