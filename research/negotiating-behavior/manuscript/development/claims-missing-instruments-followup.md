# Claims/assets: following the missing instruments

**16 September 2026; bounded follow-up to the draft at b7eadd4.** This pass sought the June 14, 1974 Chinese reply, the December 22, 1973 American memorandum, and the unprinted attachments to Solomon’s December 31 memorandum. None of those exact texts has been recovered. It did recover a more precise microfilm lead for the June cable, a complete second Lin–Habib meeting, a contemporaneous presidential briefing paper, and two released State cable texts through a public mirror. Those sources clarify the disputed substance without deciding who broke an assurance or why Beijing withdrew Zhou’s offer. The [separate register](claims-missing-instruments-followup-register.json) records five substantive documents, two metadata-only records, reading scopes and hashes. No chapter, baseline or shared apparatus has been edited.

## What the second meeting adds

The complete November 28, 1974 morning meeting occupies physical pages 35–45 of the [Ford Library scan](https://www.fordlibrarymuseum.gov/sites/default/files/pdf_documents/library/document/0331/1553936.pdf), printed pages 1–11 (**CLA-I01**). Its first page supplies an unusually important qualification: the secretary who took verbatim notes mislaid her stenographic pad after returning to Washington, and the memorandum was prepared from Oscar Armstrong’s notes. It is an official contemporary reconstruction, not a surviving verbatim transcript. All eleven pages were read; the header and complete claims portion, printed 6–11, were visually checked.

Habib returned to the issue after the November 26 disagreement already used in Chapter 1. He said he had reviewed the record and concluded that the old bonds need not obstruct settlement. Washington had not asked Beijing to accept them as claims against the PRC: it had raised them to explain their exclusion. He even allowed that perhaps the Americans should not have raised the subject. His review of asset definitions similarly presented American and Chinese drafting changes as attempts to preserve the content of an earlier package, not to introduce new demands. These were Habib’s assessments, not the memorandum’s independent findings. (Printed 6–7 / physical 40–41.)

Lin identified a different problem. The American statement about bonds, he said, disclaimed government espousal but could not prevent private claimants from acting. To Lin, the disclaimer and the continuing possibility of claims were contradictory. Habib’s answer—that the bonds were not part of the settlement—did not provide the protection Lin was asking about. Lin briefly recast Habib’s position as saying that Washington had never raised the issue; Habib corrected him. This correction matters: exclusion from the proposed agreement and absence from the discussions were different propositions. They agreed to disagree. (Printed 7–8 / physical 41–42.)

The discussion of asset definitions also makes the stakes concrete. Habib wanted wording that would prevent an owner from suing the United States on the ground that an asset had not been assigned. Lin objected that the categories came from the hostile American measures of 1950; accepting them would have political consequences. He said the December 22 aide-mémoire changed the method but not the substance. That is a more specific objection than aversion to legal precision, but it remains his characterization of an unavailable instrument. His warning that acceptance would produce a strong reaction is a negotiating statement, not independent evidence of a leadership instruction or an actual domestic reaction. (Printed 8–9 / physical 42–43.)

Finally, both invoked a package while disagreeing about its contents. Habib wanted all items, including Zhou’s $17 million, inside the eventual settlement. Lin said the money had already been returned through third-country banks, that relations with those countries were involved, and that the banks had raised the matter. He linked withdrawal to the American side’s alleged new demands. A political package, he maintained, need not contain every small item. The source establishes the competing uses of the package concept; it does not independently verify the banks’ interventions, the asserted American breach or the Chinese internal decision. (Printed 10–11 / physical 44–45.)

## An earlier American disclaimer can now be read

The public reproduction of **State 105075, May 31, 1973** (**CLA-I04**) includes the complete two-page cable, drafting and clearance header, and release metadata. Paragraph 1 quotes the American statement made in Paris on March 21. It excluded the pre-1949 bonds from the proposed exchange of letters, asserted a general successor-government principle while declining to address it then, disclaimed US government espousal, and left direct negotiation and settlement to bondholders or their representatives and the governments concerned. Paragraph 3 reports Ni Yao-li telling Chas. Freeman on May 4 that the new bond subject required Chinese study. Paragraph 4 recommends a quiet inquiry when an opportunity arose, rather than requesting a special meeting. [Full released-text reproduction.](https://wikileaks.org/plusd/cables/1973STATE105075_b.html)

This is contemporaneous support for the substantive distinction Lin later raised. It does **not** prove his stronger claim that Washington had demanded PRC acceptance of all old liabilities as a condition of the settlement. The quoted American statement expressly excluded those claims from this instrument. Nor does it resolve which assurances Kissinger subsequently gave or whether the December memorandum fulfilled them. The cable quotes an earlier statement; it is not the original March enclosure or its complete drafting history.

A second complete released-text reproduction, **State 248454, October 18, 1975**, expressly repeats **State 209591 of October 24, 1973** (**CLA-I05**). The reproduced 1973 instructions go further than non-espousal: Washington would actively discourage attachment of Chinese property, doubted such litigation would succeed, and wanted no present Chinese action on the bonds. Its internal paragraph names American claimant/Congress problems and the implications for recognition of competing Chinese governments as reasons not to include the bonds. These are actual instructions to make a presentation, including one point left to the liaison office’s discretion; the cable alone does not establish when or exactly how they were delivered. [Full reproduction.](https://wikileaks.org/plusd/cables/1975STATE248454_b.html)

The dates must stay separate. This is a 1975 retransmission of a 1973 instruction, not a newly authorized October 1975 concession. The October 27, 1973 aide-mémoire that Habib proposed withdrawing in the already-read 1975 meeting may be related, but textual identity has not been demonstrated. The reproduction also contains a duplicated line and an irregular subparagraph label; these should be retained as transcription features rather than silently corrected into a supposed original.

Both texts were obtained from WikiLeaks’ public library of previously released US diplomatic records. Their metadata identifies them as released/unclassified, with State systematic-review markings. They were not acquired from an official government host in this pass. The linked raw PDF for State 105075 returned 404, so no scan comparison is claimed. The earlier advocacy compilation that supplied the identifiers is not counted as independent evidence; its legal allegations and its excerpts from William Burr are not substituted for the documents or the unread article.

## Senior intervention and American conditions

Later on November 28, 1974, Kissinger told Deng that Habib had briefed him and that he would ask the lawyers for a definition compatible with Chinese principles. He accepted the principle that American law did not apply to China and promised to seek a formulation. The exchange is in **FRUS XVIII/98**, printed 628–629 (**CLA-I02**); the claims exchange, immediate context, header and all editorial notes were read, not the entire long meeting. [Official publication.](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v18/d98)

This is a useful bridge between the officials’ disagreement and the 1975 impasse. The sovereign-law objection reached the senior interlocutor, and Kissinger offered a principle and further work. Neither was a completed asset definition. The sequence does not support a simple picture in which a leader’s settled agreement was merely thwarted by officials below him: the senior conversation itself still left the operative wording to be found. It also does not disprove political resistance inside China.

A four-page State **“Claims/Assets” briefing paper, November 1975**, supplies a revealing contemporary limit on the American explanation (**CLA-I03**). It judged from the June note and subsequent talks that China did not want settlement, but explicitly said that the reasons could only be speculated about. It listed lack of normalized relations, domestic rivalries, low priority given existing trade, linkage to most-favored-nation treatment, and precedent for other countries’ claims. These were alternatives considered inside the American government, not verified Chinese motives. [Official Ford scan, physical 3–6 / printed 1–4.](https://www.fordlibrarymuseum.gov/sites/default/files/pdf_documents/library/document/0358/035800374.pdf)

The same paper recommended that Ford not raise the issue. Even if China unexpectedly accepted, it advised against formalizing settlement before consultation with key members of Congress. This is evidence of State’s recommended American approval sequence, not proof of a presidential order, subsequent consultation or an actual vote. It strengthens the chapter’s attention to authority on both sides without establishing that American institutional delay caused the failure. All four pages were read and visually checked; other papers in the 32-page folder were not systematically read.

## Where the exact instruments remain

The [June 14 cable record](https://wikileaks.org/plusd/cables/1974PEKING00985_b.html) (**CLA-IA01**) matches the FRUS lead, **Peking 985**, and supplies microfilm **P850038-1564**. Its metadata says released/unclassified, but its body reports an error reading the text index and no available telegram text. It identifies State 126228 as a reference. That [June 13 record](https://wikileaks.org/plusd/cables/1974STATE126228_b.html) (**CLA-IA02**) likewise has unavailable electronic text and microfilm **P850038-1561**. Its reference field includes “74 PEKING 979” and “74 STATE 240796”; this is retained literally, not repaired or treated as a verified chronological trail. Neither metadata record supplies the Chinese note. Even a later retrieval of the cable would have to establish whether it reproduces the full note or reports it.

The exact-number public-mirror request for **1973 Peking 1695**, cited by FRUS as the December 29 attachment, returned a general full-text search page rather than that document. The page is preserved as a failed retrieval. FRUS XVIII/65 still points to Nixon NSC Files, Box 527, PRC Volume 8, for Solomon’s memorandum and attachments. The earlier folder-level finding aid remains a lead, not an acquired attachment. The December 22 Nixon almanac’s linked catalog route yielded no exact document; targeted date/name/subject searches returned previously known meeting references, not the memorandum itself.

The CASS Institute of American Studies page for Mao Ruipeng’s 2012 article, *关系正常化前夕的中美资产要求问题谈判*, was pursued for citations: the HTTPS connection timed out and HTTP returned 403. Burr’s 2009 *Diplomatic History* article was likewise not acquired (publisher access failed). No full-reading claim is made for either. The remaining useful targets are now specific instruments and microfilm frames; further broad searching would add less than these exact originals. This pass stops without treating online unavailability as evidence of continued classification or a reason to suspend the bounded narrative.

## Proposed Chapter 1 insertion (204 words, excluding note)

Place after the November 26 paragraph and before “Habib brought another package.” If integrated, remove that paragraph’s final sentence (“One official saw…”), whose interpretation the new passage makes concrete.

When they met again two days later, Habib said he had reviewed the record. Washington had raised the bonds to explain that they were outside the settlement, not to ask China to accept them. Lin identified the protection still missing: refusing to support bondholders did not stop them from making claims. An earlier American cable confirms the distinction. Its account of the March 1973 statement promised that Washington would not espouse the claims, while leaving direct negotiation to bondholders and the governments concerned. Later instructions offered actively to discourage attachment of Chinese property. Neither assurance amounted to extinguishing the claims.

The remaining asset definitions mattered for a different reason. Habib wanted to prevent owners from suing Washington over whether their assets had been assigned. Lin objected to accepting categories inherited from the American measures of 1950. He maintained that the December memorandum had altered the method without abandoning the demand. Even “package settlement” meant different things: Habib wanted all the items included; Lin said a political package need not contain every small item. Their disagreement concerned what an agreement would protect and what it would concede. The missing December and June instruments still prevent deciding whether Washington had failed to honor an earlier assurance.[^claims-instruments]
