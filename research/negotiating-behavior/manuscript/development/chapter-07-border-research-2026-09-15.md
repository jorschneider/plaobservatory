# Russian boundary development: sovereignty, working access and renewal

September 15, 2026. This bounded pass produces a replacement for the two Russian boundary subsections of Chapter 7, not a rewrite of the India or gas material. The replacement has approximately 1,470 narrative words, followed by integration notes. The chapter and shared apparatus are unchanged. The [new-acquisitions register](chapter-07-border-register.json) contains the additional documents; the original Russian border inventory remains untouched.

## What materially changed

The principal recovery is **Annex 2 of the December 9, 1999 joint economic use agreement**. The earlier FAO reproduction expressly omitted both annexes. A targeted search found a scholarly footnote directing readers to a fuller Russian legal reproduction; a Chinese-language search then located the official MFA publication. All nine pages of that Chinese publication were read visually: the complete twelve-article body and all six articles of Annex 2. The MFA catalogue's Russian attachment supplies a facsimile with a dated signature block and the same annex. That attachment was inventoried page by page; selected physical 8, 10 and 11 were read closely. Neither official attachment contains Annex 1's maps and numerical caps.

The new sources turn a general statement about permits into a specific administrative bargain. The Chinese body, physical 4, already distinguishes a home-country crossing pass from a host-country activity permit. Annex 2, physical 7, names the issuing authorities: China's Public Security Ministry and Russia's border representative for crossing passes; the governments of Inner Mongolia and Heilongjiang, and the Amur administration, for activity permits. Physical 7–8 imposes sixty-day requests and fifteen-day issuance while preserving an express right to refuse an individual applicant. Physical 8–9 provides for replacement of departing users without increasing the total, removal of equipment and produce, overnight stays by consent, and withdrawal of access for violations. The Russian facsimile, physical 10–11, confirms the named offices and the tension between an issuance timetable and individual refusal power.

The argument is therefore more precise than “legal agreement produced access.” It created an administratively usable route, with both obligations and reserved discretion. The documents do not reveal whether an application was refused, whether deadlines were met, or how many people benefited. The three blank forms in the private Russian reproduction are templates, not completed permits. The replacement does not present an invented farmer's experience or claim that every established practice continued unchanged.

A second material recovery is the **October 14, 2004 renewal protocol**, obtained directly from the MFA treaty catalogue. Both pages were read visually. Its preamble identifies **March 1, 2005** as the existing end date; Article I extends joint economic use for five years from that date. Article III makes the protocol effective on signature, and the catalogue separately records October 14, 2004 for both signature and entry into force. This supplies a documented renewal of a temporary accommodation. It does not prove present-day continuation or permit volume. The five years in the original agreement began with the activity, on dates to be agreed diplomatically; they did not run automatically from December 9, 1999 or January 19, 2000. The rewrite avoids that common error.

## Existing sources independently revisited

The current Chapter 7 body and notes, Russian boundary case, and relevant register entries were read before drafting. Iwashita's Chapter 6, printed 161–171, was reread, especially 164–168. His actual account is richer than the old compressed summary: residents used Menkeseli for pasture and grass collection; the local Border Guard consulted the Chita administration and developed the local-use proposal; demarcation chief Genrikh Kireev praised Chita's cooperation in July 1995. The proposal-origin chain is his regional newspaper evidence, not a newly recovered Chinese offer. His reported change from an initial ten-year period to five is likewise attributed; no offer draft identifies who requested the reduction. His claim that the solution was decisive is not converted into an independently estimated causal result.

Chapter 1, printed 18–20, was reread for Nazdratenko's contrasting campaign and federal reaction. Its unverified claim of a statutory five-year deadline invalidating the 1991 agreement remains excluded. The existing project previously read Chapters 1, 3 and 6 in full; this pass does not claim a new whole-book reading. Fravel's printed 63–64 and 76, with notes 102–106, were reread against the existing complete-article memo. The replacement retains competing explanations of the 1991 sequence and does not extend his domestic-insecurity account to 2004, which he expressly excludes.

The 1991 agreement's Articles III–X and signature/effectiveness block were rechecked visually on physical 9–11; coordinate material on physical 8 was also displayed, but no full coordinate or map verification is claimed. The 1999 FAO Russian body was reread completely. The 2004 supplementary agreement's seven-article Chinese body was reread from the existing extraction; the existing published order's paragraphs 1–2 were reread directly. The order concerns three drafts: boundary, navigation and applicability of existing agreements. The local-use renewal is separate, and the replacement does not invent a combined mandate over four documents.

The complete substantive bodies of the October 22 and November 4, 2004 and May 21, 2005 *Kommersant* reports were reread. The October 22 action is a committee initiative toward an appeal, with a later full regional-duma discussion anticipated; it is not described as a completed regional legislative rejection. Ishaev's assurances about retained airport access, fortifications, farming and dachas remain his assertions. The newspaper's loose initial attribution of signing to Putin is corrected with the agreement and November report identifying Li Zhaoxing and Lavrov. The May article reports the 307–80 ratification vote and different military and border concerns. Its allegations about Chinese manipulation of river channels are not adopted as facts. The existing October 14, 2008 Chinese briefing's boundary exchange was reread to distinguish the descriptive protocol and annexes from the 2004 agreement.

## Targeted search and remaining limits

Searches used the complete Russian and Chinese treaty titles; the Russian simplified-crossing-rule title; the exact LawRussia URL cited by Tadeusz Dmochowski; Chinese terms for Annex 2, permits, sixty-day requests and the Menkeseli name; the exact Annex 1 title; Russian island names combined with haymaking, permits and 2000/2001/2005; and the renewal's 2004 date. The linked Russian attachment in the official catalogue was checked specifically for missing maps. All fourteen pages are text, with Annex 2 beginning at physical 9; there is no Annex 1 or completed permit.

The search recovered **one of the two missing annexes**, a signed-instrument facsimile, blank document forms, and the subsequent official renewal. It did **not** recover Annex 1, issued permits, annual applicant lists, inspection reports or signed twice-yearly consultation minutes. This is a bounded negative result, not a claim that those records do not exist.

Dmochowski's 2018 article, printed 64–68, especially note 20, supplied the successful acquisition lead and the renewal lead. The author distinguishes the incomplete official Russian bulletin from a fuller online text; his interpretation of the arrangement's importance is not an independent local-impact measurement. Only the relevant section and bibliographic opening were read, not the whole article. Li Chuanxun's Hokkaido-hosted Chinese study was read through the regional-cooperation and local-use discussion, printed 16–17 and the opening of 18. It says residents had pursued haymaking, fishing and cultivation under the agreement for three years, but supplies no source for that particular claim and misdates the agreement September 9 rather than December 9, 1999. Its broad implementation claim is recorded, not treated as a permit record or used to create a scene. The paper's remaining economic sections were not read.

The separate Russian resolution 1415 reproduction includes an expressly labeled draft, dated December 21, 1999—after the signed agreement's date. Its Annex 1 is explicitly not reproduced. It is useful for distinguishing document status, but the unexplained chronology is not reconstructed from conjecture. The agreed rules in the narrative rest on the Chinese official text and Russian facsimile, not on this draft.

## Editorial choices and handoff

The replacement opens with the cost imposed on local use rather than a catalogue of instruments. It then identifies a Russian-origin implementation proposal, China's acceptance and the terms that prevented permission from becoming shared sovereignty. A short legal and historical bridge preserves the distinction between 1991 partial settlement and 2004 closure without making motive auditing the narrative center. The 2004 mandate and contested ratification show why coordination with regional executives did not establish public or legislative unanimity. The last paragraph draws the usable lesson and its cost: sovereignty, access and duration can sometimes be separated, but temporary users bear the risk of host discretion and expiry.

Existing note identifiers retained: 18–20, 22–24 and 26–29. Existing note 21 is folded into 19; note 25's 2001 treaty discussion is omitted from this fragment. New definitions are **B-new1** and **B-new2**. Main text contains no direct copyrighted quotation. The proposal-origin and duration-change claims remain specifically attributed to Iwashita; the new permit and renewal facts rest on government documents. Nothing here establishes a uniquely Chinese technique, a Xi-era transformation, the decisive cause of the 2004 allocation or equal economic benefits from reciprocal permission.
