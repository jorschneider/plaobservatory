# Claims and assets: the contested condition behind the withdrawn offer

**16 September 2026. Bounded archival follow-up.** The exact June 14, 1974 Chinese message and the unprinted attachments to Solomon's December 31, 1973 memorandum remain unrecovered. Two newly acquired Ford Library scans nevertheless materially improve the reconstruction. In November 1974, Lin Ping explicitly explained the withdrawal as a response to what China regarded as an unmet American assurance about pre-1949 liabilities. Philip Habib disputed that interpretation. In October 1975, the two officials debated a new American drafting package, and Lin again identified the June 14 letter as withdrawing the repayment offer. These are contemporary American records of attributed Chinese statements, not Chinese internal instructions.

## What the newly read exchange changes

On **November 26, 1974**, Habib opened the claims discussion by treating Zhou's $17 million proposal as accepted, the bond issue as resolved by American governmental noninvolvement, and identification of the assigned assets as the remaining technical problem. He connected the definition to compliance with American legal requirements and protection against litigation directed at the US government. He asked Lin to confirm that an agreement in principle still existed and needed only an acceptable means of execution. **CLA-F01**, printed 7 / PDF 46.

Lin did not accept that description. He said the Chinese reply of **June 14** had answered an American memorandum of **December 22, 1973**. In his account, the American March draft had introduced two objections: the designation of Chinese nationals and responsibility for liabilities predating the People's Republic. The December response had changed the handling of these questions without removing their substance. Habib answered that Washington was not attached to the particular designation and had never required China to acknowledge the old liabilities. Printed 7–8 / PDF 46–47.

The decisive next passage identifies the disagreement over conditionality. Lin described the $17 million as a considerable concession made on an understanding that the earlier liabilities could easily be settled. He said the December response failed to conform to the American November commitment, and linked that claimed failure to withdrawing the proposal. Habib maintained that the December statement had been the same as the November one and promised to examine it again. The two did not resolve the issue in this first session. Printed 9–10 / PDF 48–49.

This is more specific than explaining the loss of the offer through an unexplained bureaucratic refusal. The officials disagreed about whether the bargain's political core was already settled and whether the American legal treatment fulfilled the understanding on which China had offered the money. It is also more limited than proving that Washington broke a promise. The actual December 22 memorandum, the complete successive draft letters and the June 14 reply have not been acquired. Lin's explanation was a negotiating position, while Habib's competing interpretation was one too. The record does not establish that either reproduced the full internal decision process.

The document is a ten-page State Department memorandum, drafted by Christine Vick, preserved in the Ford Library's *Kissinger Reports on USSR, China, and Middle East Discussions*, folder “November 25–29, 1974—Kissinger's Trip (1).” Its complete first-session record, physical pages 40–49, was read. The rest of the 50-page folder scan was not systematically read. The meeting header and printed pages 7–9 were visually inspected, particularly because OCR separates some speaker labels from the text. [Official scan](https://www.fordlibrarymuseum.gov/sites/default/files/pdf_documents/library/document/0331/1553934.pdf).

## A changed proposal, followed by a continued refusal

On **October 22, 1975**, Habib acknowledged that China had withdrawn its previous position but sought to restore Zhou's proposal within a larger settlement. He offered to withdraw the American statement of March 21, 1973 and aide-mémoire of October 27, 1973 concerning the bond issue. For the identification problem, he presented two alternatives: put matching phrases in the main agreement or put them in an exchange of letters. He preferred a single document and described both options as symmetrical. The record describes the paper handed over; its actual underlined language is not reproduced in these read passages. It cannot support a substantive legal comparison of the alternatives. **CLA-F02**, printed 4–5 / PDF 7–8.

Lin's response explicitly said the June 14, 1974 letter had withdrawn China's offer to return an amount equal to the funds already recovered from third-country banks. He maintained the objection to the bond liabilities and rejected the proposed asset definitions as imposing American law on Chinese affairs. He was prepared to leave the question unsettled. Habib concluded that there was no point in further claims discussion at that meeting. At the close, he still asked the Chinese to study the ideas and said the Americans would do so too. The refusal was therefore specific to the presented package and the day's discussion, not evidence that all later contact was closed. Printed 10–11 and 16–17 / PDF 13–14 and 19–20.

The relevant exchanges were read completely: header and printed 1–5, the transition to Lin's response at printed 10–11, and the complete closing printed 16–17. The intervening cultural-exchange discussion and the other documents in the 38-page folder were not fully read. Header, printed 4–5 and 11 were visually inspected. The record lists Solomon among the American participants; that fact matters for source independence. His later description is not an independent witness added to a record of a meeting he attended. [Official scan](https://www.fordlibrarymuseum.gov/sites/default/files/pdf_documents/library/document/0331/1553943.pdf).

## What was and was not recovered

**June 14 message.** The complete [FRUS XIII/27](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v13/d27) entry and all notes were acquired and read, following the lead already identified in the chapter-development memo. Its note 4 points to **telegram 985 from Beijing, June 14, 1974, RG 59, Central Foreign Policy File, no film number**. The note calls the item an “agreement”; that editorial noun must not be used as proof of a new bilateral agreement. The November 1974 and October 1975 records describe a Chinese reply/letter and dispute its meaning. In the April 29, 1977 meeting recorded by FRUS, Han Hsu claimed continuity in China's position and repeated the three objections. That claim does not make the missing June letter available or cancel Lin's explicit account of withdrawing the monetary proposal. **CLA-F03**.

NARA's [CFPF guide](https://www.archives.gov/research/foreign-policy/state-dept/rg-59-central-files/1973-1979) distinguishes electronic telegrams, microfilmed paper documents and subsequently released telegram images. Its introduction and access explanation were read; the acquired release table was searched for the target numbers and Beijing/Peking entries. It did not contain 1974PEKING00985 or 1973PEKING01695. This is a negative result for that particular supplemental list, not for the entire archive. AAD's public landing page could not be opened through the web tool; a direct database-page retrieval returned HTTP 403. No AAD search result or withdrawal card was obtained. Exact-number, date, title and archive-domain web searches produced citations and the later Ford records, not the target telegram's text. The missing message's classification or survival cannot be inferred from these access failures.

**December 31 attachments.** The existing complete FRUS XVIII/65 capture was consulted for source notes and attachment references. The identifiable attachment is **telegram 1695 from Beijing, December 29, 1973**, Tab B. The reporting at Tab A remains unnamed in the published redacted text. Its archive location is Nixon NSC Files, **Box 527, PRC Vol. VIII, July 10–December 31, 1973**. The current NSC finding-aid page did not supply an identified digital copy of those target folders. The September 2026 release lists were also screened for the target box, names and dates without recovering the documents. No claim that they remain classified follows from this search.

The June 14 letter, telegram 985, telegram 1695, unidentified Tab A report, December 22 American memorandum, and underlying Chinese approval/withdrawal instructions remain the precise missing items. A serious targeted public search has produced usable process evidence; expanding it into unrelated background would not close those gaps. This is an appropriate stopping point without outreach, purchases or an archive visit.

## Implication for the Solomon assessment

The new records strengthen the case for examining the condition attached to an offer, rather than treating senior sponsorship as a substitute for agreement on remaining liabilities. They do not decide **S-P24's** predictive reliability. For **S-P25**, Lin pressed an alleged American earlier commitment while defending withdrawal of a Chinese proposal as a response to nonfulfillment. That is evidence of contested reciprocity, not an established asymmetric rule in which only the other side was bound. For **S-P05**, asset definitions carried disputed legal and financial consequences; “technical detail” was itself one participant's characterization. For **S-C19**, the stated substantive reason supplies an alternative that must be assessed alongside Solomon's political diagnosis, without proving internal politics irrelevant.

The main chapter can now state the withdrawal through a contemporary attributed exchange rather than relying only on the 1977 American review. It should not say the missing Chinese decision has been recovered. The practical consequence is concrete: when trying to revive a senior offer, establish which accompanying assurance the counterpart says made it available, and test the proposed legal language against that asserted condition.

## Proposed replacement passage for Chapter 1

The proposal below is retained as research history. It targeted the paragraph beginning “China withdrew Zhou's monetary offer in 1974,” before the May 1977 deferral paragraph. The authorized September 16 integration used these local note labels with small narrative adjustments described below.

When Philip Habib took up the issue with Lin Ping in November 1974, the two officials disagreed about what remained to be negotiated. Habib treated Zhou's $17 million proposal as accepted and the old-bond question as resolved by Washington's decision not to involve itself. The remaining task, he said, was to identify the assets in a way that met American legal requirements and protected the government against litigation. He asked Lin to confirm that only the means of carrying out an agreement in principle remained.[^claims-followup-1]

Lin refused that description. China's June 14 reply, he said, had answered an American memorandum of December 22, 1973. The American wording had changed, but the demands had not. China had offered the money on an understanding that the old-liability question could be settled; the December response had failed to honor the November assurance. That was his explanation for withdrawing the offer. Habib disputed it: Washington had not asked China to acknowledge the old debts, and he believed its November and December positions were the same. He promised to examine the matter again. One official saw a bargain awaiting technical completion; the other saw an unmet condition for making it.[^claims-followup-1]

Habib brought another package in October 1975. He offered to withdraw two earlier American statements on the bond question and presented alternative ways to place matching asset-identification language in the agreement or accompanying letters. Lin still rejected the proposed treatment as imposing American law on Chinese affairs. He explicitly reaffirmed withdrawal of the repayment offer, and Habib ended that day's claims discussion. These exchanges recover a contested explanation of the failure, without deciding whether it exhausted the reasons for Beijing's position.[^claims-followup-2]
