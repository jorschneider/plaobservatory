## Finding

The contemporaneous record now extends beyond April’s inspection negotiations. It shows a public Chinese refusal on May 8, continuing American efforts to obtain the aircraft, and a June announcement of technical arrangements for disassembly. It does **not** recover the particular flyout authorization remembered by Prueher, a Chinese order rescinding such authorization, or an American instruction abandoning an already accepted flyout. Those propositions remain unresolved.

The improvement is more specific than another warning about memoirs. A repair assessment, senior assurances of return, permission to depart under the aircraft’s own power, acceptance of a cargo-aircraft alternative and execution of that alternative were different decisions. The evidence now supplies additional observations of the first, second and later decisions without filling the third by inference. It also shows why an agreement to remove the aircraft could coexist with unfinished engineering and procurement work.

The strongest new narrative material is the pairing of May 8’s positions with the June implementation problem. Tang’s later account adds a participant’s explanation of the symbolic objection; it should enter as attributed testimony, not as the missing contemporary order.

## Senior contact did not specify the means of return

The [April 30 Qian cable](https://foia.state.gov/DOCUMENTS/MaySept2015/F-2010-07070/DOC_0C17665631/C17665631.pdf), **EPM-05**, reports an April 28 farewell dinner with Prueher. Qian twice expressed an expectation of a speedy aircraft return. He had expected agreement on an assessment team during the talks beginning April 18 and hoped that issue could be resolved before Prueher’s departure. This is contemporary American reporting of an important Chinese official’s position. It is not consent to fly the aircraft out. The paragraph discusses assessment access, and supplies no approved return method or associated conditions: paragraph 4, physical p.2.

The cable itself contains a small date inconsistency. Paragraphs 1 and 3 identify April 28 as the meeting date; a parenthetical note in paragraph 4 calls April 30 “the next day.” The source should not be silently repaired to produce a more exact sequence. Its substantive distinction between expected return and assessment access is unaffected.

The [April 27 Dai cable](https://foia.state.gov/DOCUMENTS/MaySept2015/F-2010-07070/DOC_0C17665629/C17665629.pdf), **EPM-04**, is especially useful against a shortcut in the authority argument. At their April 26 meeting, Dai Bingguo said he was speaking personally and had not been a participant in the incident: paragraph 4, physical p.3. The embassy nevertheless believed his access to Zeng Qinghong and Jiang Zemin made the conversation valuable, and hoped its message would reach Jiang: paragraph 16, physical p.9. The evidence establishes a contact, the embassy’s reason for using it and its expectation of onward transmission. It does not establish that transmission occurred, that Dai could authorize a return method, or that the conversation changed an instruction.

These records are useful checks on the aircraft story, but neither needs a new narrative digression in Chapter 4. The existing argument about identifying authority is stronger if these distinctions remain in the note or supporting dossier.

## The May positions and the competing recollections

On [May 8](https://www.fmprc.gov.cn/web/gjhdq_676201/gj_676203/bmz_679954/1206_680528/fyrygth_680536/200105/t20010508_9361193.shtml), **EPM-01**, the Chinese Foreign Ministry answered a question about Rumsfeld’s reported hope to fly the aircraft home. It said China had repeatedly made clear in negotiations that the EP-3 could not fly from Hainan to the United States. This is a contemporary official public refusal, accompanied by a claim that the position had already been communicated privately. It does not prove what was said at every subsequent meeting.

The [White House briefing that day](https://georgewbush-whitehouse.archives.gov/news/briefings/20010508.html), **EPM-02**, put the American position differently. Fleischer described discussions as continuing, invoked Rumsfeld’s efficiency argument and made the prospect conditional on the aircraft’s capacity to fly. There was no announcement of Chinese consent. The two statements therefore support a disagreement continuing into May, rather than an agreed flyout awaiting routine implementation.

[Powell’s May 14 interview](https://usinfo.org/usia/usinfo.state.gov/topical/pol/arms/stories/01051466.htm), **EPM-06**, adds a further contemporary checkpoint. Andrea Koppel asked about China’s refusal to allow a flyout and the possibility of shipping the aircraft after disassembly. Powell answered that negotiations were under way and predicted a satisfactory resolution. He praised the embassy’s work but did not announce a settled method. The corresponding [CNN transcript](https://transcripts.cnn.com/show/se/date/2001-05-14/segment/01), lines 166–174, preserves the exchange and the immediately following questions. His public answer cannot demonstrate either the existence or nonexistence of an unannounced private offer.

Tang’s [2009 excerpt](https://news.hndaily.cn/html/2009-12/21/content_28_1.htm), **EPM-03**, describes rejecting a May 10 request to repair and fly out the aircraft. He connects the refusal to renewed American reconnaissance on May 7 and explains the departure method as politically symbolic. This is a third participant account, alongside Zhou and Prueher, not contemporary corroboration of the internal rationale. The April cables and May 8 statement show that refusal preceded the episode Tang emphasizes. They prevent treating the May 7 flights as the demonstrated origin of the no-flyout position.

The current chapter should consequently retain the unresolved status of Prueher’s claimed later opportunity. The stronger formulation is that the recovered **contemporary checkpoints show refusal and continuing negotiation**, while the alleged intervening authorization is still missing. April’s prohibition alone cannot rule out a later change. Equally, a recollected opportunity cannot erase the explicit refusals now visible on several dates.

## Agreement was followed by engineering and procurement

The existing complete MFA [May 29 announcement](https://www.fmprc.gov.cn/web/ziliao_674904/zt_674979/ywzt_675099/2355_676073/2379_676137/200105/t20010529_7965465.shtml), **EP310**, remains the firm basis for the agreement in principle. It identifies removal of the disassembled EP-3 by commercial An-124 and continuing technical discussions. The newly located May 29 State indexed exchange is consistent with that account. It is not a substitute for an agreed instrument.

The [June 7 China Daily report](https://news.sina.com.cn/w/272038.html), **EPM-07**, attributes an announcement of agreed technical arrangements to Sun Yuxi. It also reports that he declined to disclose the schedule and carrier details. The headline’s description of the incident as basically resolved should not be substituted for the body’s prospective account. This is reporting of an official announcement, not a recovered signed protocol.

The [same-day Pentagon briefing](https://usinfo.org/wf-archive/2001/010607/epf403.htm), **EP311 / EPM-08**, makes the remaining work unusually tangible. An engineer had yet to arrive to assess Lingshui’s runway. His findings would determine allowable loads and hence the number of An-124 flights. Without a flight count and schedule, the contractor could not yet seek bids from cargo-aircraft companies. Local support discussions also continued. Quigley reported an acceptable communications arrangement, while estimates for component departure and the wider operation remained conditional.

The inference should be narrow: political agreement enabled a recovery operation whose practical requirements were not yet fully known. The briefing does not establish that all remaining delay was technically unavoidable, or identify which private bargaining produced the communications terms. Conversely, assigning every later day to Chinese political obstruction would discard a documented source of implementation work.

## Proposed bounded narrative insert

This passage can replace or expand the existing paragraph beginning “Prueher later remembered,” followed by the June 7 paragraph. It preserves the unresolved recollection and gives the subsequent engineering work a causal place. Retain the chapter’s existing July 3 completion/payment sentence afterward.

> The disagreement continued into May. On May 8, China’s Foreign Ministry said it had repeatedly told the Americans that the EP-3 could not fly home from Hainan. The White House still described a flyout as the efficient solution, if the aircraft proved capable of it. Six days later, asked about China’s refusal, Powell said negotiations were continuing. Return was an objective; permission to use the simplest means of return remained contested.[^may-positions]
>
> Tang Jiaxuan later explained the refusal in political terms: an intact departure could make the incident appear to have had no consequences. He recalled rejecting a renewed American request on May 10. Prueher remembered a flyout opportunity that was lost; Zhou remembered refusing it. The contemporary records show repeated objections, but no recovered authorization settles the disagreement over whether an opportunity briefly opened.[^may-memory]
>
> The alternative required a different kind of work. On May 29, China announced agreement in principle to take the aircraft apart and remove it aboard a commercial An-124. Technical discussions continued. On June 7, Sun Yuxi announced agreement on technical arrangements, while withholding the timetable and carrier details.[^9][^may-technical]
>
> An agreement to use a huge cargo aircraft did not establish how heavily it could be loaded on a runway built for fighters. That day, the Pentagon said an engineer was still due to assess Lingshui’s runway. His findings would determine the loads and number of flights. Those figures were needed before the contractor could seek bids. The political accommodation had settled a method of return; engineers and procurement staff still had to turn it into a removal operation. Washington could recover the aircraft while Beijing retained its objection to letting the EP-3 itself fly away.[^10]

## Search boundary and remaining gap

All 46 catalogue titles and dates in State case **F-2010-07070** were inspected. They are April records. The two newly read cables above were selected to test senior assurances and authority, not described as May documentation. A broader public catalogue query initially failed because of missing parameters; a second used an incorrect date format. The corrected date-filtered request returned 153 records but did not reliably constrain the keyword. Restricting origin to Beijing returned one June 25 Kelly cable, whose catalogue entry was read but whose text was not acquired. These attempts do not establish that the State archive has no relevant May records.

Targeted searches also covered the official MFA archive, State briefings, USINFO and CNN reproductions, and contemporary Chinese news indexes. The relevant People’s Daily index now supplies placeholder links. The retrieved CCTV index points to later operational stories rather than an agreement text. No outreach, restricted access or authentication workaround was used.

The missing objects remain exact: a dated Chinese authorization for an EP-3 flyout, any American instruction declining such an authorized option, and the agreed May–June technical text or exchanged final drafts. The present evidence supports a better account of how the acceptable return method changed and why removal required further work. It does not warrant choosing a winner among the retrospective accounts by assertion.
