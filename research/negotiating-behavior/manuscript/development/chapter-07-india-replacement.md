## India: deciding what progress was worth

After three hours with Wang Yi in New Delhi on March 25, 2022, Subrahmanyam Jaishankar faced a question that exposed the difficulty of his position. The Indian foreign minister had discussed trade, travel, students and international affairs with his Chinese counterpart. Did an agenda that broad itself amount to a return to normal relations?

Jaishankar answered no. He welcomed progress at several confrontation points along the disputed border, notably Pangong Tso. But large deployments remained, and their presence, he argued, violated earlier agreements. A ministerial visit could help resolve that problem. It could not make the problem cease to matter. India wanted stable, predictable relations; restoring them required peace at the border.[^7]

Wang had offered a different way forward. According to China’s account, the two large developing countries should preserve the wider significance of their relationship and prevent the boundary question from defining it. Yet that same account recorded Jaishankar’s call to complete disengagement in the remaining areas. It also described agreement to restore border peace and move toward regular management on the basis of disengagement. The ministers could agree that military separation mattered while disagreeing about how much of the relationship should wait for it.[^india-march-cn]

This was a negotiation over the value of the relationship, with India pressing the condition. Beijing’s appeal to common interests encountered a counterpart unwilling to grant the political meaning Beijing sought for partial progress. The question was practical: what could each government do next without conceding that the other’s remaining demands had become unnecessary?

### Keeping contact without conceding normality

Jaishankar did not answer that question by refusing other business. He raised the predicament of Indian students unable to return to China under pandemic restrictions and pressed for fairer market access. Wang, he reported, undertook to speak to the relevant authorities about the students. That was a promise to pursue the matter, rather than an immediate decision restoring their access. The relationship contained requests worth making even while India disputed its overall condition.

Nor did Jaishankar publicly offer a border concession in return for a summit. Asked whether a timetable had been set so that Narendra Modi could attend the BRICS summit in China, he acknowledged discussion of the invitation but described the border talks as a parallel, separate subject. Wang’s presence had provided an opportunity to press for implementation. Jaishankar stopped short of presenting either that presence or a future leaders’ meeting as the consideration for a bargain.[^7]

He had reason to distinguish a new understanding from carrying out an old one. The ministers had met in Moscow in September 2020 and again in Dushanbe the following year. Fifteen rounds of senior military talks had followed the confrontation that began in 2020. Some friction points had been addressed, but implementation was slower than India wanted. Jaishankar’s immediate objective was to complete disengagement so that discussion of de-escalation could follow. Moving forces apart at a particular confrontation point and reducing the larger military concentration were different decisions.

The dispute was not over whether principles should precede negotiation. Earlier principles already supplied arguments for both sides. The 2005 agreement stated that boundary differences should not obstruct the broader relationship. It also required respect for the Line of Actual Control and maintenance of peace pending a final settlement. Wang could invoke the value of cooperation; Jaishankar could insist that the agreed security foundation had been disturbed. The text contained both propositions. Appealing to one did not dispose of the other.[^2]

Turning those propositions into a local arrangement required officials with different responsibilities. The diplomatic Working Mechanism for Consultation and Coordination, established in 2012, included military representatives and could address border incidents. Its founding agreement excluded settlement of the boundary question, which remained the concern of the Special Representatives. Commanders, meanwhile, had to negotiate arrangements that forces could carry out on the ground. A conversation about managing an immediate danger did not authorize a territorial settlement.[^3]

Saheb Singh Chadha’s interviews with Indian officials make the limits of delegated authority more concrete. His reconstruction describes political meetings setting a framework, diplomatic meetings elaborating steps and commanders working on their application. Before corps-commanders’ meetings, India’s China Study Group set the limits within which its commander could negotiate. The military interlocutor was therefore not arriving with unlimited discretion to exchange concessions. Chadha did not interview Chinese officials, so the corresponding Chinese instructions remain unknown. But the Indian evidence alone makes it unsafe to read every stalled military round as evidence of a dispute inside Beijing.[^6]

### What the local bargain required

The sixteenth military round, held on July 17, 2022, did not publicly announce closure. Its joint release promised continued work on remaining issues and interim stability. It also linked resolving those issues to progress in bilateral relations. That formulation gave both governments room to continue: India had not accepted that the border no longer mattered, and neither side had committed publicly to a complete settlement at that meeting.[^8]

The next arrangement concerned one confrontation point in eastern Ladakh, in the western sector of the disputed frontier. On September 8, troops began disengaging in the area India called Gogra–Hot Springs, or Patrol Point 15. China’s publication used the name Jianan Daban. The joint announcement was brief. India’s explanation the following day showed that the undertaking it described was considerably more specific.

The process had begun at 08:30 and was to finish by September 12. Both sides would cease forward deployments in a phased, coordinated and verified manner, with troops returning to their respective areas. Temporary structures and associated infrastructure erected by both sides would be dismantled and checked through mutual verification. The terrain would be restored to its condition before the standoff. India also said the agreement required respect for the line and prohibited unilateral changes to the status quo.[^9]

These terms made the next decisions concrete. A commander needed to coordinate a withdrawal with the other force, remove structures and verify the reciprocal action. A diplomat needed to preserve the distinction between completing that task and accepting that all remaining issues were resolved. The September explanation expressly kept further talks on the agenda. India’s October diplomatic account then reported that the September 8–12 process had been completed and connected it to the foreign ministers’ understandings, including their July meeting in Bali.

The published explanation is the Indian government’s account of the bargain; its maps and verification records are not available here. It cannot establish every change in patrol access or independently verify every movement. What it does establish is the specificity of the duties India publicly described. Inferring a vague operative agreement from the brevity of the joint announcement would miss the timetable, reciprocal tasks and checks that officials said lay behind it.

Completion at one location brought a further decision into view: how much wider cooperation should it unlock? On September 27, Chinese ambassador Sun Weidong described a relationship recovering momentum. He pointed to disengagement, resumed processing of some visa categories and business travel. He advocated restored direct flights and broader exchanges. In his account, the emergency response following Galwan had largely ended and the border was moving toward routine management. Outstanding differences should be handled without allowing them to govern the whole relationship.[^10]

India resisted that conclusion. Asked about the ambassador’s remarks on October 7, spokesperson Arindam Bagchi acknowledged positive steps but maintained that more were needed. PP15 was another step. India still sought disengagement, de-escalation and restored border normality before normality in the wider relationship. Sun’s account of progress and Bagchi’s refusal to call it sufficient were not necessarily competing claims about whether the PP15 process had occurred. They assigned different political consequences to it.

This distinction matters to a negotiator deciding whether to accept a partial agreement. Local separation can reduce danger even when the parties cannot agree what it should purchase elsewhere. India could accept the reciprocal arrangement while withholding the broader response China advocated. China could participate in the arrangement and continue arguing that relations should improve. Continued bargaining over that response does not, by itself, show that either side had repudiated the local terms.

### A different threshold for moving forward

Two years later, the negotiators reached an additional arrangement. After further diplomatic and military work, India announced an agreement on October 21, 2024, concerning Depsang and Demchok. When Modi and Xi Jinping met in Kazan two days later, they welcomed the progress and directed further work. Their meeting endorsed an arrangement already reached. Its anticipated occurrence may have encouraged agreement; the sequence cannot assign the terms to the encounter that publicly celebrated them.[^1][^15]

In December, Jaishankar explained to Parliament what India understood it had obtained. The problem in the two areas had concerned obstruction of patrolling; Demchok also involved civilian grazing and access to places of local significance. He reported verification patrols followed by regular activity under the understanding. Patrolling and grazing access had been agreed, while temporary restraints at other locations remained dependent on local conditions and open to review. He called disengagement complete and identified de-escalation as the next task.[^15]

His account also changed the practical question facing the relationship. India could now consider other aspects of engagement in a calibrated fashion while continuing to address military concentration. The choice was no longer whether PP15 alone justified a return to normality. It was how much cooperation to resume after the additional arrangements, with another category of security work unfinished.

By August 2025, the two governments publicly combined those courses. Their accounts of the Special Representatives’ talks called for discussion of de-escalation, beginning with its principles and modalities. They also commissioned work on border management and possible early results in delimitation, and extended military mechanisms to additional sectors. India’s account listed intended steps on flights, visas, border trade and other exchanges. Those commitments did not demonstrate that all measures had been delivered. They did show that renewed cooperation need not await completion of every military adjustment.[^16]

In May 2026, diplomatic teams were still discussing delimitation, border management and cooperation while preparing another Special Representatives’ meeting. India described progress toward gradual normalization. Neither account announced a completed boundary settlement or supplied a census of forces withdrawn.[^17]

The Indian sequence consequently offers more than a counterexample to a national style. It shows the counterpart helping determine what a familiar practice can accomplish. Appeals to the larger relationship did not automatically override India’s security demands; neither did those demands prevent every other exchange. Senior access mattered because officials used it to press for work through diplomatic and military channels. Local agreements mattered because they specified actions while leaving other questions open. The negotiator’s task was to decide which unfinished question still justified withholding the next benefit—and to recognize that the other government might value the same step differently.
