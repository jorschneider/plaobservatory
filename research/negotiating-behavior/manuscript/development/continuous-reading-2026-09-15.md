The manuscript now contains several persuasive historical narratives. Woodcock carrying new instructions into Deng's meeting, Barshefsky bringing a potential Washington obstacle into her delegation, Prueher obtaining permission to narrow his objective, Xie explaining reciprocal acceptability, and Gackle disputing the wisdom of further pressure all give the reader decisions with consequences. The remaining weakness is at the level of the book: the chapters frequently arrive at the same general conclusion rather than building a progressively sharper explanation. It is substantially better than an archive of research memos. It is not yet a consistently compelling account of what changed in Chinese negotiating behavior and how a present-day reader should act differently.

## 1. Distinguish the historical change from the method of reading any negotiation

**Anchors:** introduction, paragraph beginning “This book follows that problem”; Chapter 3, “What a specialist can change”; Chapter 4, “What changes between the two crises”; conclusion, “What the update requires,” especially “What the later cases add is a wider field…”.

The positive thesis often reduces to identifying the relevant authority, valuing a usable concession and separating agreement from performance. The narratives make these useful. They do not make them distinctively new. Chapter 1 establishes precisely that these problems already existed in 1978. The manuscript correctly refuses to invent a change of national temperament, but has not fully replaced that rejected explanation with a historical argument of comparable force.

**Revision:** make three demonstrated developments carry the chronology: membership lets China contest a foreign government's decisions through an external procedure; later agreements specify more particular obligations affecting domestic administration and scrutiny; the 2025 case makes access to important Chinese-controlled supplies part of reciprocal bargaining. Explain their different consequences, rather than calling them collectively a multiplication of instruments. In particular, connect the accession chapters to the formation and use of legal capability. Chapter 3 presently moves from entry to expert legal arguments with little account of how the people and organizations acquired the ability to make them. The already-read Shaffer/Gao and Gao scholarship is a place to recover that bridge, with its interview population and period limits preserved.

A short orienting passage should also say what the cases **do not** establish as a change: the prevalence or effectiveness of old-friend cultivation, the comparative reliability of senior intervention, or a general increase in rigidity. The current qualifications are scattered and repeated; one clear division between demonstrated developments and unmeasured behavioral changes would strengthen the answer.

**Evidence requirement:** the narrower institutional developments can be organized from existing evidence. Explaining their acquisition requires returning to the actual passages in the existing literature before drafting. A claim that one negotiating practice became more frequent, more successful or more centralized requires additional comparative evidence. The gap between the normalization-era narrative and accession should not be silently treated as a documented continuous evolution.

## 2. A historical mechanism is not automatically an available option in 2026

**Anchors:** Chapter 3, paragraphs beginning “For a negotiator, the preparation begins before victory” and “China was using membership”; conclusion, “Rules allocate discretion as well as constrain it,” especially its first three paragraphs and note 10.

The WTO case supplies one of the book's clearest contrasts with bilateral bargaining. Yet the present-day reader is told only in a note that unchanged appellate machinery should not be assumed. That is a material limit on the practical lesson, not merely bibliographic caution. The body does not explain which route a China–US or China–EU negotiator could actually contemplate now.

**Revision:** add a compact, dated paragraph after the historical DS379 sequence distinguishing the mechanism examined in 2011–2012 from current possibilities. Specify availability by counterpart and legal route. Do not replace this with “the WTO is broken”; a tribunal, appeal, agreed arbitration and bargaining over compliance are different opportunities. Keep the historical finding intact even where its current application is conditional.

The same editorial test should be applied to the Paris and 2023 military-channel material before making a present-tense recommendation: does the lesson concern a historically demonstrated design, or an arrangement the reader can presently use? The book need not narrate every later event, but it must not make the reader infer continuing availability from an old agreement.

**Evidence requirement:** a bounded official-record update is required for current WTO arrangements. This memorandum does not establish their September 2026 status. Current legal participation or channel availability should likewise be checked if the text makes claims about them; no such new check was performed here.

## 3. The defended boundary must not become a new stereotype

**Anchors:** introduction, “For anyone negotiating with China…” and the closing paragraph; Chapter 2, “What Europe still wanted” and “The limits inside the opening”; Chapter 6, “Getting into business” and “Permissions in exchange for permissions”; conclusion, opening three paragraphs and “The price of a limited bargain.”

The dominant model is attractive: Beijing retains a political limit while bargaining over useful terms beneath it. But the manuscript also contains counterexamples to treating the currently defended limit as a lasting constraint. The 49 percent mobile ceiling stayed in the accession bargain; the later securities ownership ceiling was removed. The 2025 export-control authority survived relief; Chapter 6 also records China's report that earlier condemned rare-earth measures were removed in 2015. Party leadership, a particular foreign-equity ceiling and an export-control instrument are not equivalent kinds of boundary.

**Revision:** put these contrasts next to one another in the conclusion. Ask whether the negotiator is facing a protected purpose, a current instrument for achieving it, or a provisional price. A government can retain control through regulation while surrendering a particular ownership rule. The story should not teach visitors to accept every asserted limit and seek compensation underneath it. Lamy's successful accommodation was one option, not a rule for how China must be approached.

Give refusal equal analytical standing. The current prose explains why an incomplete bargain can be valuable more often than it explains when its price is unacceptable. Use the existing distinction between a firm that needs control and one that can use earlier minority access; then apply the same logic to temporary licensing relief purchased with a more durable concession. A conditional authorial judgment is possible without inventing an empirical finding: if the substitute does not provide the benefit for which the government is paying, an announcement of agreement is no reason to take it.

**Evidence requirement:** these contrasts and conditional judgments need no new source acquisition. Judging whether the actual 2025 package was worth its price would require its fuller terms, duration and credible alternatives. The public record presently supports the choice and exposure, not a calculated verdict on its welfare or strategic value.

## 4. The introduction, chapter endings and conclusion repeatedly spend the same evidence

**Anchors:** introduction, “The inheritance” and “What changed at the table”; Chapter 1, “The relationship Solomon studied” and “Freeman's next question”; Chapter 2, “The price of another day”; conclusion, “The price of a limited bargain,” “Authority that can be used,” and “Performance and the cost of complaint.”

The introduction and conclusion together occupy about 5,650 words. Both give substantial retellings of cases, and the chapter endings then restate many of the same conclusions about authority, scope and usable performance. The repetition is consequential because readers meet an episode's result before the episode, follow it in detail, then receive another synopsis with nearly the same lesson. Kissinger's access, the departure letters, the confidentiality problem and the contrasting industry witnesses are especially exposed to this pattern.

**Cuts and moves:**

- Keep the introduction's documented 1999 opening, but reduce the later case tour. Give the three historical developments in issue 1 a short paragraph each. Move the two-paragraph Kissinger assessment wholly to Chapter 4, retaining at most a sentence identifying the question it raises. Leave the full description of Freeman's adaptations to Chapter 1.
- In the conclusion, cut the renewed miniature narratives beginning “Pascal Lamy's accession negotiation makes the choice visible,” “Barshefsky's account of taking Gene Sperling…,” and “The American departure letters…”. Replace them with direct comparisons that require evidence from more than one chapter. Their facts remain available in Chapters 2 and 4; a conclusion should make a relationship among them visible, not replay their chronology.
- Preserve the Gackle–Cooper contrast in Chapter 6, where it poses and resolves a real problem. In the conclusion, use one sentence to recall the divergence and spend the saved space on the implication for a remedy's credibility.
- Choose one main-text location for the general warning that Solomon and Freeman already recognized institutional variation and partial agreements. Retain exact scope wherever necessary, but remove redundant reminders that the later case did not invent expertise or continuing negotiations.

**Evidence requirement:** none. These cuts should be made only after deciding which synthesis replaces the recaps; compression alone will not produce a stronger argument.

## 5. Turn requests for omniscience into choices under incomplete information

**Anchors:** Chapter 1, “The practical response is to ask what the intervention can accomplish”; Chapter 2, “Deng's account does not make every Chinese pause innocent”; Chapter 4, paragraph beginning “Tyler Jost's institutional study offers an explanation”; Chapter 5, “An official in such a negotiation needs a record of clearance”; conclusion, final two paragraphs.

The manuscript often tells the reader to identify the real authority, determine what China wants or distinguish inability from unwillingness. Those are the problems the person bought the book to solve. A visitor normally cannot inspect the Chinese instruction record, and the narrative repeatedly acknowledges that the historian cannot either. The general advice can therefore sound easier than the cases warrant.

**Revision:** organize practical conclusions around what an official can ask, test or protect against without solving the hidden motive. The examples already supply concrete alternatives: request confirmation of the disputed limit while proposing specified compensation; make an extension conditional on an identified decision; record which draft provisions remain contingent on the package; separate the immediate task from the contested account of responsibility; require information that would make a promised processing average assessable. Explain the cost and failure condition of each move. Do not call any of them a reliable diagnostic test without evidence.

The Chapter 4 information-versus-deliberate-refusal distinction is the clearest place to demonstrate this discipline. If neither motive can be established, the choice is not to announce the right explanation; it is to try another authorized route while preserving the government's position and avoiding an unnecessary concession. Prueher's uncertainty about which channel helped should remain part of the lesson.

Specify the intended actor where it changes the available action. The government can threaten reciprocal restrictions; an exposed company may only be able to supply confidential evidence, delay investment or seek its government's help. Moving between those actors without identifying the difference makes “the negotiator” too elastic.

**Evidence requirement:** the moves above are grounded in existing cases; presenting them as options is an editorial task. Claims about how reliably they reveal a Chinese mandate, or about which channel caused a concession, require evidence not presently available.

## 6. Chapter 6 contains two major arguments, and the older frame is strained by the new one

**Anchors:** Chapter 6 opening, “Buying a number,” “Permissions in exchange for permissions,” “The cost of enforcing an agreement,” and “Negotiating the next mechanism.”

The chapter begins with Gackle and Cooper, then asks the reader to retain their problem through finance, biotechnology, purchases and a substantial independent history of export controls before returning to the hearing. The export-control section is strong and gives the contemporary book much of its power, but it is not another implementation outcome of Phase One. The current transition, “The bargaining over performance took a different form,” understates the change of dispute and instrument. The final Board discussion then adds another mechanism after the hearing has resolved the original dramatic question.

**Revision options:** if preserving the chapter structure, finish the Phase One arc through the 2025 hearing before introducing export-control bargaining. Add a clear bridge explaining that the new dispute demonstrates leverage over future transactions rather than compliance with the old purchase promise. End that second movement on the cost of accepting relief with licensing authority intact. Reduce the Board material to a short coda, or move it to the conclusion where it can test the book's questions against an unfinished design. If subsequent research substantially expands the 2025 sequence, it merits its own chapter; it should not keep growing inside the Phase One frame by accretion.

A smaller rhythm problem occurs in Chapter 3: the memorable Aifudi opening invites a business-level answer, but the recovered evidence supplies decisions and rates, not the company's commercial experience. The qualification is honest. Keep the question framed around what the ruling changed, rather than implying a recovered story of how this exporter used the result. Its absence should not be filled with an imagined manager or invented sales consequences.

**Evidence requirement:** reordering and the stronger bridge need no new sources. Aifudi's actual commercial effects would require company, importer or entry-level evidence; the missing calculation alone would not supply that story.

## 7. The strength of the explanations varies with who can be heard

**Anchors:** Chapter 3, “In Washington, the favorable interpretation therefore became another investigation”; Chapter 4, “Before Sullivan and Wang could use a strategic channel”; Chapter 6, “Permission to sell a seed”; conclusion, “What the later cases add…”.

The manuscript is careful about attribution, but its evidence still offers uneven access to decisions. Xie's Chinese interview makes the climate argument unusually strong: it supplies an articulated Chinese problem and a process of choosing among estimates. The WTO implementation account largely obtains Chinese administrative arguments through the American agency's decision. The 2023 strategic-channel preparation is reconstructed through former American officials. The biotechnology sequence gives an excellent account of lobbying and announcements in Washington but much less of the Chinese review process. These are not equally complete explanations of Chinese decision-making.

**Revision:** keep that asymmetry visible at the point of the argument, without appending another warning to every paragraph. One opening attribution—“Commerce's account records China's objection…”—can establish the evidentiary position for the trade sequence. The channel section already handles its American perspective well; do not let the conclusion convert that into knowledge of a parallel Chinese mechanism. In biotechnology, distinguish the demonstrated dependence on permission from an explanation of why particular applications took so long. The existing EU comparison is an important check and should survive cuts.

For the next research pass, prioritize evidence that would discriminate explanations rather than additional complaints or success announcements: a Chinese request for additional application information, a dated technical review or refusal, a firm's response, and the subsequent disposition. On litigation, the underlying Chinese administrative submissions would test the agency's account. Such material could establish an actual contested decision comparable in depth to the climate interview. More public assurances alone will not do that work.

**Evidence requirement:** clearer attribution needs no acquisition. An explanation of individual Chinese licensing delays or of the internal strategic-channel decision requires further evidence. Until then, the book should claim a well-documented account of access and bargaining around administration, not a uniformly recovered account of the administration's motives.
