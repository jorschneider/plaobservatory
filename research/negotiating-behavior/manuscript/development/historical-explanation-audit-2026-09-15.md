# Does the manuscript explain historical change?

Independent substantive audit, 15 September 2026. Baseline: commit `a219d6c6cc4088d7a6e62a2b82c4757c87b0e2f1`. No chapter edits. This is an assessment of the historical explanation, not a new verification of every source or a publication-readiness judgment.

## Judgment

The manuscript now offers more than generic advice. Its strongest demonstrated change is the acquisition of a usable legal capability after WTO accession: membership supplied rights, while recruitment, counsel relationships and interagency work helped turn them into claims against powerful counterparts. The DS379 narrative then shows a consequence, not just the existence of trained lawyers. The dated contrast between presently available China–EU and China–US appellate arrangements also prevents that history from becoming a simple progress story.

The 2025 licensing narrative establishes another consequential development in bargaining instruments: reciprocal relief could be delivered through administrative permissions while China's authority to restrict survived. It identifies some things each government obtained. It does not yet measure how much more effective this instrument was than earlier pressure. The book is appropriately cautious about that distinction.

The weaker historical claim concerns the growing domestic reach of agreements. And the biggest omission is that Xi's period appears mainly as the date of episodes, followed by a warning against explaining them through his personality. That warning is justified but does not answer the institutional question. These are the two priorities below. Neither calls for a national-style verdict or a new inventory of disconnected examples.

## 1. Xi's institutional changes are acknowledged, but not given an explanatory task

**Anchors:** Introduction, “Since Richard H. Solomon studied the opening…” and “These are demonstrated changes…”; conclusion, “What the update requires,” especially “The cases cannot assign the differences…to Xi’s personality”; Chapter 5, “The missing agreement”; Chapter 3, “What Copenhagen left unfinished” through “National choice and reciprocal acceptance.”

The ending currently groups “a more centralized leadership” with economic size and summit friendliness as context. This is too quick. Party supervision, information channels and the scope for a professional negotiator to explore an offer can change the work even when the available evidence cannot identify the leader's psychology or show that concessions became uniformly smaller. Conversely, the creation of a commission does not prove tighter instructions at every meeting. The manuscript needs to say what actually changed at this intermediate institutional level, and where its effect is demonstrable or still unknown.

The existing Cabestan and Jakobson–Manuel reading memos already distinguish prescribed authority, information, approval and execution. The preserved primary checks establish formal organizational changes in 2014 and 2018 and a later statutory description in 2023. They do not establish improved coordination or the deciding body in May 2019. At this audit's cutoff, the parent's new Taylor–Garlick/Loh reading, the separate agent's 2018/2023 original-document work and the ongoing military-channel investigation are **planned or in progress**, not evidence already integrated into these chapters.

**The most economical behavioral test is the existing climate sequence.** Keep the actors, policy area and relationship visible: Stern and Xie across Copenhagen and the 2014 announcement. The current chapter documents a 2013 working group and agency assignments; Stern's retrospective account of extended confidential preparation; and Xie's contemporary account of expert disagreement, central choice and reciprocal acceptability. This permits a narrower history of how specialists prepared a jointly endorsable proposal under Xi. It does not establish that Xi gave them more or less discretion than Hu or Wen. Copenhagen had extensive preparation too; “improvised old diplomacy became professional under Xi” would be false to the chapter.

The source target should therefore be the **change in the envoys' assignment and route to approval**, not a search for an adjective describing the leader. Reconstruct the 2013 working group's authorized tasks against the earlier bilateral climate arrangements, then use the complete Xie *Caixin* interview, Stern's account and the 2014 statements to distinguish drafting, evaluation, recommendation and approval. The 2009 bilateral climate cooperation instrument is a necessary acquisition target before calling the 2013 machinery the first continuing channel. Conrad's Copenhagen explanation remains interview-based and should not become a definitive account of Wen's instructions.

May 2019 supplies a demanding contrasting case under the same later leader: the manuscript cannot recover the redline's approval status or identify who changed the instruction. Do not use that absence to demonstrate centralization's effect. Instead, say that a more explicit central supervisory structure did not make a counterpart's clearance boundary observable. The different evidentiary results are themselves useful: specialization and political supervision coexisted; they did not mechanically determine whether a particular bargain was authorized.

**A bounded improvement now:** add a short dated account of the formal changes, then connect it to those two concrete processes. If the new institutional scholarship supplies evidence of altered reporting incentives, delegation or correction, identify whose observations support it and during which years. If it supplies only organizational theory and coherent outcomes, leave the behavioral effect open. This would answer the Xi question more directly without claiming that centralization caused either the climate accommodation or the trade breakdown. It also explains why a contemporary visitor may need technical access and political clearance through different channels.

## 2. “Further into domestic administration” needs a pre-Xi bargain to compare

**Anchors:** Introduction, central thesis paragraph and “The later American trade negotiations placed more demands…”; conclusion, “Other agreements changed what a commitment required…”; Chapter 5, “Whose reform?” and “The right to complain.”

The present evidence shows particular later duties, but not their greater domestic depth relative to a consistently specified earlier baseline. Relative to the opening of diplomatic relations, that direction may be plausible. Relative to Freeman's 1999 account, it is not a discovery: his printed p. 191 expressly discusses foreign demands to change Chinese domestic policies and laws, including intellectual-property and sensitive-export disputes in the early and middle 1990s. Accession itself already required more than a promise of cooperation. The chapter's detailed market-access comparisons reinforce that point.

This is not a reason to remove Phase One. It is a reason to identify its actual historical novelty. A demand for Chinese legal changes, American threats of retaliation, an action plan and continued implementation bargaining could all have predecessors. The possible change may instead concern the specified complaint route, confidentiality, deadlines, the good-faith/no-counterresponse bargain, or the scale and setting of the exchange. Those possibilities must be compared rather than bundled into “more demands.”

**Named case and original-source targets:** the 1995 intellectual-property bargain and the ensuing 1996 enforcement controversy, compared with Phase One Chapters 1 and 7. Solomon's own bibliography identifies the House International Relations Subcommittee on International Economic Policy and Trade hearing, *The U.S.-China Intellectual Property Rights Agreement: Implications for U.S.-Sino Commercial Relations*, March 2, 1995, and *The U.S.-China Intellectual Property Rights Agreement and Related Trade Issues*, March 7, 1996 (the bibliography dates publication of the latter to 1997). These are precise acquisition leads, not readings already completed in this pass.

Read the actual exchanged letters/action plan if reproduced in the hearing, plus the negotiating official's explanation of monitoring and the next threatened remedy. Do not read hundreds of pages merely to add a complete-reading count. Extract the earlier duty, who had to perform it, what information Washington could obtain, whether implementation was to be monitored jointly, and whether tariff pressure arose from the agreement or independently from American law. Compare these with Phase One Articles 1.34–1.35 and 7.4, preserving that distinction between contractual remedy and unilateral Section 301 action. A later congressional allegation of nonperformance is evidence of the allegation and enforcement dispute, not independent adjudication of every factory's conduct.

This is an unusually productive missing middle: the same large counterpart, a related domestic enforcement problem, and a baseline explicitly anticipated by Freeman. A successful comparison can show either a specific innovation or important continuity. Neither outcome is a research failure. It should occupy a short bridge in Chapter 5, with the conclusion revised to name the demonstrated difference. Acquisition and reading of this bounded original-source target are the next authorized task; their results will be recorded separately.

## What the historical answer can already say

The book can distinguish three periods without pretending to have isolated a national personality change. The original opening supplied the relational and political problems Solomon described. Accession and subsequent legal-capacity building supplied new routes for contesting powerful counterparts, before Xi's tenure. Under Xi, the examined record contains both negotiated scrutiny and openings, and confrontations in which China sought reciprocal relief while preserving control. Industrial dependence and altered procedures changed the available transactions; they do not establish a uniform change in willingness to compromise.

The revisions should make that mixed historical result explicit. “Not every difference can be assigned to Xi” is an evidentiary boundary, not a sufficient answer. “Agreements need authority and implementation” is useful advice, not the historical finding. The finding needs the dated institutional change, the behavior it enabled or constrained, the counterpart's changed choice, and the older example against which its novelty has been tested.
