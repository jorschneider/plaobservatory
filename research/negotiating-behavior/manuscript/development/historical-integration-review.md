# Review of the historical comparison and institutional integration

15 September 2026. Bounded, read-only review of the changed introduction, Chapter 5 and conclusion passages and their immediate context. This is not a new whole-book review or publication-readiness judgment.

## Result

The substantive historical correction succeeds. The introduction and conclusion no longer make domestic specificity, monitoring or tariff-backed enforcement a Phase One innovation. The added Roth–Barshefsky exchange gives the comparison an actor and a decision rather than simply adding an exception in a note. Chapter 5 distinguishes private domestic petitions, the American domestic-law route back to pressure, and the later bilateral interstate procedure. Its comparison establishes a particular difference in accepted terms without ranking implementation or Chinese willingness to concede.

The institutional additions also remain within the evidence reviewed. The 2018 passage identifies a concrete transfer of work rather than treating the commission's new title as sufficient evidence of complete operational control. The conclusion describes the 2023 law as stating responsibilities, not originating every function. Its note explicitly prevents the earlier May channel sequence from becoming evidence of that law's effect. Loh's fieldwork is used for reported contact constraints and continuing initiative, not as proof of a reduced willingness to exchange concessions or a recovered Liu He mandate. The resulting historical argument is more informative without claiming that the institutional changes caused the May 2019 breakdown.

Three small corrections remain in the reviewed version:

1. **Introduction, line 39, “a timetable for accepting complaints.”** The annex permits refusal on stated criteria and requires a written explanation within the same fifteen days. The note gets this right, but the introductory summary should too. Replace with **“a deadline for accepting petitions or explaining refusal.”** This also avoids confusing the older private petition with the later interstate appeal.
2. **Chapter 5, line 97, “acceptance of a good-faith measure without counterresponse.”** The preceding paragraph accurately attributes the good-faith judgment to the respondent, but the new summary can imply an obligation to accept the merits or an objectively determined good-faith status. Replace with **“no counterresponse to a measure it judged taken in good faith.”** Article 7.4.4(b) constrains the next response; the analysis need not claim substantive agreement with the measure.
3. **Chapter 5, line 101, “had become an explicit part of this settlement.”** This surviving transition implies newly explicit post-signature negotiation immediately after the text has demonstrated its earlier formalization. Replace the first sentence with **“Both settlements formalized the continuation of negotiation after signature.”** The remaining sentences properly distinguish continued consultations from Solomon's stronger claim about asymmetric accommodation.

The conclusion's good-faith sentence already uses the narrower respondent-judgment formulation. No additional correction is requested there. With the three local changes above, no stronger novelty claim remains implied in the particular passages reviewed. This does not turn the cases into a measured before-and-after study of Xi's concession flexibility: the manuscript now claims institutional and procedural changes, identifies their practical implications, and preserves the missing causal comparison.
