## The change in the argument's delivery

The chapter now sustains the normalization negotiation before explaining the baseline. It begins with Woodcock arriving late on December 14 because his instructions had changed, then follows the preceding offers into the final arms-sales impasse. The account specifies what Deng wanted changed, what Woodcock had to refer to Washington, what Carter accepted or resisted, and why the announcement still endangered an otherwise available agreement. The record supports these actions; no physical scene, dialogue or interior state has been invented.

The one-year pause is distinguished from already committed deliveries. The wording change from principles expressed to principles agreed on makes the difference between a common instrument and assent to every position concrete. The December 15 decision preserves the Chinese objection and the American intention to speak publicly; it is not recast as Chinese consent to arms sales. Woodcock's estimate of Deng's political difficulty remains his assessment. Deng's knowledge of Brzezinski's message is not turned into proof that it caused the meeting or changed his instructions.

Solomon enters after the reader has encountered the problem he was explaining. Publication history overlapping the introduction is mostly in note 2. The relationship argument remains affirmative: access, obligations, official discipline, existing American divisions and the purposes that made an accommodation attractive. His scope restriction concerning a constructive relationship is retained.

The claims case now follows the usefulness and limits of Zhou's $17 million proposal. Congressional acceptability is Kissinger's estimate, not an observed vote. Terms concerning nationals, old bonds and the payment route explain why the political agreement still needed work. Solomon's December 31 memorandum is presented as advice: a personal message, a senior trade visitor and broader leadership contacts, followed by Kissinger's distinct decisions on those proposals. This restores the author as an actor trying to make the relationship more durable, while preserving the source limits on his later internal-politics explanation.

The monetary offer's withdrawal is distinct from nonperformance of a completed agreement. Hummel's account of American bottlenecks is not asserted to have caused the claims failure. Vance's 1977 decision to defer the issue shows a government choosing what not to make a condition of normalization. The chapter stops there; it does not purport to narrate the final 1979 claims settlement.

Solomon's original “the most reliable sign” remains strong. Its ranking is unresolved, not disproved by the selected counterexample. The Gang of Four explanation and the conflict diagnosis are accurately presented as his claims; their mechanisms are not independently proven. Freeman's institutional and specialization arguments remain inherited observations, rather than new discoveries of this update.

## Bounded attempt to recover the June 14, 1974 message

Two short web searches found an exact public archival lead but not the message itself. [FRUS XIII/27](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v13/d27), April 29, 1977, note 4 identifies **telegram 985 from Beijing, June 14, 1974**, National Archives, RG 59, Central Foreign Policy File, without a film number. The full published April 1977 entry was returned and read; it includes Han Hsu's claim of continuity in China's position. That claim is not independent proof of what the missing 1974 instruction said.

Search also returned the Ford Library's [October 19–23, 1975 trip file](https://www.fordlibrarymuseum.gov/sites/default/files/pdf_documents/library/document/0331/1553943.pdf), whose indexed excerpt reports the Chinese side saying the 1973 payment proposal had been withdrawn in its June 14 letter. The full PDF was not acquired or read in this pass and is not a new source for the chapter. No new primary document is adopted on the strength of a search snippet. The next acquisition should target the specific telegram/message, not another general account of the disagreement. Its absence did not block this rewrite.
