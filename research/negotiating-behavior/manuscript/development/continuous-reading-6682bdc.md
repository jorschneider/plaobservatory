## 1. Let the decision arrive before explaining its lesson

**The clearest break occurs in Chapter 2, lines 55–69.** The Americans have stayed beyond their announced departure, accused China of reopening issues and asked for Zhu. Before the reader sees Zhu arrive, the narrative pauses for the conflicting recollections, a paragraph beginning “The distinction should survive the story’s happy ending,” and another beginning “For a negotiator considering a deadline.” We are asked to assess the outcome before reaching it. The vivid summons, Shi's misunderstanding and Zhu's arrival lose force.

Move the arrival, signature and unfinished American/WTO work directly after the November 14 letter. Then present the disagreement between Barshefsky and Deng. Retain one paragraph about the actual choice between departure and an extension tied to a specific meeting. Cut the duplicate abstract explanation. The disagreement about causation stays explicit; it no longer postpones the event it qualifies. This is a local rearrangement of available evidence, not a license to credit the deadline with causing agreement.

**Chapter 5, lines 35–37, has the same problem with a new insertion.** The preceding paragraph asks whether the principals had accepted the same obligations; the next section supplies the May rupture. Between them come maritime coordination, the commission's office, unpublished rules and three hypothetical ways a stronger center might act. None identifies what happened to Liu's draft. The passage ends by posing the authority question again, which lines 49–53 then answer as far as the record permits.

Keep approximately fifty words about the dated institutional change and the unknown mandate here; place the maritime transfer and detailed assigned functions in the existing note. For example:

> China had reorganized central foreign-affairs supervision in 2018, converting the leading group into a commission chaired by Xi. The published remit clarified coordinating responsibilities. It did not disclose which terms Liu could accept or what approvals the draft required. The May dispute left that distinction consequential and unresolved.

This preserves the historical development without implying that it caused the rupture. The introduction and conclusion can give its larger significance once. The existing two-paragraph interruption does not earn its length **at this location**.

The other additions should not suffer indiscriminate trimming. Chapter 3's “Learning to use membership” earns its space because it explains the acquisition of the capability the chapter tests. Loh's paragraph in Chapter 4, line 99, changes how a reader should interpret accompanied meetings and ordinary access after Kissinger's exceptional reception. Most importantly, **the 1995 insertion in Chapter 5, lines 85–87 and 97, earns its length**: Roth asks a concrete question, Barshefsky proposes a response, the earlier instrument changes the historical baseline, and the later remedy comparison states an actual difference. Preserve that sequence. Its repetition elsewhere is the problem addressed below.

## 2. Make the ending choose among bargains rather than inventory the cases again

The introduction's “What changed at the table” already previews almost every principal result. Chapter endings draw practical lessons. The conclusion then reviews the cases by authority, performance and rules, before “What the update requires” reviews several again. On a continuous reading, the argument seems to restart when it should accumulate.

The clearest duplication is inside the conclusion. Its Phase One remedy appears at lines 59 and 75; legal capability and procedural availability at 55–57 and 73; climate discretion at 63 and 77. Those are substantive ideas, but the second appearance rarely changes the reader's decision. The institutional and 1995 additions have strengthened the historical verdict while increasing this duplication.

Make these exact cuts and moves:

- In the introduction, lines 35–49, remove the separate climate, Phase One performance and 2023 military-channel previews. Keep the historical developments and the 1995 qualification; the full chapters can disclose the outcomes. This removes roughly two hundred words of advance summary without losing the opening predicament or the actual change thesis.
- Remove the conclusion's standalone “Rules allocate discretion as well as constrain it” section, lines 53–67, after transferring its essential material. Carry one sentence about counterpart-dependent appeal availability into the existing legal-capacity paragraph at line 73. Keep the single 1995/Phase One comparison at line 75. Preserve the point that ordinary implementation work is not automatically a reopened promise in the performance discussion or its note. The treaty-detail recapitulation and repeated proposition-testing instructions are already available in the chapters and appendix.
- Give the space and emphasis to the conclusion's best synthesis, lines 19–21: a completed release versus a benefit requiring repeated permission. Connect it directly to the costs of enforcement rather than introducing another case tour.

A possible connecting passage, explicitly offered as practical inference rather than another historical finding:

> Once the EP-3 crew had left, continued disagreement over responsibility could not undo their departure. An approved magnet shipment left the next shipment exposed. These bargains call for different reciprocal offers. A completed act may justify payment of the agreed price at once. Continuing access gives the other side reason to seek a longer permission, a staged exchange or an alternative source of supply. Those protections have costs and may be refused. The negotiator must then decide whether immediate relief is valuable enough to accept without them—and whether today's concession leaves anything useful to offer when the next permission is needed.

Keep the distinct historical verdict at the end: acquired legal capacity; changed supervision and access conditions; and industrial dependence that makes Chinese permissions valuable abroad. Preserve the pre-Xi enforcement comparison so this does not become a claim that detailed bargaining began recently. This would leave readers with both an answer about change and a consequential choice. The current repeated summaries leave them chiefly with the instruction to identify the relevant act, an instruction they have understood well before the final chapter.
