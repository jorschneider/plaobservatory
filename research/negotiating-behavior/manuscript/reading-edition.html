An original reassessment of Richard H. Solomon’s Chinese Negotiating Behavior, with seven chapters, a conclusion and three research appendices. Individual cases have different event endpoints. No new interviews or private Chinese archive access are claimed. This is not an authorized new edition.

Introduction: What an agreement buys

On November 12, 1999, an American diplomat in Beijing put a deadline in writing. The delegation led by US Trade Representative Charlene Barshefsky would leave its hotel at eight the next morning. If Premier Zhu Rongji would meet her, the Americans could postpone departure. They wanted an answer by seven. Two days later, another letter declared another impasse and urgently requested another meeting with Zhu. The letters invoked the intentions of the two countries’ leaders while arguing over what their negotiators had settled.1

It is a familiar scene in accounts of Chinese diplomacy: exhausted visitors, apparently immovable officials, a threatened departure, a senior leader who might unlock the bargain. Yet these particular documents complicate the familiar story. The Americans were setting deadlines and seeking the leader’s intervention. Their negotiating papers were changing, too. They distinguished ownership from managerial control and offered different durations for protections against Chinese imports. A Chinese proposal made its concessions conditional on completing a larger package. The question was not simply whether Beijing would become more flexible. It was which combination each government could authorize, and which earlier positions still counted.2

Those distinctions mattered. Fifty percent ownership of an insurance venture was not the same thing as control of it. Permission to operate was not the same thing as immediate access to every city or customer. Signing a bilateral market-access agreement was not the same thing as China becoming a member of the World Trade Organization. The agreement signed on November 15 expressly directed the governments to send its annexes onward for incorporation into the multilateral accession negotiations. Its signatories knew that they had completed one bargain inside a larger process.3

This book asks how that process of making commitments has changed. Its starting point is Richard H. Solomon’s Chinese Negotiating Behavior, a study of how Chinese officials pursued national interests through political relationships, disciplined delegations, principles, pressure and bargaining after signature. Its endpoint is the negotiating environment of 2026. The United States remains the principal counterpart, but American experience alone cannot establish a Chinese national style. Europe, India and Russia help reveal which practices travel, which depend on the relationship, and which appear on both sides of the table.

Political authority and specialized bargaining have to be explained together. Relationships help determine what a negotiation is for and which channels are usable. Ministries, regulators, legal procedures and commercial actors help determine what an agreement can deliver. The value of alternatives constrains what either country will pay to obtain it. A leader can settle a question of policy while leaving other authorities to determine when, and under what conditions, its benefits become usable. Greater expertise or a stronger position can change the instruments available without removing political limits on their use. The chapters test those connections through offers and decisions, rather than inferring them from an announcement of success.

The Solomon we need to update

The 2005 printing date is misleading as an empirical starting point. Solomon’s principal study concerns 1967–1984. It originated in a government commission in 1983; an unclassified briefing appeared in 1985, declassified portions in 1995, and the new edition in 1999. Charles W. Freeman Jr.’s essay in that edition provided a separate assessment of subsequent developments. Updating the book therefore requires distinguishing the normalization-era evidence from the later commentary already attached to it.4

Solomon had an unusual vantage point. He had participated in the opening to China, obtained access to official American records, and interviewed senior negotiators. His book preserved experiences that final communiqués could not convey: the effort to establish a relationship before resolving an issue, the unease of being reminded what an earlier American visitor had promised, and the difficulty of knowing whether an official was bargaining or waiting for instructions. These were observations about a demanding political process, grounded in more than a list of outcomes.

He also understood more of the qualifications than a shorthand account of the book might suggest. He distinguished a characteristic practice from a uniquely Chinese invention. He warned that analytical compression made Chinese conduct appear more coherent and prescient than it had been. He gave state interests and official discipline priority over the effects of interpersonal tactics. Most consequentially, he studied a period when Beijing had decided that a constructive relationship with Washington served its purposes, and expressly left open whether the findings would apply under renewed confrontation.5

That last qualification changes the question. If China behaves differently in a confrontation, the difference may expose a boundary Solomon recognized. It does not automatically show that Chinese political culture changed. Conversely, if an apparently familiar practice survives, its cause may have changed. A request to speak to the president could seek personal reassurance, a new mandate, a concession outside a minister’s jurisdiction, or a public signal to an audience elsewhere. Similar performances can do different work.

Freeman had already begun this revision. He connected venue preferences and reluctance to improvise with communications, budgets and the difficulty of obtaining instructions. He described the growth of technical specialization and participation in multilateral organizations. He argued that officials learned practices suited to particular counterparts. The later book cannot announce institutions, learning or variation as discoveries that its predecessors overlooked. It must establish what developed further and how that development reached an actual negotiating choice.6

The changes that need explaining

Several developments deserve a sustained historical explanation. One concerns the institutions through which commitments reach domestic policy. A 2014 notice required trade-policy compliance assessments and provided for consultation with the commerce ministry when the drafting department considered it necessary. A 2025 opinion replaced it, expressly requiring assessment before issuance, written findings accompanying policy submissions for approval, and changes where incompatibility risks were identified. Departmental self-assessment and optional consultation with the commerce ministry remained. Those are documented changes in formal procedure; their effectiveness cannot be read off the rules. Scholarship on post-accession trade expertise supplies a broader account of training, counsel and interagency work, whose effects need to be followed into particular decisions. Detailed Chinese documents existed well before accession. Precision alone is not a new trait.7

Another is the coexistence, in the later American trade negotiations, of coercive measures, partial settlements and elaborate procedures for reviewing their implementation. Phase One’s detailed obligations and consultation machinery did not end disagreement about performance. Different sectors produced different results. China’s securities regulator expressly attributed an eight-month acceleration of ownership liberalization to Phase One; JPMorgan Securities (China)’s initial operating permission and later full ownership were separate events. That concrete acceleration belongs in the same account as delayed agricultural approvals and the failure to reach the purchase levels examined in the research. The contrast requires explanation at the level of the obligation and implementing actor. Neither a claim of complete transformation nor a claim of uniform evasion fits that record.8

Climate bargaining supplies an intervening development in the form of cooperation itself. The 2009 Copenhagen Accord already distinguished national mitigation actions from external consultation and analysis. Cancun specified procedures; the 2014 US–China statement endorsed different national intentions; the September 2015 statement developed principles for transparency and review; and the Paris text established common procedural duties with differentiated application. Paris imposed duties to prepare, communicate and maintain contributions and pursue domestic mitigation measures, without an internationally binding duty to achieve each contribution’s announced result. China disputed particular review proposals during the negotiations before accepting adoption of the final architecture. The record establishes a change in instruments and specificity, with national discretion preserved on a crucial bargaining object. It does not isolate learning or a leader’s personality as its cause.9

These developments do not establish a single trajectory from Mao to Xi. WTO legal practice and climate negotiations reveal substantive uses of expertise between accession and the trade war, while leaving the origin of a particular capability harder to identify. The 2001 EP-3 crisis and the 2023 confrontation over the balloon test Solomon’s cooperation-based account in different disputes. They show that communication, a shared interpretation and a limited commitment can come apart. The crew arrangement left the aircraft and responsibility for the collision to further decisions; the 2023 diplomatic channel operated while specified military contacts remained unavailable.10

Kissinger’s July 2023 visit also gives the original subtitle’s “old friends” a later test. It shows cultivation, requests for advocacy and unusual access, alongside evidence of consultation with American officials. It does not supply the private message and resulting instruction needed to establish his effect on the November agreements. Formal channels were already operating. The distinction between an honored adviser and an authorized negotiator becomes an essential part of the update, rather than a conclusion supplied by the reception itself.11

The comparisons with other major powers ask a different question. Russia and China have reached agreements within a close strategic relationship while continuing to disagree over commercial terms for new gas projects. Border settlements have distinguished sovereignty from residents’ continued economic use. India’s local security arrangements involve different rights and a different territorial setting. These cases show why a relationship’s political label cannot tell us the content of a particular concession. They also prevent a study dominated by American trade negotiations from quietly presenting itself as an account of every Chinese encounter.12

Reading what the actors could know

An account written after an agreement has a dangerous advantage over the people who negotiated it. We know that the threatened departure did not end the talks, that an apparently firm position changed, or that an announced project remained unfinished. The participants did not necessarily know which outcome would follow. To understand their behavior, the book reconstructs offers, instructions where recoverable, and the alternatives available at the time. It distinguishes what a participant could know from what an outside observer could learn, then or through a later release. A later recollection helps identify what to look for; it does not erase an earlier paper.

That approach sometimes changes a dramatic story. The November 1999 offers already contained figures later associated with the final encounter. The meaningful remaining dispute could concern control, timing or an exchange elsewhere in the package. Sometimes it changes the judgment of failure. A political promise to review an application differs from an unconditional promise to approve it. A later dispute about which applications were covered matters even when the jointly agreed list cannot be recovered. Sometimes it strengthens a criticism: when an obligation, its conditions and the relevant performance measure are established, a shortfall can be described without explaining it away as cultural misunderstanding.

The sources make unequal kinds of knowledge possible. A declassified American memorandum records what an American note-taker understood to have been said. A Chinese ministry’s documentary collection can preserve a signed text and surround it with a much later explanation of why it mattered. A participant may remember the authorization chain while misdating a meeting. A white paper is authoritative evidence of the government’s public position; its account of a disputed private exchange remains an account to test. The book draws these distinctions where they change the story and leaves the detailed reading records in the accompanying research apparatus.

This project has not conducted new interviews or gained access to private Chinese negotiating files. That limits some explanations, particularly of who resisted a concession and why. It does not require abandoning the history or substituting rhetoric for evidence. Available records can establish a sequence, disprove an inaccurate chronology, identify the operative condition, or show that competing explanations remain alive. A serious update should be precise about both what it has learned and what it cannot see.

The practical payoff is a different way of reading an encounter. A negotiator needs to know whose decision is required, which part of the package remains open, what the proposed instrument would authorize, and whether the promised benefit can survive the next institutional step. Cultural familiarity can help interpret the exchange. It cannot supply those answers on its own. The history begins with the agreements inside the agreement.

1. What Solomon actually explained

On December 15, 1978, Leonard Woodcock reported that Deng Xiaoping remained opposed to American arms sales to Taiwan. Deng nevertheless agreed to proceed with the schedule for establishing diplomatic relations. Washington then instructed Woodcock to make clear that the United States would reaffirm its own position when asked, while acknowledging that China did not endorse it. The two governments were preparing to announce an agreement whose most consequential unfinished business was known to both.13

This is a useful place to begin an update of Richard Solomon’s Chinese Negotiating Behavior. It prevents a misleading story about a newly pragmatic China learning to separate issues, accept partial settlements or live with disagreement. Those capacities were present in the period Solomon studied. They were also part of his account. The historical question is how the forms and uses of agreement changed, and how far his explanations can travel beyond the relationship that supplied his evidence.

The closing exchanges also reveal limits on both sides. Deng could authorize movement, but Woodcock still needed instructions from Washington. Their agreement settled recognition while preserving the arms-sales dispute. Later objections to a reserved issue cannot simply be counted as departures from a shared commitment.

The book’s two historical baselines

The date on the supplied printing is 2005; the edition is copyrighted 1999. Neither date describes the principal empirical period. Solomon’s study was commissioned in 1983 and completed in the mid-1980s. An unclassified RAND briefing followed in 1985, and declassified portions appeared in 1995. The core study chiefly examines the American–Chinese negotiating experience of 1967–1984. The later edition adds framing and an essay by Chas. W. Freeman Jr. assessing developments since that period.14

That genealogy matters for the meaning of change. A practice described by Freeman in the late 1990s cannot fairly be presented as a discovery of a 2026 update merely because it differs from the early Kissinger years. Nor should a book’s later publication date be mistaken for evidence that its central propositions had been tested against the intervening negotiations. Solomon and Freeman supply related but distinct baselines: an intensive account of the establishment of a relationship, followed by a participant’s interpretation of its subsequent institutional development.

Solomon’s evidence went well beyond public outcomes. He used official memoranda of conversation and reporting cables, interviewed more than thirty senior American negotiators, and supplemented those interviews with officials outside the State Department and businessmen. His own service on the National Security Council staff and subsequent access helped him interpret the record. The acknowledgments show a deliberate effort to assemble documentation from successive administrations. Personal experience and institutional memory were research assets.15

They also shaped what the study could see. Its documentary and interview perspective was principally American. A memorandum recording a Chinese official’s words is evidence of an encounter, mediated by interpretation and recording. It is not automatically evidence of the instruction the official received, the debates behind it or the reason a concession became possible. The public edition cannot be assumed to disclose every source available to the classified study. The right response to this asymmetry is to identify it and seek counterevidence, without pretending that public Chinese statements now provide equivalent access to internal decision making.

The study’s scope is narrower than a theory of how China always negotiates. Solomon expressly asks whether insights developed while Beijing wanted a constructive American relationship would apply in a future return to confrontation. He leaves the answer open. He also contrasts the experience of earlier American interlocutors with that of the normalization years, sometimes involving the same Chinese officials. Strategic purpose already helps explain variation inside his account.16

These qualifications make the original more useful. They also make the later test harder. More confrontational conduct does not automatically refute a study of cooperation; it may cross an explicit boundary of that study. Conversely, invoking that boundary cannot excuse an update from examining confrontation. The modern inquiry must ask which mechanisms still operate when the relationship itself is less attractive, the available costs are different and avoiding agreement is a more credible choice.

What a relationship could accomplish

Solomon organized his account around the construction and use of a political relationship. Chinese officials sought shared principles, assessed the counterpart over extended exchanges, cultivated selected interlocutors, preserved earlier statements and continued bargaining during implementation. Personal access could create obligations. An official who valued recognition as a trusted friend might be pressed to demonstrate that friendship through policy, explain China’s concerns inside his own government or rescue a relationship threatened by an impasse.17

That account contains several mechanisms which should not be collapsed into one. A familiar interlocutor may convey information credibly. He may possess authority to obtain a decision. He may serve as an advocate because he agrees with the policy. Or he may become vulnerable to pressure because losing access would damage his status or ambitions. The same meeting can be consistent with all four explanations. To establish interpersonal leverage, a history needs the demand, the interlocutor’s response and some evidence of what changed inside his government. Familiarity alone is not that evidence.

Solomon recognized an important part of this problem. Beijing did not create every rivalry it could exploit among American officials. Differences between agencies and senior advisers existed before Chinese officials selected particular channels. A successful appeal to one official might reveal an existing alignment rather than transform the balance of policy. Equally, a personal channel might overcome an information problem that formal communication had failed to resolve. Labeling every such contact manipulation would erase the reciprocal work required to establish a previously hostile relationship.18

The book’s treatment of discipline and authority also complicates a purely interpersonal account. An official could be constrained by instructions, vulnerable to political criticism or unable to reconcile the interests of different organizations. The senior leader’s role partly reflected that structure. Access mattered because someone could make a decision, not simply because someone could confer warmth. Solomon already describes the expansion of ministries and technical processes after the initial opening. The baseline is therefore not a China in which personal diplomacy had eliminated institutions.

The December 1978 records let us see these distinctions in a successful sequence. The earlier claims-and-assets talks show why they matter when senior intervention fails to finish a bargain.

Normalization with a disagreement preserved

Woodcock’s December 4 report describes Han Nien-lung presenting Chinese conditions and a bilingual draft. Han opposed continued arms sales and refused a Chinese pledge to resolve the Taiwan question peacefully. He allowed the United States to express its own hope for a peaceful resolution. The parties were distinguishing their common text from the positions each might announce separately. At the close, Han informed Woodcock that Deng wished to meet him.19

The announcement of senior involvement fits Solomon’s account of an approaching endgame. Its meaning becomes clearer when placed beside what the Americans were doing. In Washington on December 11, Zbigniew Brzezinski encouraged Ambassador Chai Zemin to meet the January 1 target and discussed the political value of a senior Chinese visit. A possible Soviet summit entered the conversation too. Sequence, access and the wider strategic relationship were instruments used by the Americans as well as concerns of the Chinese.20

Deng’s opening in the December 13 meeting acknowledged the December 11 exchange. That establishes his knowledge of it before the meeting. It does not establish that the message arrived before the meeting was scheduled, or that it caused Deng’s readiness to compromise. Michel Oksenberg’s contemporary covering note offered an inference about timing; his 1980 retrospective made a stronger claim about the message’s effect. The distinction is small in a narrative of successful diplomacy but large in a causal explanation: a leader can know of an appeal without having been persuaded by it.21

Deng’s discussion with Woodcock separated several clocks and commitments. He accepted the American communiqué draft as a basis, while proposing anti-hegemony language either in the common text or in separate statements. The period for withdrawing American forces was distinct from the one-year notice associated with the defense treaty. Acceptance of the treaty-termination wording was tied to a pause in new arms sales during that year and to how the termination would be described. Detailed work would pass to vice ministers. Woodcock would obtain further instructions.22

This was substantive senior participation. It did not eliminate technical bargaining or American limits. Carter’s recorded annotations favored accommodating the one-year pause, resisted a broader anti-hegemony formulation and recognized that public explanations could not hide every legal detail. In his own assessment, Woodcock warned against treating Chinese willingness to proceed as permission for arms sales after 1979. He was recommending a way forward while identifying the boundary of what had been accepted.23

On December 14 the wording of the communiqué remained consequential. The reported changes distinguished principles agreed by both sides in Shanghai from all the positions expressed in the Shanghai Communiqué. A common declaration containing unilateral positions could not simply be transformed into a list of mutually accepted obligations. This was not a dispute over ceremonial language alone. The distinction limited what one party could subsequently attribute to the other.24

The last exchanges made the arms-sales disagreement explicit. Woodcock expected later Chinese objections and believed their effect would depend on the broader relationship. Washington thought public silence impracticable because Congress, the press and Taiwan required explanations. Deng then agreed to proceed with normalization while maintaining his objection. Brzezinski’s final instruction required the Americans to acknowledge China’s non-endorsement when asserting their own position.25

The resulting communiqué established diplomatic relations effective January 1, 1979, with embassies and ambassadors separately scheduled for March 1. It stated common principles and the agreed description of future unofficial American relations with Taiwan. It did not convert the documented dispute over arms sales into shared consent. The official Chinese and English versions are evidence of the common instrument, not two independent accounts of how it was negotiated.26

The achievement was considerable precisely because agreement did not require agreement on everything. Recognition, diplomatic representation, withdrawal, treaty termination and public explanations could be arranged while an important dispute survived. The record supports Solomon’s attention to partial settlements and reserved positions. It does not by itself establish why the parties found that arrangement tolerable. Shared strategic purpose, the value of normalization, domestic constraints and the handling of public presentation are all visible; the independent effect of saving face is harder to recover.

When Zhou’s intervention was insufficient

The private-claims/blocked-assets dispute is particularly valuable because Solomon included it himself. American property claims against China and Chinese assets blocked by Washington created a potential exchange, but the assets were not a single uncontested pool. Some deposits in third-country banks had already been returned to China. Definitions affected which funds could be transferred, and claims against earlier Chinese governments raised separate questions. The amount mattered to American claimants; the legal form mattered to both governments.

The American position also required authorization. Nixon’s August 1972 instruction preferred a Chinese lump-sum payment, possibly in installments, in return for unblocking assets. If that proved non-negotiable, Washington should propose retaining blocked assets to compensate American claimants. State and Treasury were to prepare a negotiating scenario for presidential approval. By February 1973, Zhou and Kissinger were advocating a broad political treatment; the later American review describes an agreement in principle on mutual assignment of claims.27

In November the senior discussion exposed what that understanding had left unresolved. Kissinger told Zhou that excluding the third-country money would reduce the prospective compensation below what he believed Congress would accept. Zhou regarded the banks’ repayment of the money to China as lawful. He also objected to definitions inherited from the period of hostility. Kissinger acknowledged that he could not settle one of those wording questions without consulting legal advisers. Hummel explained that the executive could decline to support old bond claims but could not simply prevent private parties from approaching the courts.28

Zhou then proposed restoring $17 million, favoring payment directly to the American government. Routing it back through the banks risked implying recognition of the blocking measures whose legality China disputed. Kissinger was receptive and emphasized the value of the sum for the prospective compensation. But he promised further legal consultation and another proposal. His report to Nixon six days later described continuing exchanges toward final resolution, not a concluded settlement.29

This is a clear instance of a senior leader proposing material movement. The full bargain still required work. A gesture could be politically important without resolving the definitions, payment route, presentation and domestic acceptance that would make it usable. Describing all those questions as technical must not make them seem economically or politically empty.

The later American record reports a reversal: Zhou’s monetary offer was withdrawn in 1974 and had not been reinstated by the 1977 discussions. The exact June 14, 1974 Chinese message cited in Solomon’s book has not been acquired for this reassessment. The Vance and Brzezinski memoranda establish the American government’s understanding of the withdrawal, not the Chinese instruction that produced it. An October 1975 meeting independently records the continuing disagreement, including Deng’s objection to any settlement saying that Chinese were required to observe American law.30

The distinction does not erase the reversal. It identifies its object. Retraction of a monetary offer, abandonment of an agreement in principle and failure to perform a completed instrument are not interchangeable events. The first two may damage confidence seriously without being the third. An account concerned with implementation must establish which commitment existed before measuring departure from it.

Solomon’s explanation linked the failure to bureaucratic resistance and internal political pressure. A contemporary memorandum helps recover the origin of that interpretation. On December 31, 1973, he warned Kissinger that political and bureaucratic forces threatened the durability of the relationship. The claims talks were among his examples. He drew on reporting that is partly redacted in the published record and on attachments not printed there. He also acknowledged grounds for Chinese suspicion of the settlement’s mechanics.31

This is stronger evidence than a later cultural analogy, but it remains an analyst’s explanation supported by a source chain we cannot fully inspect. The memorandum and the later book are not independent confirmations. They show Solomon developing an interpretation while participating in policy. Recovering the Chinese decision would allow a different test: which official objected to which provision, who could overrule the objection, and why the senior offer ceased to govern.

The American administrative record cautions against assuming that only one side had this problem. In February 1974, Hummel described Kissinger’s tight control of the relationship, competing demands on his attention, delayed answers and instructions held back or withdrawn. Those examples concern the broader handling of relations rather than proving an American cause of the claims failure. They nevertheless demonstrate that a Chinese counterpart waiting for an answer could also be observing constrained access to a senior decision maker.32

By May 1977, Vance recommended deferring further approaches until Washington had decided its normalization strategy. He explained why the technical differences materially affected the funds available for claimants and why he expected congressional difficulty. He also warned against making normalization itself hostage to the settlement. Carter approved deferral. The record thus contains both a political interpretation of why movement might become possible and a specific account of why details still mattered.33

Preserve the observation; test the prediction

The comparison exposes a tension inside Solomon’s book. At page 148 he records the aborted claims arrangement. On the following page he calls senior intervention “the most reliable sign” that agreement is at hand, explaining that a political leader would not risk his face in a negotiation about to collapse. His description of the endgame is confident, even though his own evidence includes failure.34

The appropriate update is to narrow that prediction explicitly. Senior involvement can disclose or alter authority, confirm a limit, resolve a disputed term or endorse work already done. Those are observable functions. These selected success and failure cases cannot rank senior intervention against other signals or estimate its predictive reliability. A practical forecasting rule must also specify what an observer could know before the outcome; much of the normalization sequence became accessible only later. They do not all imply that agreement is imminent. A leader may help settle one question while leaving another government unable to accept the package. Nor does the observation prove the proposed psychological mechanism: the desire to protect personal standing may matter, but the record must establish its effect rather than infer it from the leader’s presence.

This qualification belongs to the reassessment. It should not be attributed to Solomon by quietly replacing his strong wording with a modest claim that senior involvement sometimes matters. The same discipline applies to other propositions. His practical diagnosis that Chinese rigidity usually indicates leadership conflict is stronger than the evidence presented here can sustain. A firm position may reflect a settled preference, a legal objection, a bargaining tactic or lack of instructions. These explanations require different evidence and imply different responses.35

His treatment of principles benefits from a similar separation. Principles can organize a relationship and constrain later claims. They can also be negotiated in their wording and placement, as the normalization sequence demonstrates. In the claims case, the payment route carried a question of legal recognition. An abstract preference for flexible political language is not enough to explain either outcome. The historian must ask which interpretation a formulation admits, which it forecloses and whether both sides accepted it.

The point is not that American negotiators used some of the same practices and therefore nothing was distinctive about Chinese behavior. Solomon himself distinguished distinctiveness from uniqueness. The question is whether the frequency, combination or institutional function of a practice differed in a way that explains an offer or a result. Showing that both sides invoked principle does not answer that question. Neither does attaching a national adjective to a familiar bargaining act.

Freeman had already begun the update

Freeman’s later essay retained the importance of relationships but changed parts of the causal account. A preference for negotiating at home could reflect budgets, communications and proximity to the people empowered to issue instructions. Letting a foreign counterpart speak first could give an official a safer basis for reporting a proposal and seeking revised authority. These mechanisms could change even if the visible practice continued.36

His account of learning was equally consequential. Research institutes, foreign contacts and international exposure had increased. Participation in multilateral institutions could move from procedural study toward more active engagement. Ministries with specialized portfolios had become more involved. An American executive negotiating with China might itself be mediating demands from Congress, making the formal bilateral table only one part of the bargaining process.37

Freeman also proposed that specialists developed different approaches for different counterparts. That observation challenges a single national style without eliminating continuity. A negotiator’s accumulated experience, the organization represented and the audience addressed could affect how a shared repertoire was used. It suggests a better comparison than placing every encounter in a single before-and-after sequence: examine related issues across counterparts, trace the people and authority involved, and ask whether an approach travels with the specialist or changes with the situation.38

These were participant assessments, not a disclosed new archive proving every proposed mechanism. They nevertheless set the minimum intellectual standard for a modern reassessment. Institutional learning, specialization and adaptation cannot be treated as recent discoveries. What the later chapters must establish is their operation in particular decisions, their limits and their relationship to changes in power and strategic purpose.

The baseline that emerges is demanding. Personal access could help, but authority remained consequential. Political agreement could coexist with disagreement, but the disputed terms had to be identified. Senior intervention could move a proposal, but completion and performance were separate achievements. The next chapter follows these problems into accession negotiations, where the relationship had to be expressed through schedules, exceptions and procedures that many officials and governments would subsequently use.

2. The bargain that came in pieces

At his joint press conference with Bill Clinton on April 8, 1999, Zhu Rongji objected to the idea that he had brought America a gift. China had made concessions in its attempt to join the World Trade Organization, he said, but those concessions served Chinese interests. Competition would put pressure on domestic enterprises and help improve the economy. He turned the complaint into a joke about American campaign contributions. The laughter recorded in the transcript should not obscure the argument: access to China’s market was being exchanged for something its premier believed China needed.39

Zhu and Clinton nevertheless described the remaining distance differently. Clinton announced substantial progress without a completed deal. Zhu portrayed the commercial gap as small and the political atmosphere as the real difficulty. They could agree that the negotiation was valuable while disagreeing about why it had not finished. The public disagreement preceded the retrospective accounts in which American officials allocated responsibility for the missed opportunity.

That distinction is essential to the history. A proposition that China’s offer was good enough to accept differs from a proposition that all its terms had been settled. An American decision not to close could be politically unwise even if the document still contained reservations. Conversely, saying that issues remained open could defend a decision whose decisive cause lay elsewhere. The papers permit a more exact question than whether April produced “a deal”: which commitments had been accepted, which remained conditional, and who could authorize the final combination?

The prize was participation in a trading system whose members negotiated the terms on which China would enter. The American bilateral agreement was a major part of that work, but other members’ demands and the multilateral legal package remained. Washington also faced a domestic decision over permanent normal trade relations: the executive could bargain with China while Congress retained authority over that legislation. Those separate decisions help explain why an offer of market access, a presidential assurance and a completed accession were different things.4041

The April record

Two days after the press conference, Long Yongtu and Peter Scher signed a memorandum of understanding. Its body survives in an official Chinese documentary collection. It records a wide range of agreement on market access, including goods schedules and most services, subject to checking and correction. It also names unfinished business: banking and consumer auto finance, securities, audiovisual services, textiles and apparel, and the duration of provisions governing dumping and product-specific safeguards. The parties would verify their schedules and continue work on the outstanding issues.42

The memorandum therefore preserved both progress and limits. It was not an empty promise to talk. Nor was it an unconditional completion of the whole accession bargain. Its references to attachments matter because the body cannot answer every question about the sectoral commitments it records. The two attachments are not reproduced with the signed page. Without them, the historian cannot turn a government’s public summary into the authenticated April schedule.

There is a second reason to distinguish texts. The American statement published on April 8 and the joint statement of April 10 are not interchangeable simply because each presented progress as joint. The Chinese editorial account disputes the publication of the earlier formulation; the later signed memorandum supplies a firmer basis for identifying what the negotiators formally recorded. The April 10 public statement still called for an agreement on strong commercial terms, even while expressing support for accession in 1999. A commitment to work toward membership did not remove the question of its price.43

The disagreement within the American record is revealing. At an April 8 briefing, Barshefsky defended the remaining commercial issues and rejected the suggestion that publication was intended to mobilize business pressure. Gene Sperling acknowledged differences among advisers and referred to a combined National Economic Council–National Security Council memorandum recording their views. In her later oral history, Barshefsky placed much more emphasis on Washington’s political unwillingness to close. Lawrence Summers also criticized the April episode, but questioned the attempt to obtain public credit for concessions without completing the bargain.44

These accounts identify competing decisions rather than resolve them: whether the package should have been accepted, whether more could be obtained, and whether publishing its contents helped or damaged the effort. The contemporary briefing gives the missing decision paper a name. The later interviews give reasons to seek it. Neither permits the author to narrate an unobserved vote inside the White House.

The public exchange between Zhu and Clinton still establishes something important. Reform and competition were already part of Zhu’s stated rationale. His willingness to discuss their costs does not reveal every Chinese official’s preference, but it prevents interpreting all market opening as a favor extracted from an otherwise indifferent China. Clinton’s case likewise combined commercial gains with an expectation that integration would influence China’s development. Negotiators were seeking a commercial bargain that their leaders valued partly for different political reasons.

Returning to a disputed baseline

The interruption after the bombing of China’s embassy in Belgrade further complicates any simple April-to-November comparison. The Chinese published history records a suspension of accession talks and subsequent efforts to resume them. That suspension should not be enlarged into an assertion that the countries had no communication at all. The American record includes later contacts, and the Chinese account itself describes exchanged messages and technical contact before the announced resumption at Auckland.45

When talks returned, the problem included the status of the earlier papers. The Chinese history describes disputes over the April American list, newer Chinese positions, permanent normal trade relations, safeguards and export controls. It reports Barshefsky treating high-technology export controls as outside her WTO mandate. This is a useful account of contested scope, though the underlying exchanges and authorization records are not all available. It shows why an apparent refusal to repeat an earlier position cannot be classified before establishing whether that position was agreed, provisional or attributed by one side.

The Americans also had to demonstrate that they could complete their part of the process. Summers later described an October mission intended to reassure Zhu about Washington’s readiness. His contemporary speech confirms the visit and meetings, without independently confirming the private assurance he remembered. The distinction leaves the central problem intact: a Chinese negotiator had reason to care not only about the generosity of an American offer but also about whether the people delivering it could sustain it in Washington.46

This is where the personal and institutional explanations meet. An official known to a counterpart can transmit an assurance. Its value still depends on what the official represents. Familiarity with Barshefsky, confidence in Summers or Sperling’s participation could matter through information about presidential backing and domestic coordination. It is not enough to call those encounters friendship and infer a concession. The resulting instruction or response has to be found.

November’s written exchange

By November 12, the surviving papers permit a much closer view. China’s package was detailed and explicitly conditional on completing a final bilateral agreement in the current negotiation. It treated different telecommunications services differently, assigned phase-ins to ownership ceilings, identified banking restrictions and proposed a short duration for special trade-remedy provisions. Its technical specificity was itself part of the bargaining: percentage ownership, location, customer, date and regulatory permission could be adjusted separately.47

The American papers show changes over the following exchange. Both the November 12 paper and the one attributed by its Chinese heading to November 13 retained earlier schedules except where new terms superseded them. An omitted item in the later, shorter list was therefore not automatically abandoned. Some issues were restated; others continued under the earlier paper. A reconstruction that compared only the visible numbered lists would manufacture changes that their retention clauses expressly ruled out.

A government could preserve an ownership ceiling and yield on managerial rights. It could shorten a transition in one sector while retaining protection in another. It could accept a longer safeguard period in exchange for a different commercial package. Establishing the actual exchange requires more than observing that both changes eventually occurred, but the papers identify the separate margins on which a bargain could be made.

The documents establish separate ownership histories. Fifty percent life-insurance equity was already in both sides’ November 12 papers; the inspected American closing papers do not demand 51% life-insurance ownership. Barshefsky’s later recollection of an ownership retreat concerns telecommunications. The insurance chronology does not contradict that telecommunications recollection. A sound comparison must follow each of those provisions through its own sequence, including control and transition dates, before evaluating a participant’s account.49

The banking clocks also changed form. A deadline fixed to January 1, 2002 or 2005 differs from a period measured two or five years after accession. The practical difference grows if membership occurs later than negotiators anticipate. The November 13 offer and the post-agreement summary therefore cannot be harmonized simply because both mention two classes of customers. This is a documented difference in the terms’ public presentation; the bilateral annexes are needed to establish the complete final wording.

The distinction between proposed and announced trade-remedy periods is equally consequential. China’s November 12 proposal was much shorter than the American demands. The American November 13 paper proposed twenty years for the antidumping provision; the post-agreement summary announced fifteen. The twelve-year product safeguard was a different provision. Later disputes over the meaning and expiration of China’s accession commitments cannot be resolved by substituting one of these figures for another. Here the documents establish that the proposed terms moved and that a public account described a different final combination. They do not identify the private conversation that produced it.

Who moved the deadline?

The letters accompanying the offers make the American delegation an active participant in the management of time. On November 12, chargé d’affaires William C. McCahill Jr. described an impasse and requested direct talks between Barshefsky and Zhu. The letter set out the eight o’clock hotel departure and seven o’clock deadline for a reply. It offered an extension if a meeting could occur, but said the delegation could not remain beyond early Sunday morning. On November 14, a further letter again requested Zhu’s intervention and alleged that the Chinese side had reopened settled issues.50

Each element had a purpose in the argument the Americans were making. The announced departure presented a time limit. An offer to postpone demonstrated that the limit could change under a specified condition. Invoking the intentions of leaders framed the remaining obstruction as inconsistent with a shared objective. Accusing the other side of reopening issues sought to establish which concessions belonged in the existing baseline. The documents record these moves directly. Their causal success is a separate question.

The Chinese published chronology places Zhu’s interventions on November 13 and 15. A financial negotiator’s later account describes changes in authority during the same period. The timing is compatible with American pressure helping to force a decision. It is also compatible with a process of internal authorization and technical resolution that a departure threat alone could not replace. To decide between these explanations, the historian would need to see how the instructions or acceptable terms changed in response to the threatened departure. The fact that an agreement followed cannot supply that missing link.

This qualification does not make the letters less valuable. They overturn an account in which the Chinese side alone controlled the clock while the Americans merely endured it. They show the Americans trying to alter both the schedule and the level of decision. They also show why a visible summit intervention is an incomplete diagnostic: it can be sought by the frustrated counterpart, depend on revised instructions, and concern a specific unresolved part of an otherwise elaborate draft.

Solomon called senior intervention the most reliable sign of approaching agreement and connected it to leaders’ reluctance to risk face. A narrower authorization hypothesis is worth investigating here: what did the senior intervention enable or confirm? This is our qualification of his stronger prediction, which already sits beside an aborted claims-settlement example in his book. They do not establish that Chinese officials staged deadlock according to a predetermined script. Nor do they measure the reliability of the signal in negotiations that did not close. A successful episode can demonstrate a sequence without proving a forecasting rule.

What sector authority adds

Deng Hongguo’s account of the financial negotiations offers a specific institutional explanation. Foreign requests moved through the Geneva delegation and MOFTEC to the responsible regulator. Sector officials prepared alternatives and sought approval through their own hierarchy; MOFTEC coordinated the broader package. Deng describes new State Council authorization on November 13 and a separate request to PBOC Governor Dai Xianglong over financial leasing that evening. His account distinguishes ordinary negotiating discretion from concessions that required a higher decision.51

It is retrospective testimony, with apparent date and institutional inconsistencies elsewhere in the chapter. The instructions, if recovered, would be stronger evidence. Its value is the precise mechanism it proposes: technical officials could understand a request while lacking permission to accept it, and the delegation could need both sector approval and coordination across a package.

Auto finance makes the proposed mechanism concrete. The November 12 Chinese offer made foreign nonbank access conditional on corresponding domestic permission. Deng recalls new authority the next day allowing foreign enterprise-group finance companies to offer auto credit on accession. The American November 13 paper then expressly accepts a Chinese auto-finance offer dated that day, and the November 16 summary announces nonbank access on accession. These sources converge on movement from a domestic-permission condition toward accession-linked access. They do not supply the full intervening Chinese offer or the instruction itself, and Deng’s narrower company category should not silently become the summary’s broader description. The change is better established than its complete authorization chain.52

The document sequence is consistent with that explanation without proving all of it. The Chinese proposal specified matters a financial regulator would care about: customer categories, cities, national treatment and the relationship between foreign and domestic permissions. Subsequent American language accepted some changes while pressing others. On Deng’s account, authority was attached to particular choices, not simply to the rank of whoever occupied the most prominent chair. The detailed offers fit that account but do not independently establish the delegation of authority.

This changes how a visitor should interpret rigidity. Repeating an instruction can be a tactic, but it can also be what a delegated official is empowered to do. An appeal to a premier may unlock a decision without demonstrating that a subordinate acted in bad faith. Conversely, a claim of limited authority can itself be useful in bargaining. The decisive evidence is the instruction and its revision, not the outsider’s impression that the official seemed either sincere or obstructive.

The same reasoning applies to the United States. The April episode and the later emphasis on Washington’s readiness show that an American negotiator’s authority could also be uncertain from the other side of the table. A comparison that assumes American offers were always stable would misidentify some Chinese caution as an unexplained national habit. The two governments had different systems of decision, but both faced the problem of making an external commitment credible within them.

The signature and the bargain beyond it

On November 15, Shi Guangsheng and Barshefsky signed the agreement on market access. The Chinese and English bodies reproduced in the collection say that commitments on industrial goods, agriculture and services were set out in Annexes 1–3; provisions applying rules to access to each other’s markets appeared in Annex 4. Both bodies direct the governments to notify the WTO director-general and send the agreement, including its annexes, to the secretariat for incorporation into the multilateral aspects of China’s accession negotiations.53

This is a different documentary status from the conditional November 12 package. It records that the governments had reached agreement. Yet it is still not a declaration that China had become a WTO member. The signed instrument itself points to the next process. Its reference to rules concerning both markets identifies the scope of the instrument, not the distribution of new concessions. The body establishes the reciprocal formulation; the full allocation of obligations lies in the annexes.

The WTO’s announcement that day was explicit about the unfinished work. Director-General Mike Moore welcomed the bilateral deal while identifying remaining agreements with other governments and technical protocol negotiations. China would attend the forthcoming Seattle ministerial as an observer. His statement supplies an institutional perspective distinct from either delegation’s account of victory.54

The American domestic process remained separate as well. Briefing reporters in Ankara, National Security Adviser Sandy Berger said the administration would seek permanent normal trade relations from Congress. He distinguished that legislation from approval of accession itself, and explained that China understood Congress to be an independent branch. The administration could promise an effort; it could not simply announce the congressional result as a completed part of the November bargain.55

Berger also identified what he believed had improved since April: trade remedies, motion pictures, Internet access, satellites, auto financing, securities and capital-market dialogue. Asked what China had gained, he declined to give a balancing account and left the Chinese side to speak for itself. His statement is useful evidence of the administration’s case for its decision, including its expectations that competition would reinforce internal reform. It is not an impartial measure of the value each government obtained.

The record thus returns us to Zhu’s rejection of the gift metaphor. Both governments could value a bargain that imposed visible costs on China. They could also disagree about the remaining price, the meaning of an earlier document, or their counterpart’s ability to follow through. Political expectations helped make an accession agreement attractive; they did not settle each clause or guarantee the long-run development each side hoped to encourage.

Europe’s unfinished business

The American agreement gave Europe something to build on. In the Chinese documentary history’s account of the January 24–26, 2000 talks, European chief negotiator Hans-Friedrich Beseler invoked most-favored-nation treatment and proposed continuing on the basis of the US bargain. He also explained why that bargain was insufficient. The European Union had fifteen member states with separate interests; satisfying their priorities required a balance of its own. Long Yongtu replied that the American package already included concessions of interest to Europe, and that Chinese leaders had compensated for protecting sensitive sectors by conceding more elsewhere.56

The same earlier agreement was doing different work in the two arguments. For Beseler, it established a basis for comparable treatment and further gains. For Long, it helped explain why some demands approached decisions China would not readily reopen. Neither position required denying that a previous bargain existed. A later Chinese participant’s shorthand that Europe would not use the American agreement as its basis misses this distinction between accepting a starting point and accepting it as sufficient.

The European comparison is therefore unusually close without being a controlled experiment. It involves the same Chinese leadership and accession campaign, but Europe bargained later, with different commercial priorities and an existing American package. Moreover, the Commission had to negotiate a position it could sustain among member states. A different result could reflect any of these differences. It cannot simply measure which foreign negotiators understood China better.

One surviving paper makes the remaining work tangible. A European technical package dated February 23 proposed opening Zhuhai to renminbi banking on accession and dropping a request to advance Beijing’s opening. Its services page separated items intended for resolution by chief negotiators from unresolved telecommunications and insurance questions. The handwriting and checked lines do not identify a Chinese acceptance. The paper is evidence of a proposed package, including the effort to settle which issues belonged at which level.57

The eventual WTO schedule committed China to opening Zhuhai within one year after accession. This is a recoverable request-to-outcome comparison: immediate opening was requested; a one-year timetable was ultimately bound. Deng Hongguo’s later account adds that the earlier benchmark was three years, that Portugal wanted the change, and that he obtained permission from PBOC leaders. The final one-year term is independently documented; the starting three-year benchmark and the approval chain still depend on his recollection. The Commission also credited Europe with advancing Zhuhai’s date. These records support a specific geographic concession, while leaving its complete internal history to be established.58

A premier’s meeting that did not close the deal

Lamy’s March visit supplies the unsuccessful comparison missing from a story told only through November’s American success. Zhu met the European commissioner, detailed packages were exchanged, and no agreement followed. According to the Chinese history, Lamy explained that his authorization did not permit acceptance of the Chinese ownership ceilings in life insurance and automobiles. He would need to consult at home. The European side had an approval problem too.59

By May, the same account reports, Lamy had authority to accept the 50 percent life-insurance ceiling if compensated elsewhere in insurance. The automobile ceiling could also be accepted with improvements in tariff treatment. Mobile telecommunications remained unresolved. Its final ownership percentage, initial percentage and transition period offered separate ways of changing the bargain. A Chinese refusal to lift one ceiling did not mean every commercially important term was fixed.

The account of May 17–19 is particularly revealing. Lamy said he had understood Zhu’s March remarks to permit a 50 percent mobile ceiling, and had reported that understanding to European ministers. Accepting 49 percent exceeded his authority. On May 18 he sought a signal from a Chinese leader confirming that limit, which he could report to Brussels to obtain fresh authority. Meanwhile, disputes continued over initial mobile ownership, retail chains and insurance licenses. Shi Guangsheng agreed to consult other departments, while warning that an improved offer was unlikely. The following morning he reported that the coordination had not succeeded in improving it.60

At his May 19 meeting with Lamy, Zhu described 49 percent as a collective Chinese government decision. Lamy reported consultations with member states and industry and said that, with Zhu’s confirmation, Europe would respect the limit. He sought approval of compensating terms in telecommunications, insurance and distribution. Zhu accepted their principle, leaving the particulars to the negotiators. Agreement followed that afternoon.

Here senior intervention did more than display a willingness to settle. The counterpart had explicitly requested authoritative confirmation of a limit. Such confirmation could help a negotiator justify accepting a package to the institutions that controlled his mandate. That is a plausible mechanism in this sequence, not an independently recovered Brussels decision: the published Chinese account does not establish precisely when European authorization changed or whether Zhu’s meeting caused the change. It does establish why treating every senior meeting as the release of a new Chinese concession would misread what was being requested.

Nor does Shi’s unsuccessful coordination establish that Chinese leaders disagreed with one another. It records a failed attempt to improve an offer. A decision to preserve an agreed limit, a disagreement over compensation and a divided leadership could each produce outward rigidity. The observed refusal alone cannot choose among them. March and May instead direct attention to the content of authority on both sides: a leader could be present while a bargain remained unapproved, and confirmation of a constraint could contribute to agreement without removing it.

What the European bargain bought

The final mobile schedule retained 49 percent, reached within three years after accession, with 25 percent at accession and 35 percent within a year. Geographic restrictions followed a separate five-year timetable. Those are distinct commitments, not rival descriptions of one transition. The Commission characterized the equity phase-in as an improvement on the US agreement. The November American public summary confirms the ceiling without reproducing its complete equity timetable, so the missing US annex remains necessary for a fully authenticated bilateral comparison.61

Distribution illustrates another danger in describing a concession too broadly. The Commission’s account said the restriction on large foreign-owned chains had been lifted. The final schedule retained a restriction on foreign majority ownership for certain chains with more than thirty outlets selling specified products of different types and brands from multiple suppliers. The change could have substantial commercial value while stopping short of unrestricted foreign ownership of every large chain. The definition of the remaining exception was part of the bargain.62

Some European benefits also sat outside the accession terms. The Commission recorded seven new insurance licenses—five life and two non-life—and two sub-branch licenses to be granted before accession. Its October 2000 memorandum was still seeking timing for five further new licenses and two sub-branches. The counts concern different stages and categories. They identify promised administrative acts whose completion must be traced separately from the signature and the WTO schedule.63

That October memorandum also described continued multilateral bargaining over how China would give effect to its commitments. Existing members saw clarification as necessary; the Commission reported China accusing them of seeking obligations beyond those normally required of an acceding country. That disagreement concerned the appropriate burden of accession. The memorandum alone does not establish a dispute over the meaning of an already settled bilateral clause. The transition from bilateral agreement to multilateral rules was itself a negotiation.

The American and European records thus establish a demanding coexistence. Detailed written bargaining, sector expertise, personal assurance and senior authority were already present together at accession. Officials on both sides invoked prior agreements and domestic constraints. The Chinese delegation defended important ownership limits while bargaining over timing, geography, control and administrative permission. The result was neither a single concession nor a single moment when politics gave way to implementation.

For the later history, the question is how this combination developed. Solomon’s attention to authority and bargaining after agreement remains productive, provided those processes are reconstructed rather than inferred from a negotiating atmosphere. Institutional expertise and technical precision cannot simply be announced as discoveries of the post-accession period. What changed must be shown in the choices officials could make, the instruments they used, and the commitments their counterparts could actually obtain.

3. What expertise makes possible

In May 2012, Chinese ambassador Yi Xiaozhun and American ambassador Michael Punke signed an agreement about how their governments would continue to disagree. China had challenged American trade duties and obtained important findings in its favor at the World Trade Organization. The next dispute might concern whether the United States had complied. The ambassadors’ agreement specified how China could seek that review, when the United States would accept a panel, how a later request to suspend trade concessions would be handled and which American rights would remain protected.64

This was a small instrument beside the political declarations that dominate accounts of US–China diplomacy. It is particularly useful for understanding change. China was accepting precise procedures governing a dispute with a major power. Agreement on those procedures did not require agreement on whether American conduct was lawful. Legal detail provided a way to preserve and manage disagreement.

The episode also exposes the limits of a familiar account of institutional learning. China’s use of sophisticated arguments is observable. Where the expertise resided, how it was acquired and which internal decision it changed are different questions. Officials, outside counsel, firms and ministries could contribute to a legal position. A successful argument does not reveal their respective roles. Nor does success before a tribunal automatically translate into reduced payments at the border.

The climate negotiations posed a related problem in a different institution. The countries were negotiating new arrangements rather than litigating duties under existing rules. Between Copenhagen in 2009 and Paris in 2015, they combined nationally different mitigation intentions with more specified international procedures. Here too, precision could coexist with discretion. The question was which conduct would be subject to which rule, and who would have authority to judge it.

These sequences establish what active institutional participation made available: arguments that an adjudicator had to consider, methods that an administrative authority could accept or reject, and procedures through which future disagreement would be handled. They also show why expertise was useful without being sufficient.

From an administrative objection to an international ruling

The WTO dispute known as DS379 concerned American anti-dumping and countervailing duties on four groups of Chinese products: two categories of steel pipe, laminated woven sacks and certain off-the-road tyres. The United States had begun applying countervailing duties to imports from China while continuing to calculate anti-dumping duties using its methodology for a non-market economy. China challenged aspects of that combination and the determinations supporting the duties.65

The two duties address different problems. Anti-dumping calculations compare an export price with a measure of normal value. Countervailing duties offset qualifying subsidies. When the normal-value calculation substitutes values from outside China while the actual export price reflects a Chinese subsidy, the anti-dumping calculation can already offset some of the subsidy’s effect. Adding a countervailing duty against the same subsidization can then duplicate the remedy. Whether that occurred, and what the investigating authority had to do about it, became central issues in the dispute.

Another issue concerned public bodies. A government’s financial contribution can qualify for subsidy treatment, but the relevant rules also cover contributions by public bodies. China contested the American treatment of state-owned suppliers and banks under that category. The question was not simply whether the enterprise had a private-law form or whether the state owned its shares. It was what made an entity governmental for the purpose of the subsidy rules.

China’s April 2009 submission summary shows a delegation engaging the legal structure of the argument. It invoked treaty-interpretation rules and principles of attribution, distinguished ownership from governmental authority and addressed the separate possibility that a government could entrust or direct a private body. These were arguments about how the agreed rules applied to domestic institutions. The published summary is direct evidence of China’s position, although it is neither the complete brief nor a record identifying the originator of each argument.66

The institutional process imposed constraints as well as opportunities. China lost a general challenge to the alleged absence of American legal authority to avoid double remedies because the panel found that this claim had not been identified in the consultation request and materially expanded the dispute. China argued against that jurisdictional objection and failed. The result demonstrates that invocation of legal precedent did not guarantee success; it does not, without more, establish either incompetence or a deliberate attempt to evade the process.67

China also appealed adverse findings on particular American investigations. In March 2011 the Appellate Body delivered important victories, but not a single undifferentiated judgment that China was right and the duties must disappear. Its distinctions are central to what this case can tell us about negotiating competence.

Winning an interpretation is not winning every application

On public bodies, the Appellate Body required an entity to possess, exercise or be vested with governmental authority. Government ownership alone was insufficient. Evidence of governmental functions and meaningful government control over conduct could still be relevant; the ruling did not require an express statutory delegation in every case. The route involving government entrustment or direction of private bodies also remained available.68

That interpretation produced different results for suppliers and banks. The determinations concerning the input suppliers had relied on ownership and were found inconsistent with the subsidy agreement. The bank determination rested on a broader record involving law, industrial policy and lending and governance evidence. China did not establish the same inconsistency there. The ruling therefore cannot be summarized as declaring Chinese state-owned enterprises private, exempt from subsidy rules or outside governmental influence.69

The record also reveals technical engagement before the WTO proceeding reached its appellate stage. China had urged the American investigating authority to examine more than ownership, proposing a multifactor inquiry into the entity’s functions and control. In a related benchmark dispute, China proposed an inflation adjustment and an alternative based on savings rates. Commerce accepted the inflation adjustment and rejected other suggestions. Those benchmark arguments originated in an earlier investigation and entered the tyre record; they were not invented for the appeal.70

This is a firmer example of expertise affecting an administrative choice than simply counting trained officials or observing an eventual legal victory. A proposal was made and one adjustment was accepted. The limit is equally clear. The Appellate Body could not complete the assessment of the benchmark’s substantive legality on the available findings. Acceptance of one technical suggestion cannot be enlarged into success on the entire benchmark claim, much less proof of a general learning trajectory.

The double-remedy ruling was similarly differentiated. The Appellate Body interpreted the requirement for appropriate countervailing-duty amounts in SCM Article 19.3 to prohibit offsetting the same subsidization twice through the concurrent duties in question. It rejected the panel’s contrary interpretation. But it did not accept China’s claim that reversal of the interpretation automatically established the violation it alleged. Duplication was considered likely under the methodology, not inevitable in every instance. The decisive failure was the American authority’s failure to investigate whether, and to what extent, duplication occurred. The tribunal did not calculate a correct duty for every exporter or prohibit all concurrent duties.71

The United States objected at adoption to both the public-body reasoning and the double-remedy analysis. Those objections did not displace the adopted ruling. They matter because use of a common institution had produced an authoritative outcome without producing a shared interpretation of the underlying rules. Institutional participation did not require intellectual convergence.72

The scholarly debate also remained open. Thomas Prusa and Edwin Vermulst argued that the particular American methodology warranted a stronger conclusion about duplication, while distinguishing it from broader empirical difficulties in measuring subsidy effects. They also observed that a new public-body determination might survive if supported by better evidence. Their law-and-economics analysis helps clarify the ruling’s implications; it is not testimony about what either delegation privately intended.73

The useful historical finding is thus more precise than China learned to win cases. China’s delegation used administrative objections, legal interpretation and appellate review to challenge a powerful counterpart. Some arguments altered the applicable interpretation or an administrative method; others were rejected or left unresolved. The rules created several points at which knowledge could matter, while requiring that it survive institutional tests.

Bargaining over the next disagreement

Implementation brought the parties back to negotiation. They agreed on an eleven-month period for American compliance, ending in February 2012, and then extended it to April. The May agreement laid out the procedure for a possible compliance dispute. China would not have to repeat consultations before requesting a compliance panel. If it chose consultations, the governments would meet within fifteen days. The United States would accept panel establishment at the first relevant meeting of the WTO’s Dispute Settlement Body.74

The agreement then specified contingencies. If a later adopted compliance ruling found no complying measure or an inconsistency, China could seek permission to suspend concessions. The United States would not invoke the usual thirty-day timing objection against that request, while retaining arbitration rights over the proposed suspension and relevant procedural principles. The governments also addressed deadlines, unavailable adjudicators and further procedural gaps. These were concrete undertakings governing how a possible future disagreement would be heard.

They were not an immediate license for retaliation. Nor does the final document reveal who proposed each clause, how the draft changed or what the ambassadors were instructed to accept. Its evidentiary value is nonetheless substantial. It is a jointly published, signed procedural bargain, rather than one side’s account of a productive conversation. It demonstrates that China could accept specificity precisely where specificity preserved a means of challenging the counterpart.

This is a useful qualification of Solomon’s discussion of flexible political documents. The May instrument does not prove that Chinese officials ceased to value broad principles in high-level diplomacy. It shows that a preference inferred from that setting cannot simply be extended to every form of Chinese external bargaining. Here, detailed procedure and preserved discretion were compatible. The parties knew how to continue disagreeing without first resolving every dispute about the rules.

American implementation then illustrated why a legal win is not a commercial outcome. The August notice recorded new determinations and instructions with defined prospective application. Duties did not uniformly disappear or decline. For example, its countervailing-duty table showed Zibo Aifudi’s rate rising from 29.54 percent in the investigation to 83.34 percent in the revised determination. The notice itself does not establish why that increase occurred; the underlying redetermination files are needed for that explanation.75

The adjacent anti-dumping table requires a separate caution. Its earlier column reports a weighted-average dumping margin, while its later column reports a revised cash-deposit rate. Treating those figures as the tariff actually paid immediately before and after implementation would compare unlike measures. Intervening administrative reviews could have changed the applicable rate, and the notice specified which entries the new determinations covered. To estimate relief, one would need those instructions, entry dates and values, and eventual assessments.

At the WTO, the United States asserted full compliance and China disagreed. The examined case docket contains no later DS379 compliance ruling deciding that objection. The published procedural agreement created a route; it did not prove that the route was used or that the disagreement was adjudicated. The sequence therefore supports an account of legal agency and negotiated implementation procedures, with the extent of commercial benefit and final compliance left unresolved.76

Copenhagen: define the refusal before explaining it

Climate negotiations required a different use of expertise. At Copenhagen in December 2009, the parties were trying to construct an arrangement about future national action and international scrutiny. A Chinese refusal on one dimension did not necessarily extend to every part of the package.

The official Chinese account of the summit describes Wen Jiabao treating China’s mitigation target as not open to negotiation while allowing discussion of transparency. It also describes coordination with Brazil, South Africa and India and disputes about attendance at smaller meetings. This is a government account defending Chinese conduct, not an authenticated set of instructions. Even on its own terms, however, it distinguishes the national target from the information to be supplied about action toward it.77

Obama’s contemporaneous press conference described an agreement among major economies combining recorded action with international consultation and analysis. He acknowledged that it was insufficient and not legally binding. His team was still completing its work. The conference’s eventual decision took note of the Copenhagen Accord; it did not adopt the accord as a consensual agreement of the whole conference. Leaders could help construct a package without supplying the assent of every other participant.78

The accord’s scrutiny provisions were also differentiated. Developing-country actions would be measured, reported and verified domestically, with information on implementation subject to international consultation and analysis. Actions receiving international support would face international measurement, reporting and verification. The distinction was not external scrutiny versus none. It concerned which scrutiny applied to which action, under what conditions.

Chinese expert He Jiankun publicly described acceptance of consultation and analysis as a flexible adjustment while resisting equivalent international verification. His essay did not disclose the decisive internal instruction, but its distinction can be checked against the agreed words. It also identified further guidelines as unfinished work. A national position on sovereignty could therefore coexist with negotiation over a bounded external procedure.79

Björn Conrad’s interview-based study offers an institutional explanation of the difficulties. He argues that Wen’s foreign-policy team displaced climate specialists near the summit’s end and prioritized protection of the premier and China’s diplomatic position. The interviews give that hypothesis a basis beyond the public record, but the underlying transcripts and mandates are unavailable here. The account does not establish a struggle within China’s supreme leadership, nor rule out substantive concern about how a global target would distribute the remaining burden.80

That distinction connects the case to Solomon’s diagnosis of rigidity. A specialist who understands a possible compromise may not have authority to offer it. A senior team may evaluate the same proposal against diplomatic or political concerns the specialist weights differently. Neither situation can be identified simply by hearing a firm public position. Expertise may improve the available proposal while failing to control the decision.

The work between the summits

The subsequent record makes institutional development visible without assigning all of it to a change of leader. At Cancun in 2010, the parties specified consultation and analysis for developing-country reports, including technical work conducted in consultation with the country and a facilitative exchange of views. They bounded that process: it was non-punitive, respectful of sovereignty and excluded discussion of the appropriateness of domestic policies. The following year’s Durban mandate called for an outcome with legal force applicable to all parties.81

These steps preceded Xi Jinping’s leadership period. Any explanation locating the origin of China’s willingness to negotiate the Paris architecture exclusively in Xi’s personal preferences would need to account for them. That chronology does not resolve the motives behind Chinese acceptance at Cancun or Durban. It identifies institutional commitments already in place before the later bilateral announcements.

In 2013 the US–China Climate Change Working Group gave technical cooperation a more defined structure. Its work included specific initiatives, responsible agencies and reporting arrangements under the Strategic and Economic Dialogue. The subjects ranged from vehicle emissions and electricity grids to emissions-data management. A related initiative sought to use Montreal Protocol institutions to address hydrofluorocarbon production and consumption while retaining emissions accounting within the climate framework.82

Those arrangements could supply information about feasibility, cost and implementation. They also gave officials recurring channels through which to discuss multilateral negotiation and domestic policy. But the announcement of a working group is not evidence that its projects operated, or that the information it produced changed a leader’s acceptable target. The missing link is the report or proposal that reached a decision maker and affected an instruction.

Todd Stern’s retrospective account helps identify that link as a research question. He describes establishing his standing as the American envoy, building a relationship with Xie Zhenhua and developing confidential exchanges toward the 2014 announcement. This is specialist diplomacy with a personal dimension. Its value would lie partly in knowing what the counterpart could represent and obtain from his own government. It need not involve the pressure on a valued friend that Solomon emphasized.83

The November 2014 statement paired different national intentions. The United States intended an economy-wide greenhouse-gas reduction of 26–28 percent below 2005 levels by 2025, with best efforts toward the upper end. China intended carbon dioxide emissions to peak around 2030, with best efforts to peak earlier, and non-fossil energy to reach about 20 percent of primary energy consumption by 2030. These were not identical targets or directly comparable reductions. Both governments nevertheless endorsed the package and its contribution to the prospective Paris outcome.84

Stern describes joint announcement as conditional on mutual comfort with the other government’s ambition. Without successive drafts, that remains a participant account of reciprocal endorsement rather than a demonstrated exchange of specified increments. His recollection also folds later target components into 2014. The dated statements establish the actual terms; the interview supplies leads about how they were reached.

The bilateral announcement did not settle the multilateral negotiation. At Lima, the national-circumstances formulation appeared in the decision, while the legal nature and content of intended national contributions remained open. Contemporary conference reporting warned that encouraging bilateral signals had not immediately transformed negotiating positions. Other governments had their own interests and authority; they were not simply ratifying a US–China understanding.85

A treaty that specified where discretion remained

By September 2015, the bilateral statement was more explicit about an enhanced transparency system for action and support, with flexibility for developing countries needing it in light of their capacities. Capacity and need qualified the flexibility. The statement did not express a blanket exemption for every developing country, nor did it make all national responsibilities identical.86

Negotiation continued at Paris. China opposed proposals it feared could become advance review of national contributions; the United States and European Union described the proposed dialogue differently. Consultations led by other countries worked through the distribution of obligations across mitigation, transparency and finance. Bilateral cooperation thus coexisted with Chinese resistance to particular provisions, within a wider process neither Beijing nor Washington controlled alone.87

The adopted treaty preserved national determination of contributions while requiring parties to prepare, communicate and maintain successive contributions and pursue domestic measures aimed at their objectives. It created reporting and review requirements with capacity-related flexibility, and a facilitative, non-punitive compliance mechanism. It did not impose an internationally binding duty to achieve each announced national result. Differentiated finance obligations remained.88

This architecture cannot be described adequately as either legal obligation or discretion. It combined both, on specified questions. A reporting requirement can bind even where achievement of a particular national result is not internationally binding. A common procedure can contain differentiated application. Acceptance of that structure does not establish that officials abandoned concern for sovereignty or became indifferent to national economic costs. The form of the instrument alone cannot decide their preferences.

The same distinction limits the claim about success. Paris text adoption on December 12, 2015 was an important institutional result. It was not yet each state’s consent to be bound, and it was not evidence that the promised national actions would occur. This chapter ends at adoption. Later implementation belongs to a separate history, just as the WTO ruling and the tariff actually paid belong to different stages of the trade dispute.

The change that can be established

Solomon’s original period already contained technical detail, legal concerns and multiple organizations. Freeman had already identified learning, specialist participation and approaches adapted to different counterparts. The subsequent record does not justify a discovery narrative in which China suddenly acquired expertise after accession or ceased to negotiate politically.

It does support a more specific change in available instruments and their use. In DS379, a Chinese legal argument could require an external adjudicator to answer a question the American administration had rejected. The resulting ruling became the basis for further implementation work and a negotiated procedural agreement. In climate diplomacy, progressively specified review arrangements could accompany national discretion over ambition. An agreement’s precision could help preserve the boundary of what had and had not been accepted.

These settings put the relationship between expertise and authority in different forms. A tribunal could assess a legal argument under existing rules. Climate specialists worked inside a negotiation in which the rules themselves, and the distribution of responsibilities, were being decided. In both settings, knowledge mattered through a particular process. It could improve an argument, expose a measurement problem or specify a tolerable review procedure. It did not guarantee political approval, another government’s assent or eventual performance.

The strongest conclusion therefore concerns demonstrated capability and institutional opportunity. China could contest administrative methods, secure favorable rulings and an accepted methodological adjustment, and negotiate detailed arrangements with a more powerful counterpart while preserving substantive disagreement. The extent to which this reflected learning, new resources, specialist counsel or changed stakes requires evidence beyond the final document. The distinction keeps the update from substituting a new stereotype of inexorable sophistication for the old stereotype of immutable style.

4. Negotiating in a crisis

On April 11, 2001, the Chinese government accepted an American ambassador’s letter and authorized the departure of twenty-four American aircrew. It did not accept the American account of the collision that had brought their damaged reconnaissance aircraft to Hainan. Nor did Washington accept China’s demand to stop reconnaissance. The letter left the aircraft’s return to a further discussion and acknowledged that China intended to raise the flights. A crisis had produced an arrangement whose immediate value depended on separating decisions that the original demands had joined together.89

Twenty-two years later, the presence of a Chinese balloon over the United States produced another confrontation involving disputed responsibility, sovereignty and surveillance. This time, no detained crew supplied the central object of a bargain. Instead, the availability of official communication became one of the disputed objects. Diplomatic contact continued while particular military contacts remained unavailable. A meeting with Xi Jinping in June did not deliver the military-channel agreement Washington sought; the November summit identified specific mechanisms to restore. The two episodes ask different questions about what an agreement can accomplish before the underlying dispute is resolved.

Solomon explicitly limited the transfer of conclusions drawn from building a cooperative relationship to bargaining under confrontation.90 Taking that qualification seriously requires more than finding familiar words about respect or friendship in a crisis. It requires reconstructing the actions each government sought, the conditions attached to them and the authority behind an eventual arrangement. These cases show why trust, access, communication and agreement cannot be treated as successive steps on one automatic path. Governments may communicate to repeat incompatible demands. They may authorize one action while reserving another. They may cultivate a private intermediary while declining the official contact the other government wants.

A letter that did several different jobs

The April 1 collision between an American EP-3 and a Chinese fighter created multiple issues at once. The Chinese pilot was lost; the damaged American aircraft landed in China; the crew and plane remained there. Responsibility for the collision, permission for the landing, aircraft immunity, reconnaissance practices, return of the people and return of the machine did not have to receive the same answer. Initially, however, the public demands made them look like parts of a single confrontation.91

On April 3, China’s Foreign Ministry called for acceptance of responsibility, an explanation, an apology and prevention of repetition. Its statement advanced a specific account of the aircraft’s movements and legal arguments about reconnaissance, entry and immunity. President George W. Bush demanded the return of both the crew and aircraft and warned of damage to the wider relationship. Neither side confined its argument to the emotional meaning of the incident. Both attached practical demands to an account of responsibility.

A contemporaneous American legal packet shows how conciliatory language could coexist with the defense of those positions. On April 6, acting Defense General Counsel Daniel Dell’Orto sent guidance to the defense secretary and deputy secretary. The cover memorandum described work by Defense and Joint Chiefs legal offices in consultation with State’s Legal Adviser. The enclosed talking points expressed regret for the Chinese loss while defending the flight, emergency landing and immunity. Their attribution of responsibility was partly conditional on the reported conduct of the Chinese fighter.92

The packet also makes one piece of interagency coordination visible. A clearance page recorded agreement by State, Defense, the Joint Chiefs and the relevant regional office on aircraft-immunity guidance. Another passage specified an oral, off-camera response if pressed. Officials were deciding not only which legal position to maintain, but where and how to state it. Sympathy and legal concession were separate choices within the American apparatus before the final letter was delivered.

This is evidence of preparation, not the missing sequence of negotiated drafts. The packet still demanded immediate aircraft release. Its separate legal sections on people and aircraft do not establish that its authors proposed releasing the crew first. To move from the packet to the final arrangement, the historian still needs to distinguish a legal defense from a bargaining instruction and a later accepted instrument.

The released April 11 letter, from Ambassador Joseph Prueher to Foreign Minister Tang Jiaxuan, performed several jobs. It conveyed sorrow for the Chinese loss. It expressed regret about the absence of verbal clearance for entry and landing, while describing a severely damaged aircraft following emergency procedures and acknowledging uncertainty about the full incident. It recorded agreement to meet, stated the American expectation that the crew would leave promptly, set April 18 for further talks and identified subjects for those talks.93

The wording of the agenda was consequential. Developing a plan to return the aircraft was a future task, not a declaration that return had been completed. Acknowledging China’s intention to raise reconnaissance was not an American promise to end it. Tang’s acceptance announcement supplied the corresponding immediate action: China would allow departure after necessary formalities while maintaining that the incident was unfinished. The arrangement could be acted upon because it identified an action that did not require either side publicly to accept the other’s full account.

The Chinese presentation added another distinction. The Foreign Ministry described a letter of apology and reproduced selected expressions as 深表歉意. But this was a report selecting passages from the letter, not a complete Chinese counterpart to the entire English release. The missing emergency context therefore cannot simply be called a mistranslation of a full parallel text. Selection and interpretation were doing work alongside translation.94

Chinese journalists recognized the issue. At Sun Yuxi’s April 11 briefing, a question contrasted China’s demand for 道歉 with 深表歉意 and asked whether China had compromised. Sun emphasized the official character of the letter, a humanitarian decision and continuing negotiations. The public record thus contains both the government’s presentation of an acceptable American statement and a question challenging whether it satisfied the original demand. China’s audience did not receive only one uncontested interpretation.

This supports a limited account of politically acceptable presentation. It does not prove that a particular translation caused release. The leaderships might already have decided to end a damaging detention dispute and then accepted language they could defend; alternatively, a change in language or package might have made authorization possible. The observed instrument identifies the compromise. The order of the private decisions would distinguish those explanations.

Authority and the evidence of institutional friction

The American ambassador signed the letter, but the published chronology places approval above him. On April 11, White House spokesman Ari Fleischer said Bush had approved everything in it. He described Prueher delivering a signed copy and receiving verbal assurances of release early that morning in Washington time. The Chinese delivery report’s Beijing time is consistent with that sequence. These are contemporary public accounts of authorization, not the original presidential clearance message.95

Prueher’s later interview gives the process more texture. He described rapid approvals from Colin Powell, advice from Kenneth Lieberthal to move beyond the dispute over fault, and additional Chinese channels. Yet his remembered wording about China not hearing a landing request differs from the released letter’s reference to absent verbal clearance. The difference illustrates why a participant’s recollection is valuable for locating decisions and witnesses, while the delivered text remains the better evidence for the words the government accepted.96

There is a further complication for a study built around Solomon. Prueher explicitly recalled studying Solomon’s work. His interpretation is therefore not an entirely independent confirmation of that framework: the framework helped the participant understand and perhaps conduct the negotiation. That possibility makes the interview more interesting, but changes what it can prove. Recollection organized through a theory cannot then be counted without qualification as an unprompted observation confirming it.

Tyler Jost’s institutional explanation asks a different question: could Chinese military and diplomatic information be brought together and challenged before a decision? His EP-3 analysis attributes early miscalculation to separated channels and limited scrutiny. The acquired article and appendix supply a serious hypothesis, but not all the underlying cables, coded interviews or intelligence documents. A cited April 17 CIA assessment cannot explain the April 11 decision without establishing when the underlying information reached those who authorized release.97

Three explanations remain distinguishable in principle. An information failure would require evidence about what reached the leadership and whether a corrected assessment changed its decision. Deliberate bargaining pressure would require evidence that officials understood the limits of their claims but withheld the crew to secure a particular concession. Concern for the relationship would require a decision connecting the costs of continued detention to acceptance of a limited settlement. A forceful public demand is compatible with all three. It cannot select the cause by itself.

The American legal packet provides a useful counterpart discipline. We can demonstrate coordination of a particular piece of guidance because we have its clearance record. We cannot infer either universal American coherence or universal Chinese incoherence from that fact. Institutional explanations become strongest when they trace a piece of information, an objection or an instruction through identifiable offices to a decision. Simply giving the bureaucracy a place in the story is not yet an explanation of the outcome.

The aircraft followed a different clock

After the crew arrangement, China continued to press its account of responsibility and its objections to reconnaissance in the April 18 talks. Those demands show the limited scope of the earlier settlement. Their repetition did not itself repudiate a letter that had explicitly reserved further discussion.98

Aircraft return then moved through several states. On April 29, China announced that it had completed evidence collection and would permit American personnel to inspect the plane. On May 29, it announced agreement in principle to remove the disassembled aircraft aboard a commercial An-124. Technical arrangements remained. Permission to inspect, agreement on a transport method and the completion of transport were distinct events.

The Pentagon’s June 7 briefing gives part of the delay a concrete explanation. Runway load assessment, loading and flight requirements, technicians, equipment and local support all needed attention. Rear Admiral Craig Quigley estimated that component removal was still weeks away and reported no communications hindrance under the arrangements his team had reached. On July 3, the Chinese announcement described completed disassembly and removal, while payment negotiations remained open.99

Solomon’s attention to time and urgency remains pertinent: the crew’s detention plainly gave the Americans a pressing objective. But the later aircraft timetable cannot be read simply as a national tactic of exhausting the other side. Here there were recoverable engineering tasks and an explicit American statement about cooperation on the working arrangements. Those observations do not exclude bargaining pressure elsewhere. They prevent elapsed time from serving as sufficient proof of it.

The case also places a limit on the proposition that China keeps negotiating after an agreement. It certainly did, as did the United States. But deciding an expressly deferred transport method is different from reopening an accepted promise. An account of implementation bargaining must first identify the obligation being implemented. Otherwise every later conversation becomes apparent evidence of revision, even when the original document authorized it.

In 2023, the channel became part of the dispute

The balloon episode began against an already damaged institutional background. In August 2022, after Nancy Pelosi’s visit to Taiwan, the Chinese Foreign Ministry announced countermeasures cancelling arrangements for theater-commander calls, Defense Policy Coordination Talks and Military Maritime Consultative Agreement meetings. It also suspended cooperation in five other areas, including counternarcotics and climate. These were identified mechanisms, not the severance of all diplomatic relations.100

That baseline matters. Military channels were not all lost in a single February 2023 rupture, and their later restoration cannot be described simply as undoing the balloon incident. China had already publicly made some mechanisms contingent on American political conduct. An institution designed to manage danger could itself be withheld as a response to an objectionable act.

On February 3, Beijing acknowledged that the airship was Chinese and expressed regret. It described a civilian research craft, limited in its ability to steer, that winds had taken off course. Its explanation invoked force majeure and unintended entry. It did not concede a surveillance mission. In Washington’s account of Blinken’s call with Wang Yi that day, the secretary acknowledged the regret but characterized the presence as an irresponsible violation that undermined his planned visit. He postponed travel while affirming the intention to engage when conditions allowed.101

The American postponement is a necessary counterpart observation. Washington too could make a particular encounter conditional on political circumstances. The important difference was which encounters each side sought to insulate from the deterioration. The United States wanted military contact during a crisis even when a diplomatic visit had become untenable. Beijing publicly linked acceptance of a ministerial military call to the atmosphere the American response had created.

American aircraft destroyed the balloon on February 4. Austin’s statement characterized it as a surveillance platform and explained the delay in shooting it down by danger to people on the ground. China’s response protested the use of force, repeated the civilian and accidental-entry account and reserved further action. These were incompatible public justifications, not a mutually accepted description of an incident awaiting only a suitable apology.102

The military refusal is directly acknowledged by China. On February 9, Defense Ministry spokesman Tan Kefei said the American use of force had failed to create the proper atmosphere for dialogue, and China had therefore not accepted the proposed call between defense ministers. This connects the refusal to a stated condition. It does not specify an apology, payment or operational concession that would satisfy that condition. The evidence establishes conditional access more securely than it establishes its price.103

Nor was this an unanswered ordinary telephone in the simple sense sometimes suggested by the word hotline. Contemporary AP reporting described a process for requesting an encrypted call or video conference. A much-cited recollection of a phone merely ringing referred to an incident in 2009. The distinction matters: a technical connection can exist while political permission to use it is withheld. Building more reliable communication requires knowing which of those failures has occurred.104

Diplomacy continued in February. Wang and Blinken met in Munich on February 18. The Chinese account described informal contact at American request and demanded correction of the damage caused by the use of force. The American account recorded demands about the airspace violation, concern about support to Russia and an insistence on keeping diplomatic dialogue open. The accounts corroborate the encounter while preserving disagreement. They rule out a chronology in which all senior communication stopped until June.105

Reopening one channel while another remained closed

The leadership’s knowledge of the balloon mission remains a different evidentiary problem. Contemporary reporting conveyed an American assessment that Xi and other senior Chinese officials might not have known about it over the United States. The underlying intelligence was not disclosed. Later interviews with former American officials support treating leadership awareness as an important question; they do not provide this project with an independently established account of Xi’s information.106

The March 2026 Carnegie study by Christopher Chivvis and Senkai Hsia adds useful American process evidence. Based on interviews with thirty-three former Biden officials, it describes negotiation over the strategic channel’s agenda, confidentiality and public statements. The report expressly lacks Chinese internal records. Its value here is to make the work of preparing a channel visible, with the participants’ American perspective retained. Their account should not be converted into a complete explanation of why Beijing accepted it.107

The May 10–11 Sullivan–Wang meeting in Vienna supplies an observable result. Both governments recorded substantive exchanges and agreement to maintain the strategic communication channel. The American account emphasized responsible management of competition; the Chinese one emphasized removing obstacles and arresting deterioration. They could agree to continue a channel while differing over the purpose of the relationship it was meant to manage.108

Later that month, the proposed Austin–Li Shangfu meeting in Singapore remained unsuccessful. Li was under American sanctions. Contemporary reporting placed those sanctions in the dispute, but the Chinese Defense Ministry’s May 31 answer did not offer a simple formula in which removing one sanction would guarantee a meeting. It emphasized principles, limits, corrected American conduct and an appropriate atmosphere. It also stated that military contacts had not been interrupted altogether. The objects being withheld remained specific.109

Blinken’s June visit made the distinction still clearer. He met Qin Gang on June 18 and Wang and Xi on June 19. The Chinese account of the Qin meeting listed agreements to maintain senior exchanges, pursue consultations and working groups, encourage exchanges and discuss additional flights. Qin accepted an invitation to visit the United States at a suitable time. Each was a commitment at its own stage: a visit invitation, a discussion of flights and an agreement to continue consulting were not completed visits, operating services or settled principles.110

On the military question, Blinken was explicit. Asked at his Beijing press availability whether the Chinese side had agreed to crisis or military communication channels, he said it had not and that there had been no immediate progress. He nevertheless defended continuing diplomacy. Access to Xi was compatible with failure to obtain this particular requested outcome. The observation belongs in a test of Solomon’s strong forecast about senior intervention; it does not alone establish how reliable that signal is compared with others.111

The June statements also prevent a broader exaggeration. Wang called for an end to unilateral sanctions and suppression of Chinese technological development. Xi disputed the American conception of great-power competition. Blinken reaffirmed targeted national-security restrictions and the American Taiwan policy, including the Taiwan Relations Act, the three communiqués and the Six Assurances. Repeating opposition to Taiwan independence did not amount to accepting all of Beijing’s demands. The encounter supported further engagement while leaving those positions in conflict.

What an old friend could do

Henry Kissinger arrived the next month. Li Shangfu received him on July 18, Wang Yi on July 19 and Xi on July 20. Xi’s welcome invoked his hundredth birthday, his many visits and the opening of relations more than fifty years earlier. Kissinger thanked Xi for choosing the Diaoyutai building associated with his first visit. The meeting offered a conspicuous example of personal recognition persisting across generations of Chinese leaders.112

There was also a practical appeal. Xi asked Kissinger and other Americans favoring better relations to contribute to improving them. Wang called for diplomatic judgment and political courage associated with Kissinger and Nixon, while repeating demands on Taiwan. A longer account of the Li meeting, republished from the Defense Ministry, attributed support for better military communication to Kissinger. It did not announce that Li had authorized official talks. Access and advocacy were visible; a resulting instruction was not.113

The contrast must be drawn carefully. Kissinger reached a defense minister whom Austin had been unable to meet through the desired formal channel. He did not uniquely gain entry to Xi when all serving Americans were excluded: Blinken had met Xi a month earlier, and Wang and Blinken met again in Jakarta before Kissinger’s arrival. The formal diplomatic track was already working. His visit cannot be made the origin of the entire reopening merely because it supplied the most evocative photograph.

Washington’s public account also distinguished the private visitor from an envoy. State said Kissinger was not acting on behalf of the government and that the Chinese side had mentioned his plans during Blinken’s June visit. Reuters reported John Kirby expressing interest in hearing what Kissinger learned when he returned. That was an anticipated opportunity for a debrief, not evidence that one had occurred or changed an instruction.114

There is positive evidence of consultation too. In his November statement after Kissinger’s death, Blinken acknowledged seeking his counsel in connection with travel to China and work on artificial intelligence. That is a serving official’s retrospective acknowledgment of advice. It supplies neither the content and precise timing of the advice nor a link from the July visit to a policy decision. But it rules out treating the private status of the visit as proof that Kissinger had no access to American policymakers.115

The case therefore separates several roles that the phrase old friend tends to bundle together. A person can be honored, consulted, encouraged to advocate, asked for information and authorized to negotiate. The roles can overlap, but one does not prove the next. China’s public appeal establishes an effort to recruit advocacy. Establishing effective leverage over the recipient would additionally require his response and some consequence for an actual decision. Establishing no influence would likewise require more than the absence of a disclosed concession.

This is a bounded update to Solomon’s relationship mechanism. Personal standing remained a resource, and Chinese leaders visibly cultivated it. The evidence recovered here does not trace the crucial passage from cultivation through a specific transmitted message to changed policy. Nor does the American interest in advice establish a uniquely Chinese way of using former officials. The episode gives us observable parts of the mechanism and a precisely located missing link.

A summit agreement, then implementation

At the November 15 Woodside summit, the two public accounts finally converged on named military mechanisms: high-level military communication, Defense Policy Coordination Talks, Military Maritime Consultative Agreement meetings and telephone conversations between theater commanders. The Chinese account specified equality and respect. This was more definite than a favorable description of the atmosphere, but the readouts supplied no guaranteed response time or unconditional promise that every mechanism would remain available during the next confrontation.116

The summit also produced commitments on counternarcotics and government AI discussions. One adjacent American concession can be documented particularly well. A Commerce rule removed the Ministry of Public Security’s Institute of Forensic Science from the Entity List, effective November 16. The rule establishes the act and its date. Separately, a named State spokesman, Matthew Miller, explained that the listing had obstructed precursor-chemical cooperation and that China’s proposed steps justified removal.117

This is evidence of a particular tradeoff, not merely two events occurring close together. It still does not establish that the delisting bought the military-channel agreement. Different deliverables could involve different exchanges, and the evidence connecting the institute to counternarcotics should not be generalized to every subject at the summit. Nor did delisting establish that the underlying human-rights concerns had been resolved or that future drug-enforcement cooperation would work. Contemporary reporting included objections to the choice.

The paired summit readouts continued to register disagreement over Taiwan and technology restrictions. Their value lies partly in that coexistence: a commitment to specified communication was compatible with unresolved rivalry. The parties had identified things they were prepared to do, rather than reached a comprehensive common understanding of each other’s conduct.

On November 30, China’s defense spokesman said the departments were communicating and coordinating implementation. Both the Chinese and official English formulations placed the resumption of the mechanisms ahead of them. An agreement to restore a channel had reached a follow-up stage; the statement did not establish that all the mechanisms had already convened. This chapter ends at that boundary, rather than borrowing later contacts to make the November announcement appear complete when issued.118

What changes between the two crises

Both episodes produced useful action short of a shared account of the dispute. In 2001, the immediate crew arrangement left responsibility, reconnaissance and the aircraft to other decisions. In 2023, renewed diplomatic contact and commitments to restore military mechanisms coexisted with disagreement about Taiwan, technology and the balloon. The continuity is the possibility of a limited commitment whose scope matters more than a declaration that the relationship has improved.

The bargaining objects differed. In 2001, people and an aircraft could be separated and moved through identifiable release and transport decisions. In 2023, several of the objects were institutions for repeated contact. Their value depended on future willingness to use them, including at moments when politics made communication difficult. That creates a distinctive implementation question: an announced channel is useful in a crisis only if the relevant officials will authorize participation and can act on what they hear.

This is not a clean estimate of historical change in Chinese character. The incidents differed in location, loss, custody, political background and desired outcomes. The comparison instead identifies a change in the problem a counterpart must solve. Establishing contact and obtaining a usable commitment are separate tasks; with institutionalized channels, maintaining contact can itself become a continuing negotiation.

The sources also place different limits on explanation. The 2001 legal packet recovers part of contemporary American preparation; later participant and scholarly accounts suggest channels and institutional problems whose decisive records remain missing. The 2023 record gives unusually explicit public refusals and a later interview-based account of American preparations, but still lacks the Chinese authorization trail. More source material improves the reconstruction without making every inference symmetrical across governments.

Solomon’s strongest continuing contribution is to direct attention toward purpose, authority, the presentation of prior undertakings and the political uses of access. These cases make those questions more specific. Which contact was refused? Which action was authorized? Which unresolved issue was reserved? Which message reached someone able to decide? The answers explain more than a general claim that Chinese negotiators value relationships or protect face. They also identify where such explanations remain plausible and what evidence would make them persuasive.

5. A partial settlement under pressure

On October 11, 2019, Donald Trump announced progress on a trade agreement with China in the Oval Office. Beside him sat Vice Premier Liu He. The president praised the Chinese delegation, celebrated prospective agricultural purchases and described a first phase that could be completed in writing within weeks. The exchange had the public features of an approaching settlement: senior access, assurances of a good relationship, a message from Xi Jinping and an account of mutual benefit. It also contained an unusually clear account of what the two sides had not settled. Robert Lighthizer said the consultation and escalation structure was largely in place, but the consequences of an unresolved dispute still required work.119

That was a substantive gap. An agreement to buy goods or change administrative practice has a different value depending on what happens when the promise is disputed. The meeting did not merely await a cleaner translation of a finished bargain. Steven Mnuchin acknowledged further work on the text and within Liu’s team. Trump himself allowed for the possibility that completion could fail. The ceremony and the qualifications were part of the same event.

By January 15, 2020, the parties had signed a text with detailed obligations, scheduled purchases and a specific procedure for unresolved complaints. They had also accepted a settlement smaller than the transformation of the economic relationship that Washington had sought. Planned American duties had been suspended, and a reduction in some existing duties was promised; substantial tariffs would remain. The agreement recognized domestic choice over implementation methods, yet required performance. Either party could complain; neither gained an independent judge. These were negotiated boundaries around continuing conflict, rather than the disappearance of conflict.120

This chapter follows that transition. The central development is the construction of a narrower bargain whose procedures were more definite than the earlier announcements. Its explanation requires attention to Chinese adjustments and American ones. It also requires an unusually strict distinction between what participants said they had agreed and the terms available for inspection. The spring 2019 draft that became the subject of accusations of Chinese backtracking remains missing from this research. The final agreement can establish the eventual bargain. It cannot, on its own, tell us which side changed every disputed provision along the way.

The first truce and its different versions

The May 19, 2018 joint statement offered a framework for further work. It identified Liu as Xi’s special envoy and named Mnuchin, Commerce Secretary Wilbur Ross and Lighthizer on the American side. China would increase purchases; the teams would continue work on agriculture and energy; the parties would pursue intellectual-property cooperation and relevant Chinese legal amendments, including to the Patent Law. Manufacturing, services, investment and continued senior engagement appeared alongside the proposed commercial expansion. Neither a total purchase figure nor a tariff suspension appeared in the published joint text.121

The next day’s public accounts made the result sound more extensive. Xinhua reported Liu describing agreement against a trade war and additional tariffs. Reuters reported Mnuchin announcing that tariffs were on hold while the framework was developed. The same Reuters report carried a different emphasis from other American officials: Larry Kudlow denied that a completed trade deal existed, while Lighthizer stressed the importance of structural reform. A Chinese negotiator listening to Washington therefore encountered agreement on a pause alongside continuing qualifications about the settlement it was supposed to make possible.122

The distinction between the joint statement and the separate remarks matters in both directions. The absence of a written pause clause does not show that no oral understanding existed. But the public remarks do not supply all the terms of that understanding: its duration, exceptions, final approval requirements and relationship to further Chinese offers. The available record establishes a publicly asserted pause whose scope was incompletely documented. It does not establish either an unconditional American promise never to impose tariffs or a Chinese invention of an understanding that no American official had acknowledged.

On May 29, the White House announced that it would impose 25 percent tariffs on $50 billion of Chinese goods, with the final list to follow in June. The announcement combined continued negotiations with the intended use of pressure. China’s June 3 statement, following talks with Ross in Beijing, reported progress but reserved final confirmation of details. It also made the negotiated outcomes contingent on avoiding a trade war: American sanctions, including tariffs, would prevent those outcomes from taking effect. The June 15 White House statement confirmed its tariff decision and threatened further action in response to retaliation.123

There was movement by both governments, but not necessarily movement of the same kind. Washington moved from a publicly announced pause toward tariffs. Beijing explicitly attached a condition to the effectiveness of the outcomes then under discussion. Describing all of this as the breaking of a single completed agreement would erase the differences among a joint framework, an orally described pause and a conditional package. Describing it only as Chinese reluctance to commit would erase the American sequence.

The September 2018 Chinese white paper turned these events into an argument about responsibility. Its account presented American policy changes as evidence of unreliability, while combining opposition to tariff pressure with willingness to negotiate and further opening at China’s chosen pace. This was an authorized public case against Washington. Its authority as a statement of China’s position did not make it an independent account of why American officials changed course.124

For Solomon’s framework, the episode shifts attention from an allegedly national attitude toward promises to a more concrete problem: which promise, made by whom, had survived the next round of authorization? That question applies to the counterparty too. China’s effort to bind Washington to a prior understanding is observable in its later public narrative. Whether that narrative overstated a mutually accepted obligation requires the original terms. A charge of inconsistency cannot substitute for them.

The leaders set a process, not a finished contract

Trump and Xi’s December 1 meeting in Buenos Aires produced another opening. The American readout described a deferral of the scheduled rise from 10 to 25 percent on $200 billion of Chinese goods while structural negotiations proceeded over approximately ninety days. It also described substantial Chinese purchases but said the amount had not yet been agreed. Wang Yi’s Chinese account described a pause on additional tariffs, purchases responsive to China’s needs, and negotiation toward removing all additional tariffs. It characterized the leaders’ result as principles from which the teams should develop a concrete agreement. It supplied neither a purchase figure nor a ninety-day deadline.125

These statements overlap without being interchangeable. Both permit a claim that the leaders authorized renewed negotiations. They do not provide a jointly authenticated schedule of purchases or identical public conditions for the pause. Silence in the Chinese readout about the deadline does not demonstrate that it was absent from private discussion. Equally, the American readout’s explicit absence of an agreed quantity cannot be replaced by a later purchase figure without examining the intervening record.

Senior authorization had opened room to negotiate, but the principal American negotiator continued to describe unresolved questions. In his February 27, 2019 testimony, Lighthizer stressed enforceability and qualified the prospect of an agreement. The White House’s April 23 announcement of another visit to Beijing still listed purchases and enforcement among the subjects to be discussed. Neither document reveals Xi’s authorization of particular paragraphs, or the point at which Liu could convert a provisional text into China’s final commitment.126

The deadline also moved. The subsequent American tariff notice recorded an initial postponement to March 2 and then a postponement until further notice. The clock was an instrument under presidential control. Its extension tells us that Washington retained discretion over the timing of pressure; it does not establish either that the threat was empty or that a fixed external deadline compelled the parties’ decisions.127

Solomon called high-level intervention the most reliable sign of impending closure, alongside his attention to concrete drafting. His own failed claims-and-assets episode, examined in Chapter 1, complicates that forecast. Here, leader involvement has to be divided into several acts: authorizing a process, endorsing its direction, approving specific obligations and making the final tradeoff. The first two are visible in the public record. Their visibility should not be mistaken for evidence that the latter two had already occurred. This case helps specify what a predictive test must measure; it cannot establish the comparative reliability of different signals. Access to a senior official does not itself disclose what that official has authorized.

What the May breakdown establishes

The most consequential contemporary report appeared on May 8. Reuters described extensive revisions to a nearly 150-page draft, transmitted from Beijing several days earlier. Its account rested on three American government sources and three private-sector sources briefed on the talks. The reported revisions affected commitments across seven chapters, with particular importance attached to Chinese legal changes. According to the report, Liu proposed reliance on administrative and regulatory measures instead; Lighthizer and Mnuchin regarded that as inadequate.128

The dispute over implementation form was material. A requirement incorporated in legislation, a regulation, an administrative instruction and an assurance by a negotiator need not have the same durability, visibility or reach. Officials concerned about evasion could therefore care about the domestic vehicle as well as the promised substantive result. Chinese officials concerned about sovereignty, legal process or the appearance of accepting foreign direction could care about precisely the same distinction for different reasons. That conflict need not be reduced either to ceremonial dignity or to distrust arising from an immutable culture.

The Reuters report deserves weight as original reporting about inaccessible negotiations. It also has a clear evidentiary boundary: the article does not say that its journalists obtained the draft. Some of its sources had been briefed rather than identified as direct participants in drafting. Six sources therefore do not necessarily mean six independent inspections of the same document. The story also included a Chinese official familiar with the talks who disputed backtracking, emphasized the procedures needed to change laws, and described American demands as becoming harsher. Industrial subsidies and biotechnology approvals remained sticking points in the report. It cannot establish that every American demand absent from Phase One had once received final Chinese assent.

The escalation itself is directly documented. USTR’s May 9 notice stated the administration’s accusation that China had retreated from commitments and implemented the president’s direction to raise the additional tariff from 10 to 25 percent, subject to the notice’s entry and export conditions. The notice is firm evidence of an accusation adopted by the American government and the action taken on that basis. It does not reproduce the undertakings in dispute.129

Liu’s account after the May 9–10 round identified three concerns: removal of all additional tariffs, realistic purchases and a balanced text respecting national dignity. On purchases, he asserted that the two sides had already reached numerical consensus in Argentina and should not arbitrarily change it. The published account disclosed no schedule. That claim sits uneasily beside the contemporary American readout saying the amount remained unagreed. The discrepancy is a specific research problem, not a reason to select whichever account fits a preferred narrative.130

The June 2 Chinese white paper then challenged the meaning of changes during an unfinished negotiation. It argued that revisions to wording and content remained normal, that American demands had changed too, and that the United States was making unacceptable demands involving sovereign affairs. The argument did not require China to have left every draft paragraph untouched. Reported deletions and a Chinese insistence that revision remained legitimate could both be true. What separates an authorized retreat from revision of an unaccepted proposal is the status of the earlier text and its reservations.131

There are therefore several different propositions to judge. That the sides experienced a serious dispute about draft obligations and implementation form is well supported. That Washington treated the dispute as grounds for escalation is directly established. That Xi personally reversed a bargain already approved in all particulars, that Liu exceeded his mandate, or that China had planned a tactical retreat from the start requires evidence beyond the documents inspected here. Later accounts repeating the Reuters report cannot supply that evidence merely through repetition.

This leaves a substantial finding, even without a final allocation of blame. The negotiation failed at a point where apparent convergence on outcomes had not yielded an accepted combination of domestic implementation, purchase commitments, tariff relief and enforcement. Those elements were connected. A demand to retain tariffs while imposing domestic legal changes would be evaluated differently from the same legal obligation accompanied by their removal. A finding about the acceptable package is consequently more useful than treating each public position as an unconditional measure of resolve.

Accepting less, and specifying it more clearly

By autumn, a smaller settlement was publicly possible. A September Wall Street Journal report, citing people familiar with Chinese plans, described an effort to separate trade from national-security disputes. That is evidence of a reported strategy, not an inspected mandate. The October 11 Oval Office exchange gives firmer evidence of what the American president was prepared to announce: negotiations in phases, with several subjects addressed in the first agreement and further work deferred. Lighthizer expressly said Huawei was outside this agreement.132

The meeting also showed that tariff decisions could be separated. Mnuchin attributed suspension of the planned increase from 25 to 30 percent to a request from Liu and approval by Trump. Lighthizer distinguished that decision from the prospective December duties, on which the president had not yet made a final decision. A decision to suspend one increase was not a decision to remove all existing duties or cancel every future one.133

The enforcement question provides the clearest documented trajectory. In October, Lighthizer described consultation and escalation machinery while acknowledging that the outcome of an unresolved dispute was still being finalized. In December, Chinese officials announced agreement on the text but reserved legal review, translation checks and signing arrangements. In January, the published instrument specified what could follow failed consultations. This sequence takes us from an expressly open question to accepted language without pretending to reconstruct the missing intermediate drafts.

China’s December 13 briefing also makes the change in acceptable scope visible. Officials described partial removal of planned and existing additional tariffs. They defended the agreement as consistent with domestic reform, better protection for enterprises and China’s own demand for imports. Agricultural officials emphasized benefits sought for Chinese exports as well as the increased American sales. The public justification presented an exchange with domestic value, rather than an abandonment of Party leadership or an unrestricted remaking of the economy.134

The difference from Liu’s May demand for removal of all additional tariffs is real. It is not erased by the consistent language of equality and mutual respect. But the object of negotiation had changed too: officials were now defending a first-stage agreement. The record therefore supports a revision in what Beijing was willing to accept for a partial settlement. It does not establish that the same comprehensive agreement was available on unchanged terms throughout the year, or isolate which economic and political costs made the narrower package attractive.

Washington made a corresponding adjustment. Trump publicly defended dividing the issues into phases and retained tariffs as pressure for subsequent performance and negotiation. Neither government had secured all that its earlier public position appeared to demand. Their willingness to leave issues outside the immediate bargain was itself consequential: it reduced the set of disagreements that had to be resolved before signature. This was compatible with continued disagreement about the issues deferred.135

Even at this late stage, announcements and operative measures followed different calendars. The notice published December 18 suspended the duties planned for December 15 and stated that a separate notice would address reduction of existing duties. The January 22 notice prescribed a reduction from 15 to 7.5 percent for the relevant List 4A goods effective February 14. The December announcement cannot be used to date that reduction to December. Through January, the record establishes an actual suspension of planned duties and an issued reduction scheduled for February; it does not inspect individual customs entries.136

Domestic authority inside the final text

The agreement signed on January 15 combined precise requirements with specified discretion. Article 1.34, although located in the intellectual-property chapter, speaks about implementation of the agreement as a whole. Each party chooses appropriate methods within its own system and practice; where necessary, it is to propose legal amendments through domestic procedures. The same article requires full implementation of the obligations. The ability to choose a domestic vehicle did not make the substantive undertaking optional.137

For intellectual property, Article 1.35 adds a plan identifying implementation measures and their effective dates within thirty working days after entry into force. There is a bilingual nuance: the English text uses promulgate, while the Chinese uses 制定, meaning formulate or draw up. The common deadline and required content are clear. Treating both verbs as an identical public-posting requirement would go beyond that shared wording. Neither version turns the plan into a discretionary aspiration.

The provisions help identify what the final settlement accommodated. They do not establish who proposed the formula, which earlier demand it displaced, or whether either side privately regarded it as a victory. The missing May redline still matters. It would be especially misleading to infer from domestic procedural discretion that the United States had abandoned all requirements for legal or institutional change. It would be equally misleading to describe the agreement as prescribing the domestic form of every Chinese implementing measure.

There was also substantive asymmetry within formally bilateral language. Many intellectual-property articles required Chinese changes while affirming that existing American measures already supplied equivalent treatment; Article 1.36 addressed consistency of existing American measures with the chapter. The parties could accept reciprocal standing without identical new burdens. This gives content to the problem of a balanced text: balance need not mean matching each obligation with an equal adjustment by the other government. Nor can its meaning be recovered merely by counting reciprocal pronouns or references to sovereignty.138

The result gives political authority a specific institutional form: it accepted rules defining both obligations and permitted discretion. Compared with a general undertaking to improve relations, the text gave a counterpart more specified questions to ask: which implementing measure, due when, under which article? That contrast concerns different instruments; it is not by itself proof of a historical increase in Chinese willingness to accept precision. Nor did the text guarantee that the answer would be satisfactory, or that every agency involved would behave as one actor. Those are questions for the implementation record.

A procedure for disagreement

Chapter 7 made continued political negotiation part of the formal arrangement. It established bilateral offices, designated officials and escalation through deputies to the United States trade representative and a designated Chinese vice premier. The principals would lead a Trade Framework Group. Information requests, meeting schedules and the handling of complaints received stated procedures. An urgent matter could move directly to the principals, rather than waiting for every lower-level stage. If an urgent meeting could not be scheduled in time, Article 7.4.5 also allowed the complainant to take action under the remedial provisions of 7.4.4(b). Access to a meeting therefore could not indefinitely block that specified route.139

The timelines were more definite than a general promise to consult. The working-level and deputy-level periods ran from receipt of the appeal: twenty-one and forty-five calendar days respectively. They were not two sequential allowances totaling sixty-six days. Other provisions specified principal-level meetings and allowed the parties to extend periods in writing. The arrangement gave disagreement an organized path while preserving agreed adjustment of that path.

The procedure did not end with an independent decision on the merits. If the principals failed to resolve the matter, the parties would consult over a response to the complainant’s losses. If they could not agree, the complaining party could take action under Article 7.4.4(b), including suspending an obligation or adopting a proportionate remedial measure. The text did not prescribe automatic restoration of a particular tariff. It defined a process and allowed responsive action within it.

The respondent’s assessment mattered too. If it considered the action taken in good faith, the provision barred a counterresponse or other challenge. If it considered the action taken in bad faith, the specified remedy was withdrawal by written notice. The restriction was conditional and applied to either government. Neither side delegated the good-faith question to a neutral adjudicator.

A separate provision, Article 8.3.2, allowed either party to terminate by written notice, normally effective after sixty days or another agreed date. Bad faith under Chapter 7 was consequently not the only textual route out. The agreement used different language for withdrawal under the complaint mechanism and termination under the final provisions; it is enough here to preserve both clauses without inventing a definitive resolution of every possible interaction between them.140

The bargain also protected the confidentiality of appeals and related information unless the parties agreed otherwise, while preserving rights and obligations under the WTO and other agreements. These features matter for subsequent research. A quiet public record cannot establish that a confidential mechanism went unused. A later tariff investigation is not necessarily an appeal under this chapter. Nor does the choice of bilateral procedures establish that China agreed to abandon other forums.

Solomon’s attention to senior authority remains useful, but the unit of analysis changes. We can identify what lower officials may attempt, when a principal may intervene and what the written arrangement permits after failure. The assignment of authority is demonstrable. The personal effect of an individual leader, or the national preference supposedly responsible for the design, is not established simply by finding that authority in the agreement. American negotiators accepted and promoted the arrangement too.

Purchases: the number was part of the bargain

The purchase promises illustrate why precise wording is indispensable. Trump repeatedly described agricultural purchases of $40–50 billion in October. At the December Chinese briefing, a Reuters question asked about a $50 billion figure, but the official answer deferred disclosure of specific numbers while the text underwent review. The question was not Chinese confirmation. Neither exchange can replace the schedule in the agreement.141

The final terms required covered purchases and imports to exceed corresponding 2017 baselines by at least $200 billion across 2020–2021. That was an increment, not a total. Within agriculture, the annual increments were $12.5 billion for 2020 and $19.5 billion for 2021. A separate annex note contained an undertaking, at the American request, to strive for another $5 billion annually. A projection of continued growth during 2022–2025 was another kind of statement again; it did not renew the same quantified annual minima.142

The agreement also recognized market prices and commercial considerations, addressed American facilitation of supply, and provided consultation over conflicting trade-data assessments and circumstances in the United States affecting Chinese performance. Those qualifications belonged to the accepted instrument. They did not expressly cancel its numerical requirements. The task is to read them together, rather than choose either the headline number or the qualifying language as the entire bargain.

This matters beyond accounting. An obligation to secure purchases through many commercial transactions is different from a government’s promise to issue one regulation. Actual sales involve prices, product coverage, demand, supply and administrative access. An agreement can impose a government obligation while relying on transactions the government does not make itself. Its performance must therefore be tested at several levels: the agreed benchmark, the measured trade, the obstacles encountered and the response requested. A national aggregate alone will not explain how Chinese negotiators and implementing agencies behaved.

Explaining the adjustment

One competing explanation is learning through confrontation. Cathy Wu’s bargaining account distinguishes uncertainty about an opponent’s resolve from difficulty securing future compliance. Applied here, it asks whether costly escalation made previously unknown limits credible. That is a useful hypothesis. Observing costs followed by agreement is not enough to establish it.143

The same sequence could arise because costs changed the limits themselves, because the governments reduced the number of issues to settle, or because new implementation and enforcement terms made a particular exchange acceptable. The explanations can coexist. To distinguish their contribution, we need evidence of an earlier expectation, a consequential observation and a revised instruction or offer. A settlement reached after escalating tariffs is compatible with learning; it is also compatible with movement in the parties’ preferences or available options.

Angela Huyue Zhang’s contract-theory account adds a different distinction: changing a public law is easier to observe than changing informal administrative behavior, and a threatened sanction is credible only if the enforcing government will bear its costs. These mechanisms turn the inquiry toward the implementer and the credibility of a particular remedy. Her account includes original interview material about legal design, but its May 2019 reconstruction principally follows published reporting; it does not supply the missing Chinese authorization.144

The final agreement gives those mechanisms a concrete test. Did a formal change remove the practice that troubled a firm? Could officials identify and document the remaining obstacle? If a remedy was considered, did its expected costs prevent action, or did the governments settle on another response? Neither a statute nor a tariff provides the answer alone. Zhang’s strong conclusions about weak enforcement should therefore be tested against what the parties actually did, with the final text’s available remedies preserved. This is where the bargaining narrative must pass into the implementation cases.

The evidence presented here is strongest about scope and agreed procedure. China moved from demanding removal of all additional tariffs toward accepting partial relief for a first stage. The United States accepted phases while retaining pressure and deferring disputes. An openly unfinished enforcement question became a written procedure, and domestic implementation discretion appeared alongside requirements for performance. Those are changes in observable negotiating behavior and the resulting instrument. The relative causal weight of tariff pain, leadership beliefs, domestic opposition and technical drafting remains less certain.

The result revises rather than simply discards Solomon’s questions. Prior undertakings still mattered, but their status must be reconstructed before a later formulation is classified as repudiation. Senior involvement mattered, but authorization of negotiation was distinct from approval of a final bargain. Partial settlements were possible, but the existence of one does not by itself demonstrate a mechanism of saving face. Detailed provisions were accepted, but their precision coexisted with political discretion and continuing conflict.

Finally, formal provision for further bargaining does not verify Solomon’s stronger claim about an asymmetric Chinese pattern of demanding adherence from others while seeking accommodation for itself. Chapter 7 was formally bilateral. Whether its operation displayed that asymmetry requires the subsequent record of obligations, complaints and requested exceptions. The agreement made the next phase of the inquiry more concrete: it specified promises against which actual behavior could be assessed. The next chapter follows what happened when firms, regulators and governments tried to turn those promises into usable benefits.

6. After the signature

On March 20, 2020, JPMorgan announced that its new securities company in China had opened for business. The coronavirus epidemic had made an ordinary launch difficult; the firm marked the occasion virtually. Two months after the signing of the Phase One trade agreement, an American financial institution was beginning to use access that had occupied negotiators for years. The announcement could readily become an illustration of the agreement’s success. Its chronology makes a more interesting argument. The company already possessed its operating permit before the agreement was signed. It opened with JPMorgan holding 51 percent, became wholly owned the following year, and subsequently reported substantial brokerage revenue. These were different achievements, obtained through different decisions.145

The distinction matters for judging both negotiation and implementation. Chinese officials could deliver a meaningful opening without having originated it as a concession in the last negotiation. A signed commitment could accelerate an existing reform without securing every license needed to use it. Commercial operation could demonstrate that access was real without demonstrating that foreign ownership caused the business to prosper. Each proposition requires a different piece of evidence.

The cases produce a mixed but concrete answer. Phase One accelerated a published securities-opening timetable by eight months; company records trace access into ownership and revenue. Selected crop permissions enabled identifiable launch plans while procedural timing remained contested. Aggregate purchases fell far short. By 2026 the parties were still arguing about the old agreement while designing another mechanism for managing trade. Explaining those different results requires following each commitment to the decision that would make it useful.

An opening that became a business

Financial reform did not begin in January 2020. In November 2017, after Donald Trump’s visit to China, Vice Finance Minister Zhu Guangyao announced a prospective increase to 51 percent in foreign ownership of securities, fund-management and futures companies, followed by removal of the ceiling three years after implementation. Xinhua’s account located the announcement among summit outcomes, while also recording Chinese requests concerning American investment treatment and export restrictions. The opening would benefit investors from countries other than the United States. This was evidence of reform within a bilateral political setting, not a basis for assigning its entire cause either to domestic policy or American pressure.146

The securities regulator’s April 2018 rules made foreign control possible. JPMorgan pursued a majority-owned firm. The establishment decision, signed by the China Securities Regulatory Commission on March 13, 2019 and received by the company on March 29, authorized a business with RMB 800 million in registered capital and five Chinese shareholders alongside JPMorgan. It identified permitted lines of business but required further steps: organizing and registering the company, providing capital and obtaining an operating permit. The company could not conduct the securities business merely because its establishment had been approved. Registration followed on August 22; the securities operating permit followed on December 18.147

That sequence gives the agreement a narrower but firmer place in the story. In October 2019 the regulator had announced that the securities ownership ceiling would disappear on December 1, 2020. Phase One required its removal no later than April 1. In March, the regulator explicitly invoked implementation of the agreement when confirming the April date. Eight months of acceleration are therefore observable against the previously published timetable. The agreement did not invent the opening, and the opening was not merely a repetition of what China had already scheduled for the same date.148

This is close to the distinction Martin Chorzempa drew in his contemporary assessment of the financial-services chapter: prior liberalization must be separated from marginal improvements obtained in the bargain. Nicholas Lardy and Tianlei Huang subsequently situated institutional opening within China’s uneven financial integration, distinguishing the entry of foreign firms from the liberalization of capital flows. These analyses provide useful alternatives to a single-pressure explanation. The published Chinese timetable and the regulator’s express link to Phase One establish the change more directly than an inference from the agreement’s political celebration.149

The company then had to turn eligibility into ownership. In October 2020 a Chinese partner’s 20 percent holding was put up for sale. Contemporary reporting described what JPMorgan’s stake would become if it acquired that holding; the company’s year-end disclosure still recorded the old 51 percent structure. An announced sale was not a completed transfer. In August 2021 the regulator accepted the filing for JPMorgan to acquire the five domestic partners’ holdings. The firm completed the shareholder registration on August 25 and became wholly foreign owned. Its capital also increased substantially. Those records establish a completed ownership change, beyond an application or favorable announcement.150

The subsequent accounts establish something more valuable than permission alone. Net brokerage fees rose from approximately RMB 0.93 million in 2020 to RMB 138.44 million in 2021 and RMB 555.95 million in 2022. Investment-banking fees rose from RMB 3.80 million to RMB 41.29 million before falling to RMB 37.60 million. Revenue was being earned in the authorized activities. The different movements also warn against attributing every commercial result to the same regulatory change. Staffing, capital, clients, the firm’s preparation and market conditions intervened between permission and income. Full ownership arrived during 2021, so the annual comparison cannot isolate its effect.151

Nor did the achievement eliminate subsequent administrative work. The company’s 2022 disclosure recorded registration of a Beijing branch while its operating permit remained in process. A separate January 2023 decision authorizing a JPMorgan fund-management ownership change imposed continuing compliance requirements, including obligations concerning data and personal information. Neither item proves that regulators deliberately withheld the benefit of the securities opening. They show why a negotiator cannot equate removal of one ownership restriction with the disappearance of regulation across a financial group.152

The positive result changes how Solomon’s concern with bargaining after agreement should be applied. A government can preserve continuing regulatory authority while implementing an important concession. Further applications may concern genuinely additional activity, or they may become a means of narrowing promised access. Distinguishing these possibilities requires the particular obligation, a complete application, a decision and its reasons. The interval since a summit supplies none of those by itself. Here, actual operation and income make a general claim of illusory access untenable; the surviving approvals make a general claim of completed liberalization equally inadequate.

A certificate, a planting season and another country’s decision

Biotechnology exposes a different problem. A seed developer may have permission to cultivate a crop in its production market but hesitate to launch it broadly while a major destination has not authorized imports. Commodities move through shared handling systems, and an unapproved trait can create risks beyond the individual seed sale. The relevant Chinese permissions in the two cases examined here were for imported material used in processing, not for planting genetically modified crops in China. Treating them as cultivation approvals would mistake the commercial bottleneck.153

The effort to address that bottleneck also preceded Phase One. The 2016 Strategic and Economic Dialogue included commitments concerning timely, science-based approvals. The May 2017 joint early-harvest announcement required the National Biosafety Committee to evaluate eight pending American applications by the end of that month. It addressed written explanations for additional information, further committee meetings after resubmission, and certificates for products that passed evaluation. The undertaking was to evaluate pending applications through specified procedures. It did not promise that all eight would be approved.154

American administrative records reveal some of the work behind these demands. Emails released by the Department of Agriculture show industry representatives seeking a meeting for Dow AgroSciences chief executive Tim Hassinger, supplying briefing material and discussing possible congressional questions with department official Rebeckah Adcock in May 2017. Adcock pushed back on proposed questioning after the China announcement. In June, Hassinger credited the bilateral effort for approval of Enlist corn, and Adcock forwarded that message within the department. The record reveals access, information supply, official response and the company’s interpretation of a result. It does not establish that China approved the corn because of the particular meeting, or identify every soybean trait mentioned in missing attachments. The corresponding public congressional hearing shows how a broad demand for accountability became a question that also acknowledged the announced progress.155

Phase One made the procedure more definite. Annex 16 required an average of no more than 24 months between formal submission and a final decision to approve or disapprove specified agricultural biotechnology products. It required an initial completeness check within five working days, written explanations connecting requests for information to safety, sufficient committee meetings, and a certificate within 20 working days after a product passed the committee’s evaluation. Authorizations were to last at least five years. This was neither a guarantee of approval nor an individual 24-month maximum. The negotiated object was partly the administration of decisions: how an applicant would learn what was missing, how a stalled review would resume and how long a successful permission would remain useful.156

Enlist E3 soybeans illustrate both a real permission and the danger of dating it by the news cycle. The original Chinese list, published on January 8, 2019, recorded a certificate effective from December 20, 2018 to December 20, 2021. Publication coincided with renewed trade talks, but the certificate’s stated period had begun earlier. The January talks therefore cannot simply be credited with producing a decision made that week. Contemporary trade reporting also distinguished public approval from the company’s receipt of the paperwork.157

China’s action still did not complete the product’s international launch conditions. Corteva and MS Technologies announced the American launch on February 21, 2019 after Philippine import approval. The sequence identifies two separate gates: Chinese access was important, but the company still awaited another destination’s decision. The Chinese renewal later listed Enlist E3 for December 17, 2021 through December 16, 2026, a five-year period compared with the original three. That is a concrete improvement consistent with the agreement’s authorization requirement. It is not evidence that a new application had been processed within the promised average, or that the agreement alone caused the longer renewal.158

Optimum GLY canola presents a harder outcome. Reuters reported that the relevant Chinese application for event DP73496 had been filed in July 2012. It remained pending in the agency’s January 2019 survey. The Chinese certificate eventually listed a validity period beginning January 5, 2023; publication followed on January 13. Approximately ten and a half years separated the reported application from permission. That interval captures a company’s prolonged uncertainty, although the public record does not reveal all requests for additional material or the dates on which a complete dossier was available.159

A comparison of the same product prevents that interval from becoming an automatic explanation of a decade of lost sales. The European Union had received its application on May 15, 2012. A favorable European Food Safety Authority opinion came on June 17, 2021, and Commission authorization on March 31, 2022, excluding cultivation. The Commission decision records that both the standing committee and the appeal committee had failed to deliver an opinion. China was thus not the only major import market in which authorization took years. Its final permission came roughly nine months after the EU’s. This does not excuse Chinese delay or prove that the EU was the sole earlier obstacle; it limits the claim that China’s decision alone postponed a globally ready product throughout the entire interval.160

In late January 2023, a named Corteva representative told the Western Producer that China had supplied the final global approval, while stewardship and industry communications still had to be completed. In February the company announced commercial launch in the United States and Canada for 2023 and a later full launch in Australia. Here the Chinese decision can be connected to an identified commercial sequence more closely than a general announcement of agricultural cooperation. The announcement remains evidence of a launch plan, not a measurement of acreage actually planted or revenue earned.161

The procedural dispute nevertheless endured. At the December 2025 American hearing on Phase One, the seed industry’s representative reported an average approval time of 69 months and two approvals that had taken at least nine years. The 2026 National Trade Estimate Report asserted that China had not met its average-time commitment and criticized unpublished results from biosafety committee meetings in 2025. These are consequential industry and American government assessments, not an independently reconstructed dataset of all Chinese applications. The certificates establish that permissions existed; they do not establish that the approval system satisfied its timing obligations. Conversely, slow approvals do not show that each delay was a deliberately traded bargaining asset.162

The comparison with finance is therefore substantive. For JPMorgan, company accounts follow formal opening into sustained operation. For these crops, the record follows selected permissions into renewals and launch announcements while leaving the broader procedural promise disputed.

The purchase target and the business it could not command

Phase One’s purchase commitments made another kind of promise. They concerned aggregate flows of goods and services over 2020–2021, built on a 2017 baseline. A regulator could remove an ownership ceiling by a specified date; meeting an aggregate dollar target depended on many transactions, prices, demand, supply and the relationship’s other restrictions. The agreement itself recognized commercial considerations and market conditions. Those provisions did not erase its numerical undertaking, but they matter when explaining why performance fell short.163

Chad Bown’s Peterson Institute tracker separates two official statistical perspectives for covered goods. Using American export data, purchases were $210.1 billion against an estimated $352.2 billion commitment, or 60 percent. Using Chinese import data, the corresponding figures were $235.3 billion against $380.5 billion, or 62 percent. These are estimates for the goods covered by the agreement, not every good traded between the countries. Including covered services on the American-data basis, Bown calculated $290.8 billion against $502.4 billion, or 58 percent.164

His memorable conclusion that China bought none of the additional $200 billion described performance relative to the aggregate baseline. It did not mean that China bought nothing from the United States. Nor does the shortfall identify one cause. Bown’s analysis discusses the pandemic’s effect on services, aircraft supply problems and the continuing effects of the trade war. The agreement’s unpublished allocations below its major categories and differences in statistical coverage also limit replication. In particular, the analysis excludes some services supplied through commercial presence that the agreement encompasses. Its monthly paths are illustrative allocations of annual commitments, not an additional set of contractual monthly deadlines.165

The American government’s later enforcement benchmark must be kept separate. The October 2025 initiation notice described a commitment exceeding $535 billion and an aggregate shortfall exceeding $217 billion, citing official American export data. That is not Bown’s $502.4 billion benchmark with a different label. The notice does not provide the reconciliation needed to merge the two calculations. Both point to a large shortfall; the disagreement over denominators and coverage prevents a single exact number from being passed between them without explanation.166

The governments also disagreed about what the shortfall meant for their next steps. In January 2022, China’s commerce ministry emphasized the pandemic, recession and disrupted supply chains while describing normal communication over implementation. In March, Katherine Tai told the Senate Finance Committee that China had not met its purchase commitments and connected her administration’s response to a broader approach involving domestic investment and cooperation with allies. Continued discussion was real, but the available public accounts do not establish an agreed extension of the numerical 2020–2021 target. The agreement’s expectation of a continuing trajectory in 2022–2025 was a different provision.167

China’s April 2025 white paper developed its defense further. It described implementation measures and an extensive sequence of review calls, and asserted that neither side had formally activated the bilateral dispute procedure. It invoked unforeseen events in explaining difficulties. That argument must be compared with the text: Article 7.6.2 provides for consultation when an unforeseeable event outside the parties’ control delays timely compliance. It does not automatically waive the obligation. The white paper’s reference to Article 7.6.1 is inconsistent with the numbered clause in the agreement. Its reported meeting totals are evidence of China’s public account, not independently verified minutes of each exchange.168

Here Solomon’s emphasis on the relationship after agreement remains useful, but insufficient. China could invoke the surrounding relationship and American restrictions to contest responsibility. The United States could retain pressure while deciding how much enforcement it was willing to risk. Yet the dispute now concerned measurable purchases, competing definitions of the benchmark, a negotiated unforeseeable-event clause and distinct enforcement routes. The analytical work is to connect those instruments to the parties’ behavior, rather than to explain away the arithmetic as a difference in national attitudes toward promises.

Enforcement creates another negotiation

The United States initiated its new Section 301 investigation on October 24, 2025. It asked whether China had denied the benefits of Phase One, what burden nonimplementation imposed on American commerce and what response might be appropriate. The notice requested concrete examples and possible duties, service restrictions or import restrictions. It also recorded a request for consultations under Section 303(a) of the American Trade Act. That request cannot be silently relabeled an appeal under the agreement’s Chapter 7. A domestic investigation, a bilateral appeal and ordinary implementation reviews are different procedures even when they address some of the same conduct.169

The December hearing exposed disagreement among intended beneficiaries of enforcement. American Soybean Association representative Josh Gackle criticized implementation but worried that the investigation could jeopardize current progress with China. Renewable Fuels Association president Geoff Cooper supported the investigation and measures to secure access. The contrast was not between one industry interested in trade and another indifferent to it. They faced different markets and judged the risks of renewed pressure differently. A government seeking to impose costs on China also had to consider costs to constituencies expecting Chinese purchases.170

That concern had a concrete setting. The White House’s November 2025 account of a new deal described soybean purchases and reciprocal policy measures, including suspension of the maritime and shipbuilding Section 301 action. The existence of that suspension does not establish suspension of the separate Phase One investigation. In February 2026, China’s commerce ministry defended its implementation, blamed American restrictions for obstacles, and said Phase One had been discussed during the five negotiating rounds in 2025. It warned against additional measures. Old obligations and new accommodations were being discussed together, without a public instrument showing that the old dispute had been extinguished.171

The published records examined here establish an investigation and a contested response. They do not supply a documented final enforcement settlement.172

The 2026 mechanism was itself an unfinished bargain

The May 2026 summit generated another ambitious American account of agricultural commitments and two proposed institutions: a Board of Trade and a Board of Investment. The White House described at least $17 billion in agricultural purchases annually in 2027 and 2028, with 2026 prorated, additional to the soybean commitments made in October 2025. China’s May statements described preliminary results, further work on the mechanisms and agricultural targets whose achievement required suitable conditions. The different formulations need to remain visible. An American fact sheet was a public claim about the deal, not a jointly signed schedule of completed transactions.173

By June, there was an observable American step beyond announcing a mechanism. USTR requested public comments on reciprocal managed trade in nonsensitive products. It asked for product classifications, recent trade values and assessments of effects on producers, workers and consumers. It also asked how frequently the Board should meet, when its product coverage should change and how the parties should exchange data. The contemplated arrangement involved equal-value trade and tariff modifications subject to agreement and applicable law. Comments were due July 10, with responses due July 27.174

The Chinese side subsequently described its own consultation. At the July 23 State Council Information Office briefing, commerce official Meng Huating said the teams were discussing the Board’s structure, functions and mode of operation, and exploring reciprocal tariff reductions covering $30 billion on each side. China, she said, was soliciting views from domestic businesses, associations, local governments and American-invested businesses and associations. Specific product arrangements still had to be agreed and implemented. The paired accounts identify channels through which preferences could enter the governments’ preparation, while leaving their influence and the Board’s final design unestablished.175

Even the agricultural total remained a matter for careful attribution. On July 2, a Reuters question described the $17 billion figure as including soybeans and asked about purchase timing and tariffs. The commerce ministry’s answer emphasized a guiding target and autonomous commercial decisions without affirming that characterization of the total. The White House’s formulation cannot therefore be treated as a verified joint definition. Likewise, a May American statement that China would review additional biotechnology traits supplies neither a list of approved events nor a set of new certificates.176

These developments extend the chapter’s earlier distinctions. Summit announcements set public tasks for officials. Work on products, values and operating rules sought to turn a broad understanding into a usable arrangement. Actual tariff modifications, purchases and committee operation would be further events requiring their own evidence. Treating every stage as the same completed agreement would reproduce the very confusion the earlier cases reveal.

The revision to Solomon is therefore neither the abandonment of his attention to implementation nor its indiscriminate confirmation. The continuing relationship plainly matters. It can sustain review, supply reasons to resist enforcement and create opportunities to bargain again. But performance varies across obligations within that relationship. China accelerated a published securities timetable; a firm obtained permission, full ownership and revenue. Biotechnological permissions eventually enabled identifiable commercial plans while procedural timing remained contested. Purchase commitments fell far short under more than one benchmark. The parties then negotiated new mechanisms while disputing the old ones.

The most useful question after signature is consequently concrete: what decision would turn this obligation into a benefit, who controls that decision, and what happened when it was requested? That question makes genuine implementation visible. It also makes obstruction testable, without inferring its motive from every delay or mistaking another ceremony for delivery.

7. Rival, partner, supplier

On October 23, 2024, Narendra Modi and Xi Jinping met in Kazan and welcomed an agreement concerning their disputed border. The arrangement had been announced two days earlier. Officials had already negotiated it through diplomatic and military channels; the leaders endorsed it and directed further work. A reader beginning with the summit photograph could easily reverse the sequence and assign the agreement to the encounter that publicly affirmed its political importance.177

The distinction matters beyond the timing of one meeting. In this case, China negotiated with a neighboring rival that had made improvement of the wider relationship contingent on conditions at the border. India invoked past agreements, demanded respect for principles, limited disclosures about operational details and kept negotiators within a hierarchy. Several practices familiar from Solomon’s account of Chinese behavior were resources available to the counterpart too.

Russia offers a different comparison. An improving political relationship accompanied a boundary settlement implemented through successive agreements, fieldwork and arrangements for local residents. Later, close political relations sustained both a working gas pipeline and prolonged disagreement over another. A counterpart could be a strategic partner, a former territorial adversary and a supplier with which commercial terms remained unresolved. The label attached to the relationship did not determine the terms of each transaction.

These comparisons change an American-centered explanation in two ways. They make the counterpart’s conduct part of the mechanism, rather than background to China’s tactics. They also expose differences in what can be divided and exchanged: separation of troops, restoration of access, territorial jurisdiction, local economic use, prices and future purchases. The same language of friendship or principle can accompany different obligations because different interests are at stake.

These are selected comparisons across different periods, institutions and bargaining objects. They can reveal a mechanism outside the American relationship, a condition under which it fails, or reciprocal conduct obscured by a Chinese-centered account. They cannot isolate a national effect.

India: making the relationship conditional

The 2020 confrontation occurred within an elaborate framework of earlier agreements. This was not a relationship waiting for its first statement of principles. The 2005 agreement on political parameters protected the broader relationship from boundary differences while also requiring respect for the Line of Actual Control and maintenance of peace pending a settlement. A comprehensive boundary settlement and restraint before settlement were separate tasks, contained in the same document.178

That separation left room for disagreement over political emphasis. Beijing could argue that an unresolved boundary should not obstruct other cooperation. New Delhi could insist that peace along the line was a condition for such cooperation. Each position could invoke part of a previously accepted framework. Establishing that a party had distorted the common record would require more than observing that it emphasized the provision most favorable to its current purpose.

Institutions also divided the work. The Working Mechanism for Consultation and Coordination, established in 2012, joined diplomats with military representatives and provided for emergency consultations. Its founding agreement expressly excluded resolution of the boundary question and protected the separate role of the Special Representatives. A meeting of that mechanism, a military commanders’ meeting and a leaders’ encounter therefore had different subjects and capacities. Counting all three as occasions of contact would conceal much of what made a proposed arrangement usable.179

Following the confrontation and deadly Galwan clashes, the September 2020 Moscow understanding directed the sides toward disengagement, appropriate distance between forces, respect for earlier agreements and continued diplomatic channels. The sixth corps-commanders meeting, on September 21, added commitments against further frontline deployment and unilateral changes. The broad political direction was meaningful, but it did not publish a complete withdrawal map or settle every location. Subsequent agreements had to define how it would operate.180

There was progress, followed by further obstruction. The February 2021 joint statement welcomed completed disengagement at Pangong while reserving remaining issues. After the thirteenth military round in October, India’s account blamed China for rejecting its constructive proposals. That accusation belongs in the history; it identifies a failed round and India’s public explanation. Without the proposals and instructions, it does not establish which Chinese official blocked a possible bargain or whether disagreement arose within the Chinese leadership.181

Saheb Singh Chadha’s interview-based reconstruction helps explain the institutional setting. He describes political meetings establishing a framework, diplomats elaborating steps and commanders negotiating their application. On the Indian side, he reports that meetings of the China Study Group set the limits within which the corps commander could negotiate. The account supplies a concrete alternative to inferring Chinese political conflict whenever talks stalled: the officials at the table could be working within limits set on both sides. Chadha did not interview Chinese officials, and his anonymous interview evidence cannot recover Beijing’s parallel mandate.182

The March 2022 visit of Wang Yi to New Delhi demonstrated the limits of senior access. Jaishankar publicly described three hours of discussion and acknowledged earlier partial progress, while emphasizing that implementation had moved more slowly than India wanted. He rejected the characterization of the relationship as normal while large border deployments persisted. The visit established a channel and a continuing negotiation. It did not itself purchase the broader normality Beijing sought.183

Here the relationship became an instrument used by India. A Chinese appeal to the larger benefits of cooperation encountered an Indian insistence on the security conditions under which those benefits could be pursued. This resembles the relational bargaining Solomon described, but reverses the direction of the pressure. The observation does not make the two governments’ practices identical in frequency or effect. It makes attribution to a specifically Chinese repertoire insufficient as an explanation of this exchange.

A local bargain and a larger disagreement

The agreement at Gogra–Hot Springs, known to India as Patrol Point 15, shows what the public language of disengagement could contain. The July 2022 military communiqué promised continued work; the September announcements identified a local result. The Chinese publication used the name Jianan Daban. Different place names did not by themselves establish different obligations.184

India’s September 9 explanation supplied the operational detail. Disengagement had begun at 08:30 the previous day and was to finish by September 12. Both sides would cease forward deployments in a phased, coordinated and verified process. Temporary structures and associated infrastructure would be dismantled, with mutual verification, and the terrain restored to its earlier condition. The October diplomatic release subsequently described completion of the process.185

These were reciprocal, timed duties. They complicate an inference of operational vagueness drawn from a brief communiqué. They also leave an evidentiary distinction: the Indian explanation is an official account of the agreement, rather than the authenticated protocol, its maps or the verification logs. It establishes the government’s stated understanding of the local bargain. It does not measure every change in access or independently confirm every troop movement.

The result did not settle what improvement at one location should mean for the relationship. In late September, Chinese ambassador Sun Weidong presented movement from emergency handling toward routine management and advocated renewed exchanges. India’s October response rejected treating the border process as complete. It continued to distinguish disengagement from de-escalation and restored normality. Both sides could acknowledge a local step while disagreeing over whether that step justified wider concessions.186

That is an important form of bargaining after agreement. The disputed question was the sufficiency of the completed step, not necessarily whether the parties had performed it. If one side regarded an arrangement as a reason to resume wider cooperation and the other regarded it as one installment in a larger security process, continuing negotiation did not by itself demonstrate repudiation. The historian must recover the original scope before deciding that a later demand reopened it.

The following year provides a useful check on expectations of senior intervention. After an extended military round in August 2023, India said that a Modi–Xi conversation at BRICS had produced instructions to intensify efforts toward disengagement and de-escalation. China’s account stressed the broader relationship and appropriate handling of the boundary issue. Yet October’s military statement still referred to remaining questions. India’s spokesperson also resisted a questioner’s suggestion that the round had produced no consensus.187

The sequence allows neither an inevitable-breakthrough narrative nor a simple declaration of total failure. Senior contact and positive language did not establish immediate closure. The later statement did not establish that nothing had moved. Most importantly, no authenticated draft was recovered that would allow a full test of Solomon’s combined attention to senior intervention and concrete drafting. The visible signal was weaker than the information a forecaster would need.

Endorsement after agreement

The 2024 sequence is clearer about the relationship between channels. In July, the foreign ministers met in Astana and Vientiane. India’s Astana account described agreement to intensify diplomatic and military contacts. Subsequent working-mechanism meetings explicitly linked their activity to the ministerial discussions; the August accounts reported narrower differences. Doval and Wang then discussed progress in September. These records identify work passing between levels, although the two governments continued to emphasize different aspects of it.188

On October 21, India announced a patrolling arrangement. Its next briefing dated the agreement to early that morning and said earlier disengagement arrangements had not been reopened. China confirmed agreement through diplomatic and military communication while declining to provide operational details. India’s foreign secretary declined to speculate about why the Chinese position had changed. The restraint is analytically valuable: a participant’s public announcement of success did not purport to reveal the other side’s internal cause.189

Original Indian reporting described coordinated patrol schedules, advance communication and limits on patrol-party sizes. Those details, attributed to unnamed sources, suggested how access could resume while reducing the risk of encounters. They are plausible mechanics rather than an authenticated annex. They should not be expanded into a claim that every earlier buffer arrangement had disappeared or that the entire pre-2020 military situation had been restored.190

The October 23 summit followed. India described endorsement of the arrangement and direction for the Special Representatives to meet; China welcomed progress while emphasizing the protection of the wider relationship. The anticipated meeting may have supplied an incentive or a deadline. The public sequence establishes endorsement after the bargain, rather than the summit itself producing a bargain still absent when the leaders met. Russia’s hosting role likewise supplies no evidence of Russian mediation.191

In December, Jaishankar told Parliament that verification patrols had been followed by regular activity under the understanding and that patrolling and grazing access had been agreed in Depsang and Demchok. He characterized disengagement as complete and de-escalation as the next task. He also described temporary restraints elsewhere as dependent on local conditions and open to later review.192

The distinction between disengagement and de-escalation is substantive. Separating forces at a confrontation point reduces one danger. Reducing or relocating the larger concentration of forces concerns another. Neither automatically settles the boundary. In this sequence, the governments could make progress on access and local separation while continuing to negotiate force posture and the political relationship.

By August 2025, their paired accounts expressly put de-escalation at the stage of discussing principles and modalities. They also commissioned new work: an expert group under the diplomatic mechanism to explore early results in boundary delimitation, a border-management working group and general-level military mechanisms for additional sectors. India’s account simultaneously listed steps toward renewed travel, trade and other exchanges.193

This qualifies a rigid reading of India’s earlier insistence on sequencing. Relational recovery could proceed alongside unfinished military adjustment. The public record does not show India simply abandoning its security condition; it shows that both the condition and the permitted response to partial progress had to be interpreted. By May 2026, paired working-mechanism accounts described continuing work on delimitation, management and cooperation. They establish newly commissioned technical activity, not completed delimitation or a formally amended 2012 agreement.194

The Indian case consequently leaves a mixed result that a single success label cannot capture. Local bargains produced officially reported separation and restored activity in specified areas. They did not settle sovereignty or establish a complete return to an earlier military baseline. Political authority, technical work and conditional cooperation interacted on both sides. No public evidence examined here identifies Chinese leadership conflict as the necessary cause of delay, or the independent effect of a summit on a particular concession.

Russia: dividing sovereignty from use

The Russian boundary case begins from a different relationship and reaches a different kind of outcome. The parties ultimately agreed a territorial line. Their progress also demonstrates that detailed, flexible arrangements were available well before Xi’s leadership.

In the published Chinese selection of Deng Xiaoping’s May 1989 conversation with Mikhail Gorbachev, a forceful account of historical injury accompanied willingness to use existing treaties as the basis for settlement. Deng allowed each side to state its historical view without requiring an answer or debate, while recalling earlier Chinese demands for acknowledgment of unequal treaties. The memoir-derived Russian account preserves Gorbachev’s disagreement with the historical narrative alongside agreement to normalize relations. Neither source is an unedited contemporary transcript.195

The juxtaposition nevertheless matters. A demand for acknowledgment, a demand for territorial restitution and an opportunity to state a grievance are different acts. The presence of historical grievance does not identify the concession being demanded in return. In this instance, the published Chinese position contained both condemnation of the past and a basis on which practical negotiation could proceed.

The May 18 joint communiqué contemplated simultaneous settlement of the eastern and western border sectors. The eastern agreement was nevertheless signed separately on May 16, 1991, with two sections expressly reserved. The western-sector agreement followed in 1994. The observable package had become divisible.196

Why it became divisible is more contested. Taylor Fravel argues that domestic insecurity can increase the value of neighbors’ cooperation and encourage territorial compromise; he interprets the 1991 sequencing in light of Chinese priorities after Tiananmen. Akihiro Iwashita’s reconstruction places substantive convergence in talks reopened in 1987, drawing substantially on later participant accounts. Earlier progress does not disprove a later effect of domestic crisis on package separation. It does mean that all flexibility cannot be dated to that crisis. The record still needs the intervening offers and instructions to distinguish an accelerated decision from completion of work already underway.197

The 1991 agreement is more revealing about the mechanism accepted than about its decisive cause. It established an equal joint commission to identify channels, allocate river islands, erect markers and prepare documents and maps. It supplied criteria for river channels and rules concerning subsequent natural changes. It also addressed navigation separately, including passage from the Ussuri past Khabarovsk into the Amur. Difficult sections could remain unresolved while other interests received specified protection.198

Implementation then encountered constituencies whose concerns were more concrete than the tone of bilateral relations. Farms, river use, border facilities and regional authority were affected by the line. Iwashita reconstructs resistance in Primorye and differing approaches among Russian regional leaders. Federal officials did not face a uniform regional objection; they worked with some local authorities while encountering resistance from others. The completion announcement in 1997 concerned the sectors already covered, leaving the reserved areas outstanding.199

The Menkeseli arrangement made one way through these difficulties visible. According to Iwashita, Russian border and regional authorities proposed allowing established Russian users to continue local activity under Chinese sovereignty. His account traces acceptance of the idea into a 1997 framework and a detailed agreement in December 1999. The proposal’s origin remains an attributed reconstruction. The published agreement independently establishes the structure the governments accepted.200

Its definition of joint economic use was precise: the sovereign state permitted established residents of the other country to pursue specified traditional activities. Named areas under Chinese sovereignty were opened to Russian users, and a named Russian area to Chinese users. Eligibility was restricted to prior users, whose numbers could not increase. Haymaking, fishing and farming were specified; the host state’s development rights remained intact. Users needed documents from their own state and permission from the host, whose law continued to apply.201

The arrangement thus separated jurisdiction from selected benefits of use. A territorial decision need not extinguish every economic practice of the people affected by it. This could make implementation more acceptable without creating shared sovereignty. Its limits were part of that accommodation: a five-year use period, dates to be agreed diplomatically, inspections and consultations at least twice a year. The recovered publication omits the two annexes, so the exact maps, numerical limits and permit procedures cannot be verified here. Nor does the instrument establish how many residents actually benefited.

This is a particularly useful extension of Solomon’s implementation question. Later bargaining can distribute the costs of carrying out an agreement. It can specify a benefit that helps the legal settlement work. Calling all such negotiation Chinese insistence on accommodation would obscure its reciprocal and agreed character. The Russian proposal and the bilateral duties matter to the explanation.

The comparison with India also has a firm boundary. Restoring an activity under another state’s agreed sovereignty differs from coordinating patrols along a disputed line. Both arrangements separate a local practice from a larger conflict, but only the former operates on the premise that jurisdiction over the relevant area has been determined. Similar techniques do not make the outcomes equivalent.

What authorization looked like

Political reassurance preceded final territorial closure. Article 6 of the 2001 friendship treaty stated that the parties had no mutual territorial claims while directing continued negotiation of unagreed boundary alignments and maintenance of the status quo. The sentence accommodated a broad assurance and an expressly unfinished task. Reading the assurance alone would manufacture a contradiction when boundary talks continued.202

The remaining settlement in 2004 supplies unusually concrete evidence of a mandate. A Russian presidential order dated October 13 accepted a foreign-ministry proposal coordinated with federal authorities and three affected regional executives. It authorized negotiation and signature of attached drafts, allowing changes that did not alter their principles. The attachments included the supplementary boundary agreement and related navigation and cooperation instruments.203

This is the kind of evidence that improves on an inference from senior presence. It identifies an instrument, a responsible organization and the bounds of discretion. It is also Russian evidence. It does not disclose the parallel Chinese approval sequence, and its availability in a present-day legal reproduction does not establish when an outside observer could have inspected it before signature.

The next day, foreign ministers Li Zhaoxing and Sergei Lavrov signed the supplementary agreement. It defined the remaining alignments, including land segments through islands that had previously been reserved, and assigned further fieldwork to the joint commission. The settlement reduced China’s claimed area while requiring Russia to relinquish control in selected places. The two concessions use different baselines. Measuring only territory transferred from Russian control would miss what China relinquished from its claim; measuring only the retreat from China’s claim would miss the material change for Russia.204

The agreement also had a contested Russian reception. Contemporary reporting describes legislative opposition in Khabarovsk, a governor defending local interests, protests and disagreement during ratification. Officials invoked agriculture, transport, migration and military considerations. The record does not support treating Russia’s interest as the uncontested preference of a single actor, or China’s bargaining success as simply exploiting a helpless counterpart.205

Signature was followed by legislative approvals and entry into force in June 2005. The supplementary descriptive protocol and annexes came in July 2008; the October exchange of notes brought those instruments into force, accompanied by the handover ceremony. Agreement on a line, legal effectiveness, field demarcation and material transfer were separate accomplishments.206

The intervening years do not show that the 2004 agreement was merely symbolic. They show work still required by a settlement of this kind. Equally, the existence of the later ceremony does not independently verify every marker or establish that all local opposition disappeared. The strongest result is a documented progression through distinct commitments and implementation stages.

Fravel expressly excludes the 2004 compromise from his domestic-insecurity explanation. It is therefore improper to enlist his theory as a universal account of the Russian sequence. Improved relations, the value of closure, changes in Russian federal authority and expectations about future power may all matter. The documents examined here identify accepted arrangements and one side’s mandate more securely than they rank those causes. The case establishes old capacities for compromise and technical specification; it leaves a narrower causal question about why particular terms became acceptable at particular moments.

Gas: political value and commercial terms

The gas negotiations bring the limits of political closeness into sharper view. They also change what power means. A country’s diplomatic importance and aggregate resources do not determine whether it can redirect gas, finance a pipeline or find another purchaser on comparable terms. Those alternatives depend on the project.

Four projects must remain separate. The eastern route, later known as Power of Siberia, drew on eastern Siberian resources and became an operating supply relationship. The original western or Altai proposal envisaged a different connection toward Xinjiang. A subsequent Far Eastern contract was another transaction. The later Power of Siberia 2 proposal involved transit through Mongolia and a much larger prospective volume. A new name or ceremonial announcement could not make their infrastructure and commitments interchangeable.207

The eastern bargain already had substantial momentum before Russia’s 2014 confrontation with the West. In September 2013, with Putin and Xi present, Gazprom and CNPC agreed principal terms. Contemporary reporting described the document as legally binding while explicitly reserving price. Gazprom hoped to complete the contract by year-end. That target passed.208

This is an early negative observation for any shortcut from leader sponsorship to imminent closure. It also explains why the word binding cannot classify a whole project. Parties can bind themselves on particular questions while leaving a commercially indispensable term open. Without the 2013 instrument, its precise obligations cannot be reconstructed; the contemporary account nevertheless makes the reserved question explicit.

Price remained unresolved on the first day of Putin’s May 2014 visit. On May 21, the parties announced the thirty-year eastern supply contract. The Chinese account envisaged an annual build-up to 38 billion cubic metres and divided construction responsibilities: Gazprom would develop Russian production, processing and transmission, while CNPC would build the Chinese pipeline and associated storage. The contract itself remained unpublished.209

Putin’s public explanation combined the language of friendship with hard bargaining. Experts had worked until 3:30 in the morning. Both sides, he said, had compromised. Government decisions still had to follow. His account was a participant’s presentation of success, but it did not depict political friendship as eliminating commercial disagreement. Later in the exchange, he corrected his earlier description of the pricing mechanism: it was linked to a basket of oil and petroleum products.210

That correction matters more than the headline valuation. Gazprom’s $400 billion figure did not disclose the coefficients, review provisions or purchase obligations. Dividing a lifetime value by maximum volumes would produce an arithmetic average, not the actual contract price. The deteriorating Russian relationship with the West provided a consequential setting; without the formula and earlier offers, it cannot establish the discount China obtained because of that change.211

Financing remained another margin. Two days after signature, Gazprom Export chief Alexander Medvedev described a $25 billion prepayment as agreed in principle, with details under discussion. In November, Gazprom’s Alexei Miller said an advance had been considered in connection with a lower price but was no longer under consideration after price settlement. This is evidence that a financing option disappeared. An unpublished, incompletely specified option should not be converted into a binding Chinese payment obligation and then counted as breached.212

The implementation record was substantial. A December 2015 corporate release described further agreements on the cross-border section, communications, technical procedures and working groups. A delivery-start date was announced in 2017; actual supply began in December 2019. By April 2026, Gazprom’s reported record included 38.8 billion cubic metres delivered during 2025 and arrangements to maintain specially agreed supply during maintenance. These are company-reported operational results, rather than an independent audit of every contractual duty, but they establish considerably more than ceremonial alignment.213

The projects that did not follow the same path

On the original western route, the November 2014 framework and May 2015 heads of agreement did not quickly become a sales contract. The same December 2015 release that described implementation work on the eastern route scheduled further western commercial negotiations. Within a single relationship and even a single corporate announcement, completed and unfinished bargains coexisted.214

Keun-Wook Paik’s contemporary analysis treated stronger political support as grounds for expecting progress on the western project. Read beside the subsequent record, that expectation is useful because it did not establish the outcome. Senior support belongs in the history of incomplete projects as well as successful ones. Excluding the delayed rounds would make a forecasting rule look stronger by selecting only the agreements it appeared to predict.

The later Mongolia proposal also changed the commercial problem. Vitaly Yermakov and Michal Meidan distinguish engineering work in Mongolia from the Chinese supply agreement still required, and explain why eastern and western resources could not simply be redirected without new infrastructure. Their demand scenarios and estimates of pricing parameters remain analytical projections. Their central geographic distinction is essential: national export dependence is not the same as an immediately available route to a particular customer.215

China did accept another transaction. In February 2022, Gazprom announced a second long-term sales agreement for the Far Eastern route, initially adding ten billion cubic metres annually. The same release’s account of a completed feasibility study for the Mongolia project did not turn that study into another supply contract. These different stages caution against interpreting every announcement as either empty politics or a completed sale.216

In March 2023, Putin said almost all parameters of the Power of Siberia 2 agreement had been agreed. The joint statement committed to advancing research and consultation on the trilateral pipeline. The unilateral and common formulations carried different information. Neither published an agreed price, financing arrangement or purchase schedule.217

Reporting in 2024 attributed difficult price and purchase-volume demands to China. Its source chain matters: the accessible Reuters article summarized a Financial Times report based on unnamed sources. These were not two independent inspections of a Chinese term sheet. The account points to a plausible dispute over the distribution of price and demand risk, while leaving the actual offers inaccessible.218

In September 2025, Miller announced a legally binding memorandum concerning construction of Power of Siberia 2 and Soyuz Vostok, with prospective capacity of fifty billion cubic metres a year. He separately announced increases for the eastern and Far Eastern routes. The memorandum deserves recognition as an announced advance. Its full text was not recovered, however, so its enforceable duties, conditions and remedies remain unknown. Capacity is also different from a buyer’s minimum purchase commitment.219

The strongest evidence of the later stage comes from explicit statements rather than the absence of a celebratory announcement. On September 3, 2026, Deputy Prime Minister Alexander Novak said commercial terms were still being discussed and final routing would depend on agreement between Gazprom and CNPC. The next day he described continuing preparation of the contract and government assistance toward concrete company agreement. Russian reports used the name Power of Baikal. The publicly described political commitment had still not become a completed commercial package at that point.220

Chinese planning documents supply context rather than a hidden negotiating mandate. The March 2026 national plan distinguished construction of the Far Eastern pipeline from preparatory work on the middle-route project. The July oil and gas plan emphasized domestic production, diversified imports and flexible operation. Its annex covered interprovincial Chinese pipelines, so the absence of an international project from that list cannot establish rejection. These materials identify the buyer’s stated supply priorities; they do not disclose its reservation price.221

The case is consequently consistent with a buyer valuing a strategic relationship while retaining alternatives and resisting an unattractive distribution of project risks. It does not demonstrate that every month of delay was deliberately chosen to exploit Russian weakness. Route design, future demand, financing and the terms on which dedicated infrastructure would operate remained material questions. A stronger claim about tactical patience would require an otherwise acceptable offer deliberately withheld to extract a documented concession from a counterpart facing a deadline.

Nor does the unfinished project erase successful supply. The eastern pipeline’s operating record belongs beside the delayed western proposals. Their coexistence is the explanatory result: close relations can preserve a reason to continue negotiating while leaving the parties unable to agree on a particular transaction. Political value can sustain a process without determining its price.

What the comparisons change

Solomon’s attention to the purpose of the relationship survives these comparisons. Its content becomes more specific. India used the prospect of wider cooperation to insist on border conditions. Russia sought to preserve local interests within a territorial settlement and later pursued commercial projects within a valuable strategic relationship. China faced counterparts making their own linkages, preserving their own reservations and operating through their own authorization procedures. Relationship management was interactive.

The comparisons also change the interpretation of partial agreement. Local separation can coexist with an unsettled boundary. Agreed sovereignty can coexist with regulated foreign use. A binding instrument can leave price unsettled. A signed supply contract can require later engineering agreements. Continued discussion in these settings may carry out the accepted division of work rather than weaken it. This supports Solomon’s observation that implementation remains a negotiating arena, while leaving his stronger claim about asymmetric Chinese demands for adherence unproved by the mere continuation of talks.

Senior authority remains consequential, but the available evidence distinguishes its functions. The Russian order specified drafts and delegated discretion. The 2024 Modi–Xi meeting publicly endorsed an earlier arrangement. Repeated gas endorsements supported projects whose commercial questions remained open. These observations narrow the practical use of Solomon’s claim that senior intervention is the most reliable sign of closure. They show why an analyst must ask what the leader actually authorized.222

Finally, detailed institutional accommodation is not a recent invention. The 1991 boundary commission and 1999 local-use agreement predate the later developments in legal and regulatory bargaining examined in this book. Freeman had already identified specialization and adaptation to different counterparts. The modern task is to explain when a particular capability reached a decision and what it made possible, rather than narrate a simple passage from personal relationships to institutions.223

The resulting account travels beyond the American case through questions more precise than whether China was patient, rigid or friendly. Which interests could be separated? Which earlier condition remained binding? Who could authorize the next step? What alternative made postponement tolerable? The Indian and Russian records provide different answers. Their variation is part of the behavior to be explained, not an inconvenience to be removed from a national portrait.

Conclusion: What survives Solomon

An agreement with China can accomplish something important without accomplishing everything its sponsors hoped for. American aircrew could leave Hainan while the governments continued to disagree about the collision, reconnaissance and the aircraft. A securities firm could obtain full ownership and earn revenue while other financial activities still required permission. Chinese and Russian companies could operate one gas pipeline while remaining unable to complete the commercial terms for another. These are different outcomes, but they expose the same analytical mistake: treating the political relationship, the signed instrument and the benefit expected from it as one thing.

Solomon understood much of this problem. His account directed attention beyond the negotiating table to the political purpose of the relationship, the authority behind a delegation and the struggle over implementation. Those remain indispensable questions. The subsequent history changes the answers in particular settings and demands greater precision about what the questions are supposed to explain. Relationships can create access without producing a mandate. A leader can endorse an earlier bargain, authorize a new one or instruct subordinates to keep trying. Further negotiation can fulfill an agreement, address something it expressly reserved or contest whether a promise was kept. None of these distinctions is supplied by the word friendship.

The strongest conclusion of this book is therefore conditional but substantial. Chinese officials continue to bargain within politically defined relationships and hierarchies of authority. Within those constraints, the institutional developments examined here have expanded particular opportunities to make commitments usable: WTO adjudication after accession, the climate reporting architecture and explicit bilateral implementation remedies. Legal argument, regulatory timetables, application procedures, verification arrangements and commercial risk allocation can determine what a political understanding is worth. They can also become the subjects of the next negotiation. The evidence supports that development more securely than it supports a single national trajectory toward flexibility, rigidity, legalism or deception.

What remains useful

The first durable insight is that a negotiation has a political purpose larger than the item under discussion. This does not mean that every issue is exchanged for every other issue. It means that the parties’ willingness to sustain a channel, separate a dispute or accept a limited result depends partly on what they want from the relationship.

In 1978, the Chinese decision to establish diplomatic relations could coexist with opposition to future American arms sales to Taiwan. That disagreement was not secretly resolved by the communiqué. In 2023, diplomatic engagement resumed while Beijing and Washington continued to disagree over technology restrictions, Taiwan and the balloon. The two moments belong to different strategic settings; their common feature is the possibility of proceeding on a specified task without obtaining agreement on the whole relationship. Solomon’s own confrontation caveat makes the change of setting something to investigate, rather than an automatic refutation of his account.224

The second durable insight concerns authority. The person explaining a position may not have permission to change it. Technical competence and negotiating discretion are different capacities. The accession papers show why Americans sought Zhu Rongji’s intervention, but also why the appearance of the premier cannot carry the whole explanation: detailed offers and conditional packages existed before the closing encounter. In the Russian border case, an actual presidential order identified drafts, the organization authorized to negotiate and the permitted bounds of revision. It is Russian evidence, but it shows the kind of record needed to make authority an explanation rather than a synonym for seniority.225

The third concerns the work after a political agreement. The book repeatedly finds additional decisions between commitment and benefit: registration and licensing after market opening, an import certificate before a crop launch, demarcation after agreement on a border, a sales contract after a pipeline memorandum. Some were contemplated in the original bargain. Others became disputed obstacles. Keeping those possibilities separate makes performance more visible. It also makes a criticism of nonperformance more exact.

The fourth is attention to the counterpart’s own words and commitments. Negotiators benefit from remembering which principles were accepted and what a prior visitor said. But a record is useful only if its scope is preserved. A conditional Chinese offer in November 1999 cannot be converted into an unconditional commitment by deleting the package condition. An American claim that additional tariffs were paused in May 2018 cannot be read into a joint statement that did not say so. Studying the record tests both governments’ use of memory; it does not grant either side ownership of the past.226

These insights do not require the claim that the conduct is unique to China. Solomon himself distinguished characteristic from exclusive practices. The Indian use of the wider relationship to insist on border conditions, American deadlines in accession talks and Russian limits on delegated authority do not disprove the existence of a Chinese repertoire. They do require the explanation to include the counterpart. A reciprocal bargaining process cannot be explained by describing only one party’s techniques.

How the older propositions should be used

Some findings change how an older proposition should be used; others document subsequent developments. A narrower interpretation is not, by itself, evidence that behavior changed over time.

The first caution concerns the treatment of senior intervention as a forecast. Solomon described it as the most reliable sign that agreement was imminent, in a discussion that also emphasized concrete drafting. That is stronger than the proposition that leaders sometimes matter. His own account of the unfinished 1973 claims/assets bargain places a failed expectation beside the general prediction.

The later record adds useful failed or incomplete observations. Senior access did not close the March 2000 EU accession negotiation. Xi’s June 2023 meeting with Blinken did not deliver the military-channel agreement Washington sought. Leader sponsorship accompanied gas arrangements that left price unsettled. In October 2024, the Modi–Xi encounter publicly endorsed a border arrangement already announced. None of these observations alone ranks the predictive reliability of every available signal, and not every episode supplies Solomon’s full conjunction of drafting and intervention. Together they require an analyst to identify what the intervention did before using the encounter as evidence of an approaching settlement.227

A second qualification concerns bargaining after agreement. The continuation of talks is abundantly observed. Solomon’s stronger claim combines that continuation with an asymmetry: demanding adherence from the counterpart while seeking accommodation for China. The first observation does not establish the second.

The distinction changes several cases. Negotiation over the EP-3 aircraft followed an immediate arrangement concerning the crew; it did not necessarily reopen a promise to release both together. Russia and China agreed to local economic uses under settled sovereignty, with consultations and permissions expressly built into the instrument. Phase One prescribed consultation and remedies when implementation was disputed. A later negotiation might exploit weakness or dilute an obligation, but that conclusion requires the initial duty, its conditions and the subsequent demand. It cannot be inferred from the existence of another meeting.228

A third qualification concerns what the modern evidence establishes about the work done by old friends. Kissinger’s July 2023 reception demonstrates that historical status retained political value. Chinese leaders sought his advocacy and treated him as someone who could contribute to a more favorable relationship. His access to the defense minister was especially conspicuous when an official American defense encounter had failed. The public record also identifies consultation between Kissinger and Blinken on China and artificial intelligence.

The evidence does not make the visit an authorized American negotiation, identify the operational instruction he transmitted or establish that he caused the November results. Formal American–Chinese channels were already active, and Blinken himself had met Xi the previous month. Nor does the missing private transmission record prove that Kissinger had no influence. The update is to distinguish the roles that the phrase old friend can conceal: honored visitor, source of advice, advocate to another government, carrier of information and authorized negotiator. Cultivating one role does not establish success in another.229

A fourth caution concerns explanations drawn from apparently inflexible behavior. A negotiator may have reached a mandate’s limit, await clearance, face a counterpart’s unacceptable condition or expect a better alternative. An internal political struggle is another possibility. Public rigidity rarely chooses among them. The recent cases provide much better evidence of official positions and some implementing decisions than of the distribution of preferences inside the Chinese leadership. Assigning a delay to factional conflict or Xi’s personal control without the intervening evidence would reproduce the very problem that a documentary update should solve.

The same applies to cultural explanations of apology, patience and face. In 2001, the two governments found language and presentation under which the crew could depart while their accounts of responsibility remained different. That is an important result. It does not independently establish that a particular translation overcame a cultural barrier, or that preserving face was the decisive cause of agreement. A practical account can explain the limited commitment the language supported while leaving the psychology less settled.

What has changed—and what was already there

The historical update must avoid moving the baseline forward and then claiming the remainder as a discovery. Solomon’s main evidence ended in 1984. Freeman’s 1999 essay already described specialization, institutional participation and adaptation to particular counterparts. The detailed Russian boundary instruments and conditional accession offers show that precision and flexibility also predate the most recent period. A more complex agreement in 2020 cannot, by itself, establish that Chinese negotiators had become more sophisticated since 1999.

There is nevertheless documented development. Post-accession legal work gave Chinese officials and counsel repeated opportunities to formulate arguments within an accepted adjudicative system. In DS379, some arguments prevailed and others failed. A subsequent signed procedural agreement specified time, sequencing and the handling of compliance disputes. The record establishes legal agency and negotiated procedure. It does not show that every favorable ruling created equivalent commercial relief, or that the particular skill was acquired at the moment it first became visible to an outside observer.230

Climate bargaining supplies a different development. From Copenhagen through Cancun to the bilateral announcements and Paris, the published instruments became more specific about national actions, consultation, reporting and review. The architecture combined common procedures with differentiation and preserved national discretion over an essential object: the nationally determined contribution. Adoption of that architecture was a substantive negotiated result. It was neither an unconditional surrender of discretion nor an empty declaration because achievement of each contribution was not made an internationally binding obligation. The terms describe what cooperation became possible.231

Phase One points in another direction. It used unusually detailed bilateral commitments and a procedure permitting a complainant to take proportionate remedial action under stated conditions, without delegating the dispute to an independent adjudicator. The parties preserved substantial room to assess the other’s conduct. Its specificity therefore coexisted with political judgment and threatened economic costs. This differs from the WTO route without establishing a linear historical retreat from law: the negotiations concerned different demands and occurred amid a different conflict, while other legal institutions continued to exist.

Implementation makes the variation concrete. China’s securities regulator moved the published date for removing an ownership ceiling from December to April 2020 and expressly attributed the change to Phase One. The subsequent company records trace permission into ownership and revenue. Biotechnology provisions specified administration of applications, and individual certificates eventually enabled identifiable launch plans. The broader timing obligation remained contested. Purchases fell far below the examined benchmarks, even though the American government’s later calculation and the independent tracker cannot be merged without resolving their coverage differences.232

Across these episodes, commitments can be specified and contested at several distinct points. A counterpart can ask about a regulator’s timetable, a certificate’s duration, the completion of an application, the scope of a statistical benchmark or the operation of a remedy. Each creates information that a general assessment of the political atmosphere cannot provide. Each also creates room for a narrower dispute inside an otherwise valuable relationship.

By July 2026, the proposed trade mechanism supplied another example. American requests for public comments and the Chinese account of consultation with domestic and American-invested businesses showed work on product coverage and operating arrangements. These were concrete steps in designing an institution. They were not evidence that an agreed product schedule was already in operation. The observable endpoint is a negotiation over how to manage selected exchanges, not proof that a new institution had solved the older dispute over performance.233

Power, interests and the limits of a period label

The modern cases contain more than new procedures. They occur in relationships with different resources, vulnerabilities and political purposes. Yet a country’s larger economy or a leader’s stronger formal position does not, on its own, reveal the price at which a particular bargain becomes acceptable.

Gas negotiations demonstrate why. A seller’s need for another market matters, but gas cannot reach every buyer through the same infrastructure. A proposed line can require decisions about routing, finance, minimum purchases and the allocation of future demand risk. Close political relations can make continued negotiation worthwhile without resolving those terms. The operating eastern pipeline and the unfinished later commercial package belong in the same explanation. Selecting only the unfinished project would exaggerate failure; selecting only the working one would make strategic friendship appear sufficient.

The Indian case makes another limitation visible. Restored access, separation at a confrontation point, wider de-escalation and a final boundary settlement are different benefits. India’s condition for improving the broader relationship could itself be interpreted as partial progress accumulated. Later discussions of de-escalation and practical exchanges did not amount to a complete settlement, nor did they erase the earlier use of security conditions. The relevant power operates through particular interests and alternatives, on both sides.

Those comparisons do not isolate a Xi effect. The Russian boundary arrangements were negotiated under earlier Chinese leadership; the Indian episodes concerned a military confrontation; the trade cases involved different American demands and instruments. A change across these episodes could reflect the leader, the issue, the counterpart, the international setting or several together. The formal institutional changes reviewed in the research establish changes in organization and procedure. They do not measure how quickly a difficult concession was cleared or whose objection prevailed in an unrecorded meeting.

That limit does not make the book indifferent to power or leadership. It identifies where those explanations must become concrete. Which alternative deteriorated? Which official received new discretion? Which demand was withdrawn or narrowed? Which cost became politically acceptable? The shift from demands for a larger trade settlement to a partial agreement is observable. How far it resulted from learning about resolve, increased economic costs or acceptance of a narrower package remains a question for evidence about choices. A theory of bargaining should direct that search rather than replace it.

How the revised account should be used

For a reader assessing a new negotiation, the history suggests a short sequence of questions. Begin with the benefit the agreement is supposed to deliver. Identify the authority that can make the next necessary decision. Read the accepted instrument for its conditions, reservations and procedures. Follow the decision through implementation. Then compare what occurred with the original obligation, preserving the distinction between an allegation, an official explanation and an independently observed result.

This approach changes the interpretation of familiar signals. A meeting with a senior leader is more informative when it can be connected to a changed instruction or specified draft. A detailed text is more useful when the official controlling implementation is identified. A successful application says more about that application than about an entire regulatory system. A delay is more diagnostic when the complete file, promised deadline and stated reason are available. A friendly intermediary’s reception becomes stronger evidence of policy influence when a transmitted proposal and a subsequent decision can be connected; explaining influence still requires evidence that the transmission changed the choice.

It also prevents cultural interpretation from carrying more weight than it can bear. Historical language, ceremonial treatment and public dignity belong in the account when they shape what participants say or can accept. Their presence is not proof of their decisive effect. Likewise, a counterpart’s use of deadlines or principles does not make Chinese practices unimportant; it allows the interaction to be reconstructed rather than assigned to one national character.

The accompanying assessment of all 59 baseline propositions makes this discipline explicit. It separates observable practices from claims about their origin, effectiveness or comparative prevalence. Many unresolved entries contain a well-supported component beside a stronger claim the sources cannot decide. An unvalidated forecast does not become a falsified one, and an older qualification does not become a new discovery merely because a later case illustrates it.234

That last distinction changes the assessment itself. Freeman already allowed relationship-building efforts to succeed, fail or have uncertain results. An unfinished gas bargain does not refute that qualified account. Solomon already distinguished political principles from exceptions and more detailed instruments. Their coexistence in a later agreement does not establish the disappearance of a general political preference. The update is strongest where it supplies particular new decisions and instruments, and weakest where it would convert missing comparative evidence into a verdict on a national tendency.

The unresolved questions are consequential. Private Chinese instructions would improve the account of spring 2019. Intermediary communications would help distinguish advocacy from transmitted influence in 2023. Complete application records could reveal whether agricultural delays reflected incomplete submissions, administrative capacity, policy resistance or a decision to preserve bargaining room. Those records would change particular judgments. Their absence does not nullify the signed terms, released internal papers, regulatory decisions and documented commercial stages already available.

Solomon’s achievement was to make the political process behind an agreement visible. The appropriate update preserves that ambition while changing the unit of explanation. The relationship establishes purposes and channels; an authorized commitment defines a task; another decision often makes its benefit usable. The chapters show how China has used these arrangements within the institutions and relationships examined, with different results across issues and counterparts. Understanding that variation is the most useful part of the inheritance—and the work that a national portrait alone cannot finish.

S-P10 — unresolved

Baseline claim (printed pp. 90–93, 97–100): PRC leaders use relations with third powers and rival foreign political figures to advertise alternatives, influence the counterpart’s sense of dependence, and improve their bargaining position.

Baseline scope and qualification: The proposed role of the March 1969 clashes in creating a wider coalition is conditional in Solomon’s account. S-P11 — unresolved

Baseline claim (printed pp. 75–76, 93–95): PRC officials may delay substantive movement until leadership conditions or the wider strategic context permit a more favorable agreement, preserving talks in the meantime.

Baseline scope and qualification: Observed slowness does not identify its cause. The chapter relies partly on hindsight and foreign officials’ interpretations of internal politics; apparent rigidity may reflect substantive preference rather than leadership conflict or a calculated delay.

Tested component: Deliberate preservation of talks until leadership or strategic circumstances improve.

Strongest relevant support: Talks and political endorsement continued while commercial closure remained incomplete. RUS-G14, RUS-G17, RUS-G28

Strongest contrary or limiting evidence: Technical review and a similarly long EU process illustrate alternatives to strategic waiting. AG09, AG16, AG05 A counterpart’s missing authorization also delayed settlement. EWT-01

Assessment and unresolved remainder: Duration and eventual agreement cannot identify intentional delay. Need earlier acceptable offers, instructions to wait and the strategic or leadership change that released them.

S-P12 — retain within tested scope

Baseline claim (printed pp. 95–96, 148, 150–151): PRC negotiators can obtain partial agreements while explicitly reserving unresolved positions, and can preserve compromise options through wording that avoids a conspicuous reversal.

Baseline scope and qualification: An explicit reservation is not concealment and does not show agreement on the reserved issue. Solomon admits that normalization was not a fully specified plan and contrasts political credibility concerns with commercial retreats. The Sun-tzu explanation is an analogy, not demonstrated transmission of a military rule.

Tested component: Partial agreement with explicitly reserved issues; face-preserving wording treated as a separate unproved component.

Strongest relevant support: Normalization proceeded while U.S. arms sales remained expressly disputed. NOR-170, NOR-172 Crew departure did not settle responsibility or all aircraft arrangements. EP303, EP306 Reserved boundary areas were settled in a later instrument. RUS-T1991-CN, RUS-T2004

Strongest contrary or limiting evidence: Enforcement was still substantively incomplete after the partial package was announced. TUS12 A partial announcement is not a completed agreement on its own reserved terms.

Assessment and unresolved remainder: Retain demonstrated partial settlement, not an inferred mechanism of saving face or concealed future concessions. Establish each reservation and its subsequent treatment separately.

S-P13 — retain within tested scope

Baseline claim (printed pp. 104–108): PRC negotiators can increase pressure through explicit threats, limited costly confrontations, or expanded demands, with the form and willingness to act changing as the relationship develops.

Baseline scope and qualification: Solomon states both that threats were sometimes abandoned and that some were carried out. The 1975 tour failed, and its factional purpose is disputed within the chapter. Neither threat issuance nor a canceled exchange proves bargaining success.

Tested component: Explicit pressure and conditional threats as conduct, not measured success or a national monopoly.

Strongest relevant support: China conditioned negotiated outcomes on the absence of additional U.S. sanctions/tariffs. CN-TRADE-STATEMENT20180603 Defense contact was publicly withheld under political conditions. C23-06, C23-11

Strongest contrary or limiting evidence: The United States also used and implemented tariff pressure; its deadlines and demands changed. TUS03, TUS04, TUS09

Assessment and unresolved remainder: Retain the repertoire. Specific Chinese concessions extracted through a threat, calibrated military coercion and relationship-driven changes in threat effectiveness require separate causal evidence.

S-P14 — unresolved

Baseline claim (printed pp. 100–102): Foreign officials invested in a positive relationship with China may receive concentrated pressure because PRC leaders expect them to work hardest to preserve it.

Baseline scope and qualification: Guanxi is Solomon’s principal cultural explanation, but reputational investment and expected policy influence offer practical alternatives. The chapter also reports PRC weakness in reaching hostile Americans, so concentrated contact may reflect access limitations rather than superior manipulation.

Tested component: Concentrated pressure on friendly officials because of their investment in the relationship.

Strongest relevant support: A sympathetic old interlocutor was urged to contribute to better relations. K23-01, K23-02

Strongest contrary or limiting evidence: Private status and acknowledged advice do not demonstrate pressured concession or fear of losing status. K23-04, K23-10

Assessment and unresolved remainder: No recovered comparison of friendly and hostile counterparts identifies differential pressure or its effect. Availability and policy affinity remain sufficient alternatives.

S-P15 — unresolved

Baseline claim (printed pp. 103–104, 106–108, 112): PRC leaders can invoke popular sentiment or the political vulnerability of accommodating officials as a limit on their flexibility and a reason for the counterpart to concede.

Baseline scope and qualification: Solomon explicitly cannot determine whether some warnings are hollow and compares them with American invocation of Congress. The asserted change in popular influence under Deng is a period-specific interpretation, not a general model of how public opinion controls PRC policy.

Tested component: Invoking public sentiment or an accommodating official’s vulnerability to induce concessions.

Strongest relevant support: The Chinese published accession narrative associates the post-bombing pause with dignity. CN-WTO-G1 Editorial interpretation, not an internal instruction or complete public-opinion record.

Strongest contrary or limiting evidence: American officials invoked independent congressional authority. US-NOV15-BERGER Actual institutional constraint and tactical invocation can coexist on either side.

Assessment and unresolved remainder: Need the specific Chinese appeal, contemporaneous assessment of domestic constraint and foreign response. Neither later internet commentary nor a public dignity claim alone tests efficacy.

S-P16 — unresolved

Baseline claim (printed pp. 110–111, 139–140, 153–156): Negotiating tone varies with individuals, assigned roles, senior instructions, and domestic circumstances; alternating harder and more accommodating presentations can affect expectations about compromise.

Baseline scope and qualification: The chapter calls the hard–soft tactic universal and notes that Deng could be abrasive while Huang could be conciliatory. The portraits are American-facing and partly impressionistic. Unresolved Mao–Zhou politics limit certainty about who directed every presentation.

Tested component: Variation in tone across individuals, instructions and roles, and its effect on expectations.

Strongest relevant support: Conrad’s interviews describe a shift from climate specialists to the premier’s foreign-policy team. CLI05 Retrospective explanatory account without released mandates.

Strongest contrary or limiting evidence: Warm personal presentation coexisted with firm substantive demands. K23-02

Assessment and unresolved remainder: No sufficiently paired same-person or assigned-role comparison establishes orchestrated hard–soft tactics or a resulting concession. Tone and proposal changes must be coded separately.

S-P17 — retain within tested scope

Baseline claim (printed pp. 113–122): PRC officials use public communication around negotiations to establish agendas, raise or reduce expectations, advertise limits, and suggest openings, with different outlets offering different degrees of commitment.

Baseline scope and qualification: Solomon documents learning about television, failed expectation-raising, and respect for some confidentiality requests. The historical media hierarchy is not automatically valid later. His suggestion that a Chinese press criticism influenced the Mayaguez response is conjectural and needs independent causal evidence.

Tested component: Public communication used to characterize the agenda, prior obligations and permissible movement.

Strongest relevant support: White papers and briefing defended different stages of the negotiation. CN-TRADE-WP2018, CN-TRADE-WP2019, CN-TRADE-SCIO20191213 English and Chinese presentations supported immediate crew return while responsibility remained disputed. EP303, EP304, EP305

Strongest contrary or limiting evidence: The U.S. publication dispute shows counterpart publicity and disputed effects too. US-APR08-BRIEFING

Assessment and unresolved remainder: Retain observable public positioning. A contemporary outlet hierarchy, internal dissemination and causal effects on public pressure need direct evidence rather than projection of Solomon’s older media categories.

S-P18 — unresolved

Baseline claim (printed pp. 122–125): Rapid collection and internal circulation of foreign reporting can inform PRC bargaining while also generating domestic reactions that constrain negotiators or intensify later responses.

Baseline scope and qualification: Solomon says the selection procedures for internal publications were unknown. Some feedback mechanisms rely on private explanations or chronological inference. Abundant access to information does not prove unfiltered reporting to leaders or correct interpretation of a foreign political system.

Tested component: Internal circulation and selection of foreign reporting causing a changed bargaining response.

Strongest relevant support: Jost’s institutional account offers an information-flow mechanism. EP319 Selected scholarship and source-chain limits do not replace Chinese circulation records.

Strongest contrary or limiting evidence: American assessments of Xi’s possible ignorance are not direct Chinese evidence. C23-20, C23-21 Later reports partly repeat the same assessment chain.

Assessment and unresolved remainder: No acquired Chinese internal report and subsequent decision establish the feedback loop. Information availability, accuracy, transmission and political constraint remain separate variables.

S-P19 — retain within tested scope

Baseline claim (printed pp. 105–106, 125–129, 140–143): PRC negotiators preserve and invoke the prior record, including protests and predecessors’ words, but may restate disputed commitments in forms more favorable to their current position.

Baseline scope and qualification: The chapter’s translation and distortion allegations require checking against originals. Cultural claims about written Chinese do not independently explain document management. Table 2 is a set of contextual signaling hypotheses, not an automatic dictionary of intent. Solomon also acknowledges mutual overreading of symbols.

Tested component: Invocation of prior commitments; favorable restatement and distortion remain separate tests.

Strongest relevant support: Liu invoked an Argentina numerical understanding. CN-TRADE-LIU201905 Both sides used prior understandings while emphasizing different relationship obligations. IND-MEA-6534, IND-CN-2022-SUN, IND-MEA-35739

Strongest contrary or limiting evidence: The U.S. dinner readout left purchases unquantified. TUS05 Records distinguish common principles from unilateral positions and non-endorsement. NOR-168, NOR-172

S-P20 — retain within tested scope

Baseline claim (printed pp. 130–131, 134–135): Statements of independence, indifference, or foreign indebtedness can portray the counterpart as the party needing agreement and responsible for repairing the relationship.

Baseline scope and qualification: Solomon interprets these claims through the relationship game and anxiety about dependence, but real asymmetries or genuine performance disputes may also explain them. Analyst disbelief is not a reliable measure of the speaker’s actual valuation.

Tested component: Publicly assigning repair responsibility and portraying Chinese cooperation as conditional on American conduct.

Strongest relevant support: Chinese officials called for corrective U.S. action and appropriate conditions for contact. C23-06, C23-11, C23-14

Strongest contrary or limiting evidence: Chinese leaders also requested constructive advocacy and mutual improvement. K23-01, K23-02 These are compatible with valuing the relationship, not proof of indifference.

Assessment and unresolved remainder: Retain the public rhetorical practice. Actual private valuation, anxiety about dependence and whether the portrayal extracted concessions are unresolved.

S-P21 — retain within tested scope

Baseline claim (printed pp. 131–134, 138–140): PRC officials use challenges to the counterpart’s resolve, logic, motives, and policy consistency to contest its rationale and encourage behavior favorable to Chinese interests.

Baseline scope and qualification: Kissinger sometimes rejected the premise explicitly, and Deng’s debunking could resist perceived American pressure. The chapter’s linkage to dialectical reasoning is interpretive; similar forms of persuasion are internationally familiar and may express sincere substantive disagreement.

Tested component: Challenges to policy consistency and motives as visible argumentation.

Strongest relevant support: China accused Washington of changing positions and making unacceptable demands. CN-TRADE-WP2018, CN-TRADE-WP2019 Wang challenged the strategic logic of containing China. K23-02

Strongest contrary or limiting evidence: Blinken maintained U.S. national-security restrictions and Taiwan policy despite Chinese demands. C23-15

Assessment and unresolved remainder: The persuasion attempt is observed, not its success or derivation from distinctive dialectical reasoning. Sincere disagreement and familiar bargaining argument remain alternatives.

S-P22 — retain within tested scope

Baseline claim (printed pp. 135–137): Openly specifying differences can coexist with limited cooperation, reassuring domestic and allied audiences while clarifying the common interests that sustain an agreement.

Baseline scope and qualification: Solomon distinguishes the domestic and strategic conditions of different episodes and credits compromise by both parties. The explanation does not imply that all harsh rhetoric is compatible with agreement or that disagreement is merely theatrical.

Tested component: Explicit disagreement coexisting with a specified limited bargain.

Strongest relevant support: Both governments accepted normalization with the arms-sales disagreement preserved. NOR-170, NOR-172 Shared dialogue mechanisms coexisted with Taiwan and technology disputes. C23-17, C23-18 Different national responsibilities and common procedures coexist. CLI15, CLI17

Strongest contrary or limiting evidence: Immediate practical resolution did not settle responsibility or future reconnaissance. EP303, EP306 Limited cooperation does not imply the remaining disagreement is theatrical or harmless.

Assessment and unresolved remainder: Retain coexistence. Domestic/allied reassurance as the cause of explicit disagreement and the conditions under which rhetoric prevents agreement remain unmeasured.

S-P23 — unresolved

Baseline claim (printed pp. 143–147): Control over the schedule and apparent willingness to wait can help PRC negotiators exploit the counterpart’s deadline, but Chinese negotiators are also susceptible to time pressure.

Baseline scope and qualification: Kissinger described both sides using the 1972 deadline. The United States declined an unfavorable deal in February 1982, and Solomon recognizes initial British time leverage over Hong Kong. Cultural reserve does not establish an inherent Chinese advantage in patience.

Tested component: Chinese schedule control producing favorable terms, with reciprocal vulnerability retained.

Strongest relevant support: Long-running talks persisted despite Russian near-completion claims. RUS-G14, RUS-G17, RUS-G28 Optimistic targets are not proven binding deadlines or evidence of exploitation.

Strongest contrary or limiting evidence: U.S. departure letters actively managed the deadline. CN-WTO-B3 Washington controlled and extended tariff dates. TUS09

Assessment and unresolved remainder: Direct evidence supports reciprocal timing tactics more than a measured Chinese patience advantage. Need private deadlines, alternative offers and a concession sequence showing their effect.

S-P24 — unresolved

Baseline claim (printed pp. 147–151): A shift toward concrete drafting and intervention by a senior PRC leader can signal that agreement is authorized and near, even after apparent deadlock.

Baseline scope and qualification: The chapter’s face-based explanation is not independently established, and selected successful cases cannot determine reliability. Senior authority does not eliminate bureaucratic reversal: the chapter cites repudiation of the 1973 claims/assets solution. Several local date inconsistencies require original-source checking.

Original-text qualification: The unchanged S-P24 extraction says “can signal,” but this softens the printed claim. Solomon p. 149 calls senior intervention “the most reliable sign” of imminent agreement and invokes leaders’ reluctance to risk face; p. 150 asserts a highly consistent endgame. The failed claims/assets example on p. 148 creates a tension with that prediction. The current verdict evaluates this stronger original claim, in its concrete-drafting context, rather than silently substituting a weaker one.

Tested component: The original ranking of senior intervention as the most reliable sign of approaching agreement, considered with concrete drafting and the proposed face mechanism.

Strongest relevant support: Concrete text and Deng’s intervention preceded successful normalization. NOR-159, NOR-166, NOR-170 A Russian authorization attached drafts and bounded discretion immediately before signature. RUS-AUTH2004, RUS-T2004 This is a counterpart authority record, not Chinese mandate evidence or proof outsiders had it prospectively.

Strongest contrary or limiting evidence: Zhou’s movement did not close the claims bargain and the monetary offer was later withdrawn. CLA04, CLA05, CLA10 Solomon already acknowledged this failure; the new reconstruction sharpens its unfinished terms rather than discovering failure as a new possibility. Zhu’s March meeting did not close accession talks. EWT-01 This defeats a deterministic shortcut from a senior meeting to closure, but does not rank the predictive value of all available signals. Xi received Blinken without military-channel agreement. C23-15, C23-16 The record does not show the full concrete-drafting condition for the military-channel request or a promise by Xi that it would be settled.

Assessment and unresolved remainder: The comparative ranking and frequency remain unresolved: selected successes and failures cannot establish whether senior intervention is the most reliable available sign. As practical advice, identify what a leader authorizes, confirms or leaves unsettled instead of equating contact with imminent closure. That revision in use is distinct from empirical refutation. Missing later drafts prevent a full test of the conjunction, and the face mechanism is unproved.

S-P25 — unresolved

Baseline claim (printed pp. 151–153): Implementation can remain a bargaining arena in which PRC officials demand adherence from the counterpart while seeking adjustment, accommodation, or new concessions themselves.

Baseline scope and qualification: The chapter mixes formal agreements with contemplated visits and expressions of intent; these are not equivalent commitments. Deng’s May 1978 acknowledgment that a new administration could reconsider qualifies the claim of rigid continuity. Commercial complaints require independent evidence before generalization to political agreements.

Tested component: Two components kept distinct: implementation bargaining, and asymmetric Chinese demands for adherence while seeking accommodation itself.

Strongest relevant support: China defended fulfillment, pressed U.S. obligations and restrictions, and carried issues into later bargains. POI03, POI04, POI07, POI14, POI17 Closest evidence of reciprocal demands; private reservations and formal appeals are incomplete. The parties negotiated procedures for further implementation disagreement. DS379-07

Strongest contrary or limiting evidence: Negotiated access produced a regulatory acceleration and an operating U.S.-owned firm. FIN-15, FIN-26, FIN-28 Consensual arrangements made boundary implementation workable. RUS-USE1999, RUS-MEMO2004 Later aircraft work concerned issues expressly deferred, not necessarily reopened obligations. EP303, EP306

Assessment and unresolved remainder: Continuing bargaining is strongly observed, but that alone cannot retain the full proposition. Need obligation-specific Chinese demands, its own requested departures, comparable counterpart conduct and agreed exceptions to establish asymmetry rather than ordinary implementation, mutual adjustment or disputed compliance.
