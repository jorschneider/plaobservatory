# 7. Rival, partner, supplier

On October 23, 2024, Narendra Modi and Xi Jinping met in Kazan and welcomed an agreement concerning their disputed border. The arrangement had been announced two days earlier. Officials had already negotiated it through diplomatic and military channels; the leaders endorsed it and directed further work. A reader beginning with the summit photograph could easily reverse the sequence and assign the agreement to the encounter that publicly affirmed its political importance.[^1]

The distinction matters beyond the timing of one meeting. In this case, China negotiated with a neighboring rival that had made improvement of the wider relationship contingent on conditions at the border. India invoked past agreements, demanded respect for principles, limited disclosures about operational details and kept negotiators within a hierarchy. Several practices familiar from Solomon's account of Chinese behavior were resources available to the counterpart too.

Russia offers a different comparison. An improving political relationship accompanied a boundary settlement implemented through successive agreements, fieldwork and arrangements for local residents. Later, close political relations sustained both a working gas pipeline and prolonged disagreement over another. A counterpart could be a strategic partner, a former territorial adversary and a supplier with which commercial terms remained unresolved. The label attached to the relationship did not determine the terms of each transaction.

These comparisons change an American-centered explanation in two ways. They make the counterpart's conduct part of the mechanism, rather than background to China's tactics. They also expose differences in what can be divided and exchanged: separation of troops, restoration of access, territorial jurisdiction, local economic use, prices and future purchases. The same language of friendship or principle can accompany different obligations because different interests are at stake.

These are selected comparisons across different periods, institutions and bargaining objects. They can reveal a mechanism outside the American relationship, a condition under which it fails, or reciprocal conduct obscured by a Chinese-centered account. They cannot isolate a national effect.

## India: making the relationship conditional

The 2020 confrontation occurred within an elaborate framework of earlier agreements. This was not a relationship waiting for its first statement of principles. The 2005 agreement on political parameters protected the broader relationship from boundary differences while also requiring respect for the Line of Actual Control and maintenance of peace pending a settlement. A comprehensive boundary settlement and restraint before settlement were separate tasks, contained in the same document.[^2]

That separation left room for disagreement over political emphasis. Beijing could argue that an unresolved boundary should not obstruct other cooperation. New Delhi could insist that peace along the line was a condition for such cooperation. Each position could invoke part of a previously accepted framework. Establishing that a party had distorted the common record would require more than observing that it emphasized the provision most favorable to its current purpose.

Institutions also divided the work. The Working Mechanism for Consultation and Coordination, established in 2012, joined diplomats with military representatives and provided for emergency consultations. Its founding agreement expressly excluded resolution of the boundary question and protected the separate role of the Special Representatives. A meeting of that mechanism, a military commanders' meeting and a leaders' encounter therefore had different subjects and capacities. Counting all three as occasions of contact would conceal much of what made a proposed arrangement usable.[^3]

Following the confrontation and deadly Galwan clashes, the September 2020 Moscow understanding directed the sides toward disengagement, appropriate distance between forces, respect for earlier agreements and continued diplomatic channels. The sixth corps-commanders meeting, on September 21, added commitments against further frontline deployment and unilateral changes. The broad political direction was meaningful, but it did not publish a complete withdrawal map or settle every location. Subsequent agreements had to define how it would operate.[^4]

There was progress, followed by further obstruction. The February 2021 joint statement welcomed completed disengagement at Pangong while reserving remaining issues. After the thirteenth military round in October, India's account blamed China for rejecting its constructive proposals. That accusation belongs in the history; it identifies a failed round and India's public explanation. Without the proposals and instructions, it does not establish which Chinese official blocked a possible bargain or whether disagreement arose within the Chinese leadership.[^5]

Saheb Singh Chadha's interview-based reconstruction helps explain the institutional setting. He describes political meetings establishing a framework, diplomats elaborating steps and commanders negotiating their application. On the Indian side, he reports that meetings of the China Study Group set the limits within which the corps commander could negotiate. The account supplies a concrete alternative to inferring Chinese political conflict whenever talks stalled: the officials at the table could be working within limits set on both sides. Chadha did not interview Chinese officials, and his anonymous interview evidence cannot recover Beijing's parallel mandate.[^6]

The March 2022 visit of Wang Yi to New Delhi demonstrated the limits of senior access. Jaishankar publicly described three hours of discussion and acknowledged earlier partial progress, while emphasizing that implementation had moved more slowly than India wanted. He rejected the characterization of the relationship as normal while large border deployments persisted. The visit established a channel and a continuing negotiation. It did not itself purchase the broader normality Beijing sought.[^7]

Here the relationship became an instrument used by India. A Chinese appeal to the larger benefits of cooperation encountered an Indian insistence on the security conditions under which those benefits could be pursued. This resembles the relational bargaining Solomon described, but reverses the direction of the pressure. The observation does not make the two governments' practices identical in frequency or effect. It makes attribution to a specifically Chinese repertoire insufficient as an explanation of this exchange.

## A local bargain and a larger disagreement

The agreement at Gogra–Hot Springs, known to India as Patrol Point 15, shows what the public language of disengagement could contain. The July 2022 military communiqué promised continued work; the September announcements identified a local result. The Chinese publication used the name Jianan Daban. Different place names did not by themselves establish different obligations.[^8]

India's September 9 explanation supplied the operational detail. Disengagement had begun at 08:30 the previous day and was to finish by September 12. Both sides would cease forward deployments in a phased, coordinated and verified process. Temporary structures and associated infrastructure would be dismantled, with mutual verification, and the terrain restored to its earlier condition. The October diplomatic release subsequently described completion of the process.[^9]

These were reciprocal, timed duties. They complicate an inference of operational vagueness drawn from a brief communiqué. They also leave an evidentiary distinction: the Indian explanation is an official account of the agreement, rather than the authenticated protocol, its maps or the verification logs. It establishes the government's stated understanding of the local bargain. It does not measure every change in access or independently confirm every troop movement.

The result did not settle what improvement at one location should mean for the relationship. In late September, Chinese ambassador Sun Weidong presented movement from emergency handling toward routine management and advocated renewed exchanges. India's October response rejected treating the border process as complete. It continued to distinguish disengagement from de-escalation and restored normality. Both sides could acknowledge a local step while disagreeing over whether that step justified wider concessions.[^10]

That is an important form of bargaining after agreement. The disputed question was the sufficiency of the completed step, not necessarily whether the parties had performed it. If one side regarded an arrangement as a reason to resume wider cooperation and the other regarded it as one installment in a larger security process, continuing negotiation did not by itself demonstrate repudiation. The historian must recover the original scope before deciding that a later demand reopened it.

The following year provides a useful check on expectations of senior intervention. After an extended military round in August 2023, India said that a Modi–Xi conversation at BRICS had produced instructions to intensify efforts toward disengagement and de-escalation. China's account stressed the broader relationship and appropriate handling of the boundary issue. Yet October's military statement still referred to remaining questions. India's spokesperson also resisted a questioner's suggestion that the round had produced no consensus.[^11]

The sequence allows neither an inevitable-breakthrough narrative nor a simple declaration of total failure. Senior contact and positive language did not establish immediate closure. The later statement did not establish that nothing had moved. Most importantly, no authenticated draft was recovered that would allow a full test of Solomon's combined attention to senior intervention and concrete drafting. The visible signal was weaker than the information a forecaster would need.

## Endorsement after agreement

The 2024 sequence is clearer about the relationship between channels. In July, the foreign ministers met in Astana and Vientiane. India's Astana account described agreement to intensify diplomatic and military contacts. Subsequent working-mechanism meetings explicitly linked their activity to the ministerial discussions; the August accounts reported narrower differences. Doval and Wang then discussed progress in September. These records identify work passing between levels, although the two governments continued to emphasize different aspects of it.[^12]

On October 21, India announced a patrolling arrangement. Its next briefing dated the agreement to early that morning and said earlier disengagement arrangements had not been reopened. China confirmed agreement through diplomatic and military communication while declining to provide operational details. India's foreign secretary declined to speculate about why the Chinese position had changed. The restraint is analytically valuable: a participant's public announcement of success did not purport to reveal the other side's internal cause.[^13]

Original Indian reporting described coordinated patrol schedules, advance communication and limits on patrol-party sizes. Those details, attributed to unnamed sources, suggested how access could resume while reducing the risk of encounters. They are plausible mechanics rather than an authenticated annex. They should not be expanded into a claim that every earlier buffer arrangement had disappeared or that the entire pre-2020 military situation had been restored.[^14]

The October 23 summit followed. India described endorsement of the arrangement and direction for the Special Representatives to meet; China welcomed progress while emphasizing the protection of the wider relationship. The anticipated meeting may have supplied an incentive or a deadline. The public sequence establishes endorsement after the bargain, rather than the summit itself producing a bargain still absent when the leaders met. Russia's hosting role likewise supplies no evidence of Russian mediation.[^1]

In December, Jaishankar told Parliament that verification patrols had been followed by regular activity under the understanding and that patrolling and grazing access had been agreed in Depsang and Demchok. He characterized disengagement as complete and de-escalation as the next task. He also described temporary restraints elsewhere as dependent on local conditions and open to later review.[^15]

The distinction between disengagement and de-escalation is substantive. Separating forces at a confrontation point reduces one danger. Reducing or relocating the larger concentration of forces concerns another. Neither automatically settles the boundary. In this sequence, the governments could make progress on access and local separation while continuing to negotiate force posture and the political relationship.

By August 2025, their paired accounts expressly put de-escalation at the stage of discussing principles and modalities. They also commissioned new work: an expert group under the diplomatic mechanism to explore early results in boundary delimitation, a border-management working group and general-level military mechanisms for additional sectors. India's account simultaneously listed steps toward renewed travel, trade and other exchanges.[^16]

This qualifies a rigid reading of India's earlier insistence on sequencing. Relational recovery could proceed alongside unfinished military adjustment. The public record does not show India simply abandoning its security condition; it shows that both the condition and the permitted response to partial progress had to be interpreted. By May 2026, paired working-mechanism accounts described continuing work on delimitation, management and cooperation. They establish newly commissioned technical activity, not completed delimitation or a formally amended 2012 agreement.[^17]

The Indian case consequently leaves a mixed result that a single success label cannot capture. Local bargains produced officially reported separation and restored activity in specified areas. They did not settle sovereignty or establish a complete return to an earlier military baseline. Political authority, technical work and conditional cooperation interacted on both sides. No public evidence examined here identifies Chinese leadership conflict as the necessary cause of delay, or the independent effect of a summit on a particular concession.

## Russia: dividing sovereignty from use

The Russian boundary case begins from a different relationship and reaches a different kind of outcome. The parties ultimately agreed a territorial line. Their progress also demonstrates that detailed, flexible arrangements were available well before Xi's leadership.

In the published Chinese selection of Deng Xiaoping's May 1989 conversation with Mikhail Gorbachev, a forceful account of historical injury accompanied willingness to use existing treaties as the basis for settlement. Deng allowed each side to state its historical view without requiring an answer or debate, while recalling earlier Chinese demands for acknowledgment of unequal treaties. The memoir-derived Russian account preserves Gorbachev's disagreement with the historical narrative alongside agreement to normalize relations. Neither source is an unedited contemporary transcript.[^18]

The juxtaposition nevertheless matters. A demand for acknowledgment, a demand for territorial restitution and an opportunity to state a grievance are different acts. The presence of historical grievance does not identify the concession being demanded in return. In this instance, the published Chinese position contained both condemnation of the past and a basis on which practical negotiation could proceed.

The May 18 joint communiqué contemplated simultaneous settlement of the eastern and western border sectors. The eastern agreement was nevertheless signed separately on May 16, 1991, with two sections expressly reserved. The western-sector agreement followed in 1994. The observable package had become divisible.[^19]

Why it became divisible is more contested. Taylor Fravel argues that domestic insecurity can increase the value of neighbors' cooperation and encourage territorial compromise; he interprets the 1991 sequencing in light of Chinese priorities after Tiananmen. Akihiro Iwashita's reconstruction places substantive convergence in talks reopened in 1987, drawing substantially on later participant accounts. Earlier progress does not disprove a later effect of domestic crisis on package separation. It does mean that all flexibility cannot be dated to that crisis. The record still needs the intervening offers and instructions to distinguish an accelerated decision from completion of work already underway.[^20]

The 1991 agreement is more revealing about the mechanism accepted than about its decisive cause. It established an equal joint commission to identify channels, allocate river islands, erect markers and prepare documents and maps. It supplied criteria for river channels and rules concerning subsequent natural changes. It also addressed navigation separately, including passage from the Ussuri past Khabarovsk into the Amur. Difficult sections could remain unresolved while other interests received specified protection.[^21]

Implementation then encountered constituencies whose concerns were more concrete than the tone of bilateral relations. Farms, river use, border facilities and regional authority were affected by the line. Iwashita reconstructs resistance in Primorye and differing approaches among Russian regional leaders. Federal officials did not face a uniform regional objection; they worked with some local authorities while encountering resistance from others. The completion announcement in 1997 concerned the sectors already covered, leaving the reserved areas outstanding.[^22]

The Menkeseli arrangement made one way through these difficulties visible. According to Iwashita, Russian border and regional authorities proposed allowing established Russian users to continue local activity under Chinese sovereignty. His account traces acceptance of the idea into a 1997 framework and a detailed agreement in December 1999. The proposal's origin remains an attributed reconstruction. The published agreement independently establishes the structure the governments accepted.[^23]

Its definition of joint economic use was precise: the sovereign state permitted established residents of the other country to pursue specified traditional activities. Named areas under Chinese sovereignty were opened to Russian users, and a named Russian area to Chinese users. Eligibility was restricted to prior users, whose numbers could not increase. Haymaking, fishing and farming were specified; the host state's development rights remained intact. Users needed documents from their own state and permission from the host, whose law continued to apply.[^24]

The arrangement thus separated jurisdiction from selected benefits of use. A territorial decision need not extinguish every economic practice of the people affected by it. This could make implementation more acceptable without creating shared sovereignty. Its limits were part of that accommodation: a five-year use period, dates to be agreed diplomatically, inspections and consultations at least twice a year. The recovered publication omits the two annexes, so the exact maps, numerical limits and permit procedures cannot be verified here. Nor does the instrument establish how many residents actually benefited.

This is a particularly useful extension of Solomon's implementation question. Later bargaining can distribute the costs of carrying out an agreement. It can specify a benefit that helps the legal settlement work. Calling all such negotiation Chinese insistence on accommodation would obscure its reciprocal and agreed character. The Russian proposal and the bilateral duties matter to the explanation.

The comparison with India also has a firm boundary. Restoring an activity under another state's agreed sovereignty differs from coordinating patrols along a disputed line. Both arrangements separate a local practice from a larger conflict, but only the former operates on the premise that jurisdiction over the relevant area has been determined. Similar techniques do not make the outcomes equivalent.

## What authorization looked like

Political reassurance preceded final territorial closure. Article 6 of the 2001 friendship treaty stated that the parties had no mutual territorial claims while directing continued negotiation of unagreed boundary alignments and maintenance of the status quo. The sentence accommodated a broad assurance and an expressly unfinished task. Reading the assurance alone would manufacture a contradiction when boundary talks continued.[^25]

The remaining settlement in 2004 supplies unusually concrete evidence of a mandate. A Russian presidential order dated October 13 accepted a foreign-ministry proposal coordinated with federal authorities and three affected regional executives. It authorized negotiation and signature of attached drafts, allowing changes that did not alter their principles. The attachments included the supplementary boundary agreement and related navigation and cooperation instruments.[^26]

This is the kind of evidence that improves on an inference from senior presence. It identifies an instrument, a responsible organization and the bounds of discretion. It is also Russian evidence. It does not disclose the parallel Chinese approval sequence, and its availability in a present-day legal reproduction does not establish when an outside observer could have inspected it before signature.

The next day, foreign ministers Li Zhaoxing and Sergei Lavrov signed the supplementary agreement. It defined the remaining alignments, including land segments through islands that had previously been reserved, and assigned further fieldwork to the joint commission. The settlement reduced China's claimed area while requiring Russia to relinquish control in selected places. The two concessions use different baselines. Measuring only territory transferred from Russian control would miss what China relinquished from its claim; measuring only the retreat from China's claim would miss the material change for Russia.[^27]

The agreement also had a contested Russian reception. Contemporary reporting describes legislative opposition in Khabarovsk, a governor defending local interests, protests and disagreement during ratification. Officials invoked agriculture, transport, migration and military considerations. The record does not support treating Russia's interest as the uncontested preference of a single actor, or China's bargaining success as simply exploiting a helpless counterpart.[^28]

Signature was followed by legislative approvals and entry into force in June 2005. The supplementary descriptive protocol and annexes came in July 2008; the October exchange of notes brought those instruments into force, accompanied by the handover ceremony. Agreement on a line, legal effectiveness, field demarcation and material transfer were separate accomplishments.[^29]

The intervening years do not show that the 2004 agreement was merely symbolic. They show work still required by a settlement of this kind. Equally, the existence of the later ceremony does not independently verify every marker or establish that all local opposition disappeared. The strongest result is a documented progression through distinct commitments and implementation stages.

Fravel expressly excludes the 2004 compromise from his domestic-insecurity explanation. It is therefore improper to enlist his theory as a universal account of the Russian sequence. Improved relations, the value of closure, changes in Russian federal authority and expectations about future power may all matter. The documents examined here identify accepted arrangements and one side's mandate more securely than they rank those causes. The case establishes old capacities for compromise and technical specification; it leaves a narrower causal question about why particular terms became acceptable at particular moments.

## Gas: political value and commercial terms

The gas negotiations bring the limits of political closeness into sharper view. They also change what power means. A country's diplomatic importance and aggregate resources do not determine whether it can redirect gas, finance a pipeline or find another purchaser on comparable terms. Those alternatives depend on the project.

Four projects must remain separate. The eastern route, later known as Power of Siberia, drew on eastern Siberian resources and became an operating supply relationship. The original western or Altai proposal envisaged a different connection toward Xinjiang. A subsequent Far Eastern contract was another transaction. The later Power of Siberia 2 proposal involved transit through Mongolia and a much larger prospective volume. A new name or ceremonial announcement could not make their infrastructure and commitments interchangeable.[^30]

The eastern bargain already had substantial momentum before Russia's 2014 confrontation with the West. In September 2013, with Putin and Xi present, Gazprom and CNPC agreed principal terms. Contemporary reporting described the document as legally binding while explicitly reserving price. Gazprom hoped to complete the contract by year-end. That target passed.[^31]

This is an early negative observation for any shortcut from leader sponsorship to imminent closure. It also explains why the word binding cannot classify a whole project. Parties can bind themselves on particular questions while leaving a commercially indispensable term open. Without the 2013 instrument, its precise obligations cannot be reconstructed; the contemporary account nevertheless makes the reserved question explicit.

Price remained unresolved on the first day of Putin's May 2014 visit. On May 21, the parties announced the thirty-year eastern supply contract. The Chinese account envisaged an annual build-up to 38 billion cubic metres and divided construction responsibilities: Gazprom would develop Russian production, processing and transmission, while CNPC would build the Chinese pipeline and associated storage. The contract itself remained unpublished.[^32]

Putin's public explanation combined the language of friendship with hard bargaining. Experts had worked until 3:30 in the morning. Both sides, he said, had compromised. Government decisions still had to follow. His account was a participant's presentation of success, but it did not depict political friendship as eliminating commercial disagreement. Later in the exchange, he corrected his earlier description of the pricing mechanism: it was linked to a basket of oil and petroleum products.[^33]

That correction matters more than the headline valuation. Gazprom's $400 billion figure did not disclose the coefficients, review provisions or purchase obligations. Dividing a lifetime value by maximum volumes would produce an arithmetic average, not the actual contract price. The deteriorating Russian relationship with the West provided a consequential setting; without the formula and earlier offers, it cannot establish the discount China obtained because of that change.[^34]

Financing remained another margin. Two days after signature, Gazprom Export chief Alexander Medvedev described a $25 billion prepayment as agreed in principle, with details under discussion. In November, Gazprom's Alexei Miller said an advance had been considered in connection with a lower price but was no longer under consideration after price settlement. This is evidence that a financing option disappeared. An unpublished, incompletely specified option should not be converted into a binding Chinese payment obligation and then counted as breached.[^35]

The implementation record was substantial. A December 2015 corporate release described further agreements on the cross-border section, communications, technical procedures and working groups. A delivery-start date was announced in 2017; actual supply began in December 2019. By April 2026, Gazprom's reported record included 38.8 billion cubic metres delivered during 2025 and arrangements to maintain specially agreed supply during maintenance. These are company-reported operational results, rather than an independent audit of every contractual duty, but they establish considerably more than ceremonial alignment.[^36]

## The projects that did not follow the same path

On the original western route, the November 2014 framework and May 2015 heads of agreement did not quickly become a sales contract. The same December 2015 release that described implementation work on the eastern route scheduled further western commercial negotiations. Within a single relationship and even a single corporate announcement, completed and unfinished bargains coexisted.[^37]

Keun-Wook Paik's contemporary analysis treated stronger political support as grounds for expecting progress on the western project. Read beside the subsequent record, that expectation is useful because it did not establish the outcome. Senior support belongs in the history of incomplete projects as well as successful ones. Excluding the delayed rounds would make a forecasting rule look stronger by selecting only the agreements it appeared to predict.

The later Mongolia proposal also changed the commercial problem. Vitaly Yermakov and Michal Meidan distinguish engineering work in Mongolia from the Chinese supply agreement still required, and explain why eastern and western resources could not simply be redirected without new infrastructure. Their demand scenarios and estimates of pricing parameters remain analytical projections. Their central geographic distinction is essential: national export dependence is not the same as an immediately available route to a particular customer.[^38]

China did accept another transaction. In February 2022, Gazprom announced a second long-term sales agreement for the Far Eastern route, initially adding ten billion cubic metres annually. The same release's account of a completed feasibility study for the Mongolia project did not turn that study into another supply contract. These different stages caution against interpreting every announcement as either empty politics or a completed sale.[^39]

In March 2023, Putin said almost all parameters of the Power of Siberia 2 agreement had been agreed. The joint statement committed to advancing research and consultation on the trilateral pipeline. The unilateral and common formulations carried different information. Neither published an agreed price, financing arrangement or purchase schedule.[^40]

Reporting in 2024 attributed difficult price and purchase-volume demands to China. Its source chain matters: the accessible Reuters article summarized a Financial Times report based on unnamed sources. These were not two independent inspections of a Chinese term sheet. The account points to a plausible dispute over the distribution of price and demand risk, while leaving the actual offers inaccessible.[^41]

In September 2025, Miller announced a legally binding memorandum concerning construction of Power of Siberia 2 and Soyuz Vostok, with prospective capacity of fifty billion cubic metres a year. He separately announced increases for the eastern and Far Eastern routes. The memorandum deserves recognition as an announced advance. Its full text was not recovered, however, so its enforceable duties, conditions and remedies remain unknown. Capacity is also different from a buyer's minimum purchase commitment.[^42]

The strongest evidence of the later stage comes from explicit statements rather than the absence of a celebratory announcement. On September 3, 2026, Deputy Prime Minister Alexander Novak said commercial terms were still being discussed and final routing would depend on agreement between Gazprom and CNPC. The next day he described continuing preparation of the contract and government assistance toward concrete company agreement. Russian reports used the name Power of Baikal. The publicly described political commitment had still not become a completed commercial package at that point.[^43]

Chinese planning documents supply context rather than a hidden negotiating mandate. The March 2026 national plan distinguished construction of the Far Eastern pipeline from preparatory work on the middle-route project. The July oil and gas plan emphasized domestic production, diversified imports and flexible operation. Its annex covered interprovincial Chinese pipelines, so the absence of an international project from that list cannot establish rejection. These materials identify the buyer's stated supply priorities; they do not disclose its reservation price.[^44]

The case is consequently consistent with a buyer valuing a strategic relationship while retaining alternatives and resisting an unattractive distribution of project risks. It does not demonstrate that every month of delay was deliberately chosen to exploit Russian weakness. Route design, future demand, financing and the terms on which dedicated infrastructure would operate remained material questions. A stronger claim about tactical patience would require an otherwise acceptable offer deliberately withheld to extract a documented concession from a counterpart facing a deadline.

Nor does the unfinished project erase successful supply. The eastern pipeline's operating record belongs beside the delayed western proposals. Their coexistence is the explanatory result: close relations can preserve a reason to continue negotiating while leaving the parties unable to agree on a particular transaction. Political value can sustain a process without determining its price.

## What the comparisons change

Solomon's attention to the purpose of the relationship survives these comparisons. Its content becomes more specific. India used the prospect of wider cooperation to insist on border conditions. Russia sought to preserve local interests within a territorial settlement and later pursued commercial projects within a valuable strategic relationship. China faced counterparts making their own linkages, preserving their own reservations and operating through their own authorization procedures. Relationship management was interactive.

The comparisons also change the interpretation of partial agreement. Local separation can coexist with an unsettled boundary. Agreed sovereignty can coexist with regulated foreign use. A binding instrument can leave price unsettled. A signed supply contract can require later engineering agreements. Continued discussion in these settings may carry out the accepted division of work rather than weaken it. This supports Solomon's observation that implementation remains a negotiating arena, while leaving his stronger claim about asymmetric Chinese demands for adherence unproved by the mere continuation of talks.

Senior authority remains consequential, but the available evidence distinguishes its functions. The Russian order specified drafts and delegated discretion. The 2024 Modi–Xi meeting publicly endorsed an earlier arrangement. Repeated gas endorsements supported projects whose commercial questions remained open. These observations narrow the practical use of Solomon's claim that senior intervention is the most reliable sign of closure. They show why an analyst must ask what the leader actually authorized.[^45]

Finally, detailed institutional accommodation is not a recent invention. The 1991 boundary commission and 1999 local-use agreement predate the later developments in legal and regulatory bargaining examined in this book. Freeman had already identified specialization and adaptation to different counterparts. The modern task is to explain when a particular capability reached a decision and what it made possible, rather than narrate a simple passage from personal relationships to institutions.[^45]

The resulting account travels beyond the American case through questions more precise than whether China was patient, rigid or friendly. Which interests could be separated? Which earlier condition remained binding? Who could authorize the next step? What alternative made postponement tolerable? The Indian and Russian records provide different answers. Their variation is part of the behavior to be explained, not an inconvenience to be removed from a national portrait.
