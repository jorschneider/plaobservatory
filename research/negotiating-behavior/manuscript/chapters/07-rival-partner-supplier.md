# 7. Rival, partner, supplier

## India: deciding what progress was worth

After three hours with Wang Yi in New Delhi on March 25, 2022, Subrahmanyam Jaishankar faced a question that exposed the difficulty of his position. The Indian foreign minister had discussed trade, travel, students and international affairs with his Chinese counterpart. Did an agenda that broad itself amount to a return to normal relations?

Jaishankar answered no. He welcomed progress at several confrontation points along the disputed border, notably Pangong Tso. But large deployments remained, and their presence, he argued, violated earlier agreements. A ministerial visit could help resolve that problem. It could not make the problem cease to matter. India wanted stable, predictable relations; restoring them required peace at the border.[^7]

Wang had offered a different way forward. According to China’s account, the two large developing countries should preserve the wider significance of their relationship and prevent the boundary question from defining it. Yet that same account recorded Jaishankar’s call to complete disengagement in the remaining areas. It also described agreement to restore border peace and move toward regular management on the basis of disengagement. The ministers could agree that military separation mattered while disagreeing about how much of the relationship should wait for it.[^india-march-cn]

This was a negotiation over the value of the relationship, with India pressing the condition. Beijing’s appeal to common interests encountered a counterpart unwilling to grant the political meaning Beijing sought for partial progress. The question was practical: what could each government do next without conceding that the other’s remaining demands had become unnecessary?

### Keeping contact without conceding normality

Jaishankar did not answer that question by refusing other business. He raised the predicament of Indian students unable to return to China under pandemic restrictions and pressed for fairer market access. Wang, he reported, undertook to speak to the relevant authorities about the students. That was a promise to pursue the matter, rather than an immediate decision restoring their access. The relationship contained requests worth making even while India disputed its overall condition.

Nor did Jaishankar publicly offer a border concession in return for a summit. Asked whether a timetable had been set so that Narendra Modi could attend the BRICS summit in China, he acknowledged discussion of the invitation but described the border talks as a parallel, separate subject. Wang’s presence had provided an opportunity to press for implementation. Jaishankar stopped short of presenting either that presence or a future leaders’ meeting as the consideration for a bargain.[^7]

He had reason to distinguish a new understanding from carrying out an old one. The ministers had met in Moscow in September 2020 and again in Dushanbe the following year. Fifteen rounds of senior military talks had followed the confrontation that began in 2020. Some friction points had been addressed, but implementation was slower than India wanted. Jaishankar’s immediate objective was to complete disengagement so that discussion of de-escalation could follow. Moving forces apart at a particular confrontation point and reducing the larger military concentration were different decisions.

The dispute was not over whether principles should precede negotiation. Earlier principles already supplied arguments for both sides. The 2005 agreement stated that boundary differences should not obstruct the broader relationship. It also required respect for the Line of Actual Control and maintenance of peace pending a final settlement. Wang could invoke the value of cooperation; Jaishankar could insist that the agreed security foundation had been disturbed. The text contained both propositions. Appealing to one did not dispose of the other.[^2]

Turning those propositions into a local arrangement required officials with different responsibilities. The diplomatic Working Mechanism for Consultation and Coordination, established in 2012, included military representatives and could address border incidents. Its founding agreement excluded settlement of the boundary question, which remained the concern of the Special Representatives. Commanders, meanwhile, had to negotiate arrangements that forces could carry out on the ground. A conversation about managing an immediate danger did not authorize a territorial settlement.[^3]

Saheb Singh Chadha’s interviews with Indian officials make the limits of delegated authority more concrete. His reconstruction describes political meetings setting a framework, diplomatic meetings elaborating steps and commanders working on their application. Before corps-commanders’ meetings, India’s China Study Group set the limits within which its commander could negotiate. The military interlocutor was therefore not arriving with unlimited discretion to exchange concessions. Chadha did not interview Chinese officials, so the corresponding Chinese instructions remain unknown. But the Indian evidence alone makes it unsafe to read every stalled military round as evidence of a dispute inside Beijing.[^6]

### What the local bargain required

The sixteenth military round, held on July 17, 2022, did not publicly announce closure. Its joint release promised continued work on remaining issues and interim stability. It also linked resolving those issues to progress in bilateral relations. That formulation gave both governments room to continue: India had not accepted that the border no longer mattered, and neither side had committed publicly to a complete settlement at that meeting.[^8]

The next arrangement concerned one confrontation point in eastern Ladakh, in the western sector of the disputed frontier. On September 8, troops began disengaging in the area India called Gogra–Hot Springs, or Patrol Point 15. China’s publication used the name Jianan Daban. The joint announcement was brief. India’s explanation the following day showed that the undertaking it described was considerably more specific.

The process had begun at 08:30 and was to finish by September 12. Both sides would cease forward deployments in a phased, coordinated and verified manner, with troops returning to their respective areas. Temporary structures and associated infrastructure erected by both sides would be dismantled and checked through mutual verification. The terrain would be restored to its condition before the standoff. India also said the agreement required respect for the line and prohibited unilateral changes to the status quo.[^9]

These terms made the next decisions concrete. A commander needed to coordinate a withdrawal with the other force, remove structures and verify the reciprocal action. A diplomat needed to preserve the distinction between completing that task and accepting that all remaining issues were resolved. The September explanation expressly kept further talks on the agenda. India’s October diplomatic account then reported that the September 8–12 process had been completed and connected it to the foreign ministers’ understandings, including their July meeting in Bali.

The published explanation is the Indian government’s account of the bargain; its maps and verification records are not available here. It cannot establish every change in patrol access or independently verify every movement. What it does establish is the specificity of the duties India publicly described. Inferring a vague operative agreement from the brevity of the joint announcement would miss the timetable, reciprocal tasks and checks that officials said lay behind it.

Completion at one location brought a further decision into view: how much wider cooperation should it unlock? On September 27, Chinese ambassador Sun Weidong described a relationship recovering momentum. He pointed to disengagement, resumed processing of some visa categories and business travel. He advocated restored direct flights and broader exchanges. In his account, the emergency response following Galwan had largely ended and the border was moving toward routine management. Outstanding differences should be handled without allowing them to govern the whole relationship.[^10]

India resisted that conclusion. Asked about the ambassador’s remarks on October 7, spokesperson Arindam Bagchi acknowledged positive steps but maintained that more were needed. PP15 was another step. India still sought disengagement, de-escalation and restored border normality before normality in the wider relationship. Sun’s account of progress and Bagchi’s refusal to call it sufficient were not necessarily competing claims about whether the PP15 process had occurred. They assigned different political consequences to it.

This distinction matters to a negotiator deciding whether to accept a partial agreement. Local separation can reduce danger even when the parties cannot agree what it should purchase elsewhere. India could accept the reciprocal arrangement while withholding the broader response China advocated. China could participate in the arrangement and continue arguing that relations should improve. Continued bargaining over that response does not, by itself, show that either side had repudiated the local terms.

### A different threshold for moving forward

Two years later, the negotiators reached an additional arrangement. After further diplomatic and military work, India announced an agreement on October 21, 2024, concerning Depsang and Demchok. When Modi and Xi Jinping met in Kazan two days later, they welcomed the progress and directed further work. Their meeting endorsed an arrangement already reached. Its anticipated occurrence may have encouraged agreement; the sequence cannot assign the terms to the encounter that publicly celebrated them.[^1][^15]

In December, Jaishankar explained to Parliament what India understood it had obtained. The problem in the two areas had concerned obstruction of patrolling; Demchok also involved civilian grazing and access to places of local significance. He reported verification patrols followed by regular activity under the understanding. Patrolling and grazing access had been agreed, while temporary restraints at other locations remained dependent on local conditions and open to review. He called disengagement complete and identified de-escalation as the next task.[^15]

His account also changed the practical question facing the relationship. India could now consider other aspects of engagement in a calibrated fashion while continuing to address military concentration. The choice was no longer whether PP15 alone justified a return to normality. It was how much cooperation to resume after the additional arrangements, with another category of security work unfinished.

By August 2025, the two governments publicly combined those courses. Their accounts of the Special Representatives’ talks called for discussion of de-escalation, beginning with its principles and modalities. They also commissioned work on border management and possible early results in delimitation, and extended military mechanisms to additional sectors. India’s account listed intended steps on flights, visas, border trade and other exchanges. Those commitments did not demonstrate that all measures had been delivered. They did show that renewed cooperation need not await completion of every military adjustment.[^16]

In May 2026, diplomatic teams were still discussing delimitation, border management and cooperation while preparing another Special Representatives’ meeting. India described progress toward gradual normalization. Neither account announced a completed boundary settlement or supplied a census of forces withdrawn.[^17]

The Indian sequence consequently offers more than a counterexample to a national style. It shows the counterpart helping determine what a familiar practice can accomplish. Appeals to the larger relationship did not automatically override India’s security demands; neither did those demands prevent every other exchange. Senior access mattered because officials used it to press for work through diplomatic and military channels. Local agreements mattered because they specified actions while leaving other questions open. The negotiator’s task was to decide which unfinished question still justified withholding the next benefit—and to recognize that the other government might value the same step differently.

## Russia: whose island, whose hay?

On the Argun River, accepting a boundary meant deciding what would happen to the grass. Russian residents had used Menkeseli, a large island and sandbar area near Sredneargunsk, for cattle pasture and hay. The 1991 border agreement placed it on the Chinese side of the settlement. For the people accustomed to using it, the diplomatic achievement threatened an established livelihood. A line that satisfied two foreign ministries could still provoke resistance from residents and soldiers expected to live with it.[^23]

Russia's regional authorities did not all answer that problem alike. Primorye governor Yevgenii Nazdratenko campaigned against territorial transfers. In Chita, the local Border Guard consulted the regional administration and proposed preserving residents' economic use while recognizing the transfer to China. Akihiro Iwashita reconstructs this initiative from regional reporting: Chinese negotiators accepted the idea early, before its eventual incorporation into a bilateral framework. In July 1995, Russian demarcation chief Genrikh Kireev publicly praised Chita's cooperation with the commission and the local military. An official seeking to implement the settlement had found local allies as well as local opponents.[^22]

The proposed accommodation separated two questions that the territorial argument appeared to make inseparable. China could exercise sovereignty over the place. Established Russian users could retain permission to carry out limited activities there. Neither side had to pretend that the border had disappeared. The arrangement made the consequences of accepting it less absolute.

This was possible because much of the legal argument had already been settled. The eastern agreement, signed on May 16, 1991, left two sections for later negotiation while committing the parties elsewhere to a line and an equal joint commission charged with putting it on the ground. Surveyors still had to locate river channels, assign islands, erect markers and prepare maps. The western agreement followed in 1994. By 1997 the governments could announce completion of demarcation in the agreed eastern sectors without settling the reserved islands near Khabarovsk and Abagaitui.[^19]

Such progress did not require China to forget its history. Deng Xiaoping's published account of his May 1989 conversation with Mikhail Gorbachev coupled a forceful description of past injury with acceptance of existing treaties as the basis for a boundary settlement. Historical condemnation and a workable legal starting point could coexist. Nor did the subsequent division of the negotiation into stages establish why Beijing accepted it. Fravel emphasizes the increased value of Soviet cooperation after Tiananmen; Iwashita traces substantive convergence to talks reopened in 1987. The practical fact was that agreement in one area could proceed while another remained unresolved.[^18][^20]

The Menkeseli proposal addressed a different obstacle: making an agreed allocation tolerable. A November 1997 framework led to the detailed agreement signed by foreign ministers Tang Jiaxuan and Igor Ivanov on December 9, 1999. Its opening definition placed sovereignty first: the state possessing the territory would allow the other country's established border residents to pursue traditional economic activities. China designated four areas in the Menkeseli island group and an island in the Amur for Russian use. Russia made Verkhnekonstantinovsky Island and adjoining waters available to Chinese users. The accommodation was reciprocal, though that does not establish equal economic value.[^24]

The permissions were deliberately narrow. Only established users qualified; their total numbers could not rise. They could cut hay, fish and cultivate crops. They could not turn a seasonal activity into unrestricted access, erect new buildings or reconstruct existing facilities. Host-country law applied, and permitted activity could not limit the sovereign state's own right to develop and use the area. The benefit offered to residents therefore came with an unmistakable change in their position: continued access would depend on the authority of the country now recognized as sovereign.

The permit rules make that change tangible. A Russian user needed a crossing pass from Russia's border representative and an activity permit issued through the designated Chinese regional authorities—the governments of Inner Mongolia and Heilongjiang. In the opposite direction, China's Ministry of Public Security issued crossing passes and the Amur regional administration issued activity permits. Crossing the frontier and working beyond it were different permissions, assigned to different offices. Each permit specified the person, place, activity and working time.[^B-new1]

The governments also committed themselves to a timetable. Their authorities were to request permits within sixty days of the rules taking effect; the receiving authority was to issue them within fifteen days of a request. Yet the next provision explicitly preserved the right to refuse any individual applicant. A deadline could discipline processing without eliminating sovereign discretion. Work would normally take place in daylight. An overnight stay required advance agreement, except in an emergency or natural disaster. At the end of the work, equipment and produce were to return to the sending country. These were arrangements for access and removal, not an invitation to permanent settlement.

Duration supplied another bargaining margin. Iwashita reports that an initially contemplated ten years became five. The signed text confirms five years from the beginning of use, with the dates to be agreed diplomatically. It also required consultations at least twice annually to resolve practical difficulties. In October 2004, the governments returned to the question: a new protocol identified March 1, 2005 as the existing end date and extended use for another five years from that date. Temporary accommodation had become renewable through another express agreement.[^B-new2]

The surviving instruments do not tell us how many people obtained permits or what happened to every application. They establish the promised terms of access and renewal, with the territorial settlement intact.

There were limits to exporting the solution. The reserved islands still required agreement on sovereignty itself. Nor could protection of selected livelihoods answer every objection to transferring territory. Near Khabarovsk, the disputed islands lay beside a major Russian city; agricultural land, airport approaches, defense installations and the political meaning of surrendering control all entered the argument. What could be eased through a permit was only part of what officials had to defend.

The final settlement also shows what a usable mandate looked like. On October 13, 2004, Putin accepted a Foreign Ministry proposal coordinated with federal agencies and the executives of Khabarovsk, Chita and the Jewish Autonomous Region. His order authorized the ministry to negotiate and sign three attached instruments, permitting changes that did not alter their principles. The next day, Li Zhaoxing and Sergei Lavrov signed the supplementary boundary agreement. The Russian minister had a defined mandate over specified drafts; the public record does not disclose the equivalent Chinese approval traffic.[^26][^27]

Regional coordination did not mean regional unanimity. On October 22, *Kommersant* reported that a Khabarovsk legislative committee wanted federal parliamentarians to resist ratification. Governor Viktor Ishaev, who had accompanied the Russian delegation to Beijing, defended the result by saying that the airport approach, fortifications, vegetable-growing land and dachas remained on Russia's side. He also welcomed the legislators' expression of concern. His position illustrates the political work between agreement at the center and acceptance at home: defending retained interests while acknowledging that a loss had to be explained.[^28]

When Lavrov presented the settlement to the State Duma in May 2005, he again emphasized the inhabited and economically used areas retained by Russia. Military and border officials addressed different concerns about defense and unauthorized crossings. The Duma approved the agreement by 307 votes to 80. Ratification and entry into force followed; the supplementary demarcation protocol came in July 2008, and the October exchange of notes and handover brought the later instruments into effect. The line agreed in 2004 still required legal approval and fieldwork before transfer.[^29]

Both governments had conceded, against different starting points. China accepted less than the whole area it claimed. Russia relinquished control over part of the territory it held. Measuring only the land that changed hands would conceal China's retreat from its claim; measuring only that retreat would conceal the practical Russian loss. The local-use arrangement, meanwhile, offered a means of softening some losses without reversing sovereignty.

For someone negotiating with Beijing, the demanding question is whether an apparently indivisible demand contains interests that can be treated differently. Here sovereignty, working access and the duration of access could be specified separately. Doing so required the assent of governments and the participation of offices capable of issuing documents, inspecting users and managing complaints. It also left a real risk with the residents: their benefit was temporary and subject to the host's powers. That tradeoff is more useful than a general injunction to be flexible. An accommodation can help implement a settlement precisely because it preserves some interests while requiring others to yield.

## The price friendship could not settle

At the end of his visit to Shanghai on May 21, 2014, Vladimir Putin faced a question the signing ceremony had left unanswered. What had held up the gas agreement with China, and what price had finally been accepted?

He began with the larger relationship. The visit had produced dozens of agreements, and the gas contract was historic. The experts had worked until half past three in the morning and resumed in the morning. Chinese negotiators had bargained hard, he said; both parties had compromised. The deal was done, although government decisions were still required.[^33]

The questioner returned to the price. Putin described a formula but initially misstated its reference commodities. After answering questions about Ukraine, he noticed a prompt from his spokesman, Dmitry Peskov, and corrected himself: the formula was tied to a basket of oil and petroleum products. Asked how it compared with the European price, he again explained the mechanism. He did not disclose the coefficients that would determine the bill.

The exchange captures both the achievement and the unfinished work. Political leaders could make an agreement worth concluding, companies could agree a way of pricing deliveries, and each government could still have decisions to take. To assess what China had gained, a foreign negotiator needed to understand all three.

The agreement had a substantial history before Russia's confrontation with the West over Ukraine. In September 2013, with Putin and Xi present, Gazprom and China National Petroleum Corporation had agreed principal terms for the eastern route. Contemporary reporting described a binding document that left price unresolved. Gazprom hoped to complete the sales contract that year; the target passed.[^31] The leaders had endorsed an important intermediate agreement. It had not resolved the term on which the project depended.

The Chinese company had a problem of its own. A January 2014 corporate question-and-answer exchange hosted on CNPC's website explained why the project remained attractive and why profitability could not be read from the border price alone. Domestic selling prices, pipeline operating costs and taxes also mattered. Another answer described efforts to obtain more favorable price and tax policies from the Chinese authorities.[^gas-company]

This was a constraint a counterpart could investigate. CNPC's listed subsidiary, PetroChina, reported a net loss of about 41.9 billion yuan on imported gas sales in 2013, despite an operating profit in its broader gas-and-pipeline segment.[^gas-accounts] The previous June, China's price regulator had explained that low domestic prices discouraged imports. Its reform raised non-residential prices while protecting household prices and phasing the adjustment for existing consumption.[^gas-regulator] Buying more gas served a national purpose; recovering its cost required decisions about what Chinese customers would pay. A concession from the Russian seller was one possible source of relief. Chinese price and tax policy was another.

Contemporary Chinese reporting described the final bargaining in these terms. *China Economic Weekly* quoted a person close to the Chinese negotiators who said that Russia faced considerable pressure to finish during the visit, but China faced pressure too. The source claimed China had defended an affordability limit while accepting a price short of its preferred result. The same account described compromise on both sides.[^gas-reporting] An unnamed source cannot establish the private reservation price. It does, however, complicate the public image of one side simply waiting for the other to capitulate: the claimed limit and the preferred outcome were different things.

The bargain announced on May 21 assigned costly work to each party. Gazprom would develop Russian fields, processing facilities and transmission; CNPC would build the Chinese pipeline and associated storage. The thirty-year eastern contract envisaged deliveries rising to 38 billion cubic metres annually. The contemporary Chinese account expected supply from 2018, a date the later operational record would overtake.[^32] The route opened a market for eastern Siberian resources. It did not immediately give Russia a way to divert gas already moving westward to Europe.[^30]

Gazprom's $400 billion valuation gave the agreement a memorable size. It supplied no public basis for calculating the price concession China had extracted. Dividing that figure by thirty years of maximum deliveries would ignore the gradual increase in supply and the oil-linked formula. Russia's worsening relations with the West made a Chinese agreement more valuable, but that context alone cannot reveal how much it changed the accepted price.[^34]

Financing offered a further possible exchange. Two days after signature, Gazprom Export chief Alexander Medvedev said a $25 billion prepayment had been agreed in principle, with details under discussion. In November, Alexei Miller said an advance had been considered in connection with reducing the gas price but was no longer under consideration after price settlement. Chinese bank loans remained a possibility.[^35] The available record shows a financing option disappearing; it does not establish breach of a completed Chinese payment obligation. For the negotiator, Miller's explanation identifies the trade that would need to be priced: money earlier in return for cheaper deliveries later.

The eastern contract became an operating relationship. In December 2015 the companies were agreeing arrangements for the border crossing, communications and technical work. Supply began in December 2019. Gazprom subsequently reported 38.8 billion cubic metres delivered in 2025, and specially agreed quantities to maintain supply during scheduled maintenance in April 2026.[^36] These were corporate reports, not an independent audit of the contract. Yet they demonstrate what the relationship had made possible: gas delivered through infrastructure built for that purpose, with further coordination needed to keep it moving.

## The cost of another route

The result encouraged expectations about a second bargain. The original western proposal would bring western Siberian gas toward Xinjiang through the Altai region. A framework followed in November 2014 and heads of agreement in May 2015. Keun-Wook Paik's contemporary study treated stronger political support as reason to expect progress. But the same December 2015 corporate release that recorded implementation work on the eastern route scheduled further commercial negotiations on the western one.[^37] Friendship could sustain both conversations while only one supplied a completed sales contract.

The routes presented different commercial choices. As Vitaly Yermakov and Michal Meidan explain, gas entering western China would arrive in a region already served by Central Asian pipelines, far from major eastern demand centers. Reaching those customers required more transport. A later proposal through Mongolia offered access to northern China, but required another major pipeline and a Chinese purchase agreement. Engineering work and permissions in Mongolia could prepare the route without securing the customer.[^38]

China nevertheless accepted another Russian transaction in February 2022: a long-term sales agreement for the Far Eastern route, initially for an additional ten billion cubic metres annually. That project drew on a different supply geography. Gazprom announced it alongside a completed feasibility study for the Mongolia project; the latter remained a study.[^39] A buyer's refusal to close one transaction need not mean rejection of the supplier or the strategic relationship. The location of available resources, the infrastructure required and the competing sources of supply could make another transaction preferable.

In March 2023, Putin said almost all parameters of the proposed Power of Siberia 2 agreement had been settled. The joint statement used a more limited formulation: advance research and consultation on the trilateral pipeline. Neither disclosed agreed commercial terms.[^40] In 2024, the *Financial Times* reported Chinese demands concerning price and the amount of capacity Beijing would commit to buying. The accessible Reuters summary attributes those claims to the newspaper's sources; it supplies no independently obtained term sheet.[^41] The reported disagreement concerned who would bear price and demand risk over the life of a dedicated pipeline.

Miller announced another advance in September 2025: a legally binding memorandum on construction of Power of Siberia 2 and its Mongolian section, Soyuz Vostok, with prospective capacity of fifty billion cubic metres a year. He separately announced prospective increases on the eastern and Far Eastern routes.[^42] The full memorandum remains unavailable. Its announcement cannot tell a prospective supplier how much gas the customer must purchase, on what schedule or with what remedy if it does not.

The subsequent Russian explanation was explicit about work still to be done. On September 3, 2026, Deputy Prime Minister Alexander Novak said commercial terms remained under discussion and the final routing depended on agreement between Gazprom and CNPC. The following day, he described continued preparation of the contract and government help toward concrete company agreement. Russian reports were now using the name Power of Baikal.[^43] The public political commitment had still not produced the completed commercial package at that point.

Chinese planning documents also distinguished stages. The March 2026 national plan called for construction of the Far Eastern pipeline and preparatory work on the middle-route project. The July oil-and-gas plan placed imports alongside domestic production, diversification and flexible operation.[^44] These are stated supply priorities, not a disclosed reservation price. They help identify the alternatives a seller must examine before deciding that its own urgency should be shared by the buyer.

For a negotiator, the practical question is what must change to make waiting less attractive than agreement. A lower price might help, but so might a different route, less exposure to unwanted volumes, financing on better terms or a domestic decision allowing costs to be recovered. The record does not establish that each delay was calculated to extract a concession. It establishes why a valuable political relationship could support years of work without determining which allocation of commercial risks either side would accept.

The eastern pipeline belongs in that judgment as much as the unfinished proposal. Russia and China could carry out a large agreement and remain unable to close the next one. Knowing the strength of their relationship did not dispense with knowing the business.

## What could be separated

Each negotiation required a decision about which interests could be treated separately. India could agree to disengagement at one confrontation point without accepting that normal relations should resume. Russia and China could settle sovereignty while preserving specified uses for residents of the other country. They could also complete one gas contract without accepting the commercial terms of another. A relationship helped define what was worth pursuing; it did not make those choices interchangeable.

The distinction mattered because an apparent concession could move the cost elsewhere. Temporary permission could protect a livelihood while leaving the user exposed to expiry and individual refusal. An advance payment could ease the supplier's immediate financing problem in exchange for a lower future price. Accepting border progress as sufficient for wider cooperation could surrender a condition that the other side still wanted removed. Before proposing that interests be separated, a negotiator needed to decide whether the resulting accommodation would remain valuable on its own.

The practical test is in the terms. Specify the area from which troops will withdraw, the authority that will issue a permit, the date on which use ends, or the volumes a buyer must pay for. Seek approval for that defined obligation and make reciprocal commitments conditional on it where the bargain requires. Such requests cannot expose every private instruction. They can reveal whether the parties have agreed enough for the intended next step to occur.

Senior intervention must be judged against the same test. The Russian order authorized specified drafts; Modi and Xi endorsed an arrangement already announced; gas declarations left commercial terms to be completed. The visitor must distinguish permission to finish from encouragement to continue.[^45]
