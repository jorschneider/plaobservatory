# 3. What expertise makes possible

In August 2012, the United States published the results of its work to comply with a Chinese victory at the World Trade Organization. One line seemed to tell an extraordinary story. Zibo Aifudi, a producer of laminated woven sacks, had received a countervailing-duty rate of 29.54 percent in the original American investigation. The revised determination put it at 83.34 percent. China had won important legal arguments, and the American subsidy calculation for this exporter had risen by 53.80 percentage points.[^12]

Those were determination rates, not a record of taxes actually paid before and after the ruling. Elsewhere in the same notice, an anti-dumping adjustment moved in the exporter's favor. Yet the apparent paradox poses the commercial question more sharply than the language of victory: what had China obtained that a company trying to sell into the United States could use?

The answer lay in the decisions the ruling required Washington to reconsider—and the decisions it left Washington to make again.

## Learning to use membership

In February 2002, China's leaders gathered provincial and ministerial officials to study the organization they had joined. Jiang Zemin warned that inexperience would bring losses. He wanted specialists in trade law, negotiations and anti-dumping investigations, and demanded urgent recruitment and training. The published speech linked that investment to a second task: changing how Chinese governments managed the economy. WTO rules constrained members' policies, he told them; officials would have to reduce direct intervention and learn different ways of supporting industry. Expertise was part of a political program for improving China's competitive position.[^capacity1]

The work extended beyond the government payroll. Gregory Shaffer and Henry Gao's research describes how the commerce ministry paired Chinese firms with experienced foreign counsel. Chinese lawyers obtained access to earlier submissions and legal databases, helped establish the facts of Chinese measures and commented on drafts. Third-party participation in other countries' disputes supplied another apprenticeship: lawyers could study the arguments before China had to defend an equivalent measure itself. From 2011, the ministry also brought young law professors into its legal department for one-year assignments, giving them work on actual disputes.[^capacity2]

The relationships mattered inside government too. Foreign counsel needed information from the agencies responsible for a challenged measure. Commerce officials arranged meetings and brought those agencies into WTO hearings. Interviewees described this participation as a way to prepare ministries to accept an adverse finding. Expertise could help explain why a policy was vulnerable without giving the lawyers power to order its removal.[^capacity3]

Membership thus gave a growing professional network work to do, and that network made membership more useful. An objection to an American restriction could become a developed legal claim rather than remain a request for accommodation. The counterparty now had to reckon with the treaty argument and the procedure available to test it. DS379 shows how consequential that change could be—and how much bargaining could remain after China won.

## The work a victory leaves behind

China's challenge covered four groups of products: two categories of steel pipe, off-the-road tyres and laminated woven sacks. The United States was applying countervailing duties against alleged subsidies while calculating anti-dumping duties using its non-market-economy method. The former sought to offset subsidies. The latter compared actual export prices with a constructed normal value using values from outside China. If a subsidy had already increased the calculated dumping margin by lowering the export price, adding a countervailing duty against the same subsidization could offset it twice.[^2]

China challenged this combination and the findings supporting the subsidy duties. Its lawyers disputed what made a state-owned enterprise a governmental “public body,” how to value benefits and how the two duties interacted. The April 2009 submission summary argued from the treaty's wording, principles of legal attribution and the distinction between ownership and governmental authority.[^3]

The appellate ruling in March 2011 gave those arguments force without dictating a tariff. State ownership alone did not establish that an input supplier was a public body. The relevant inquiry concerned governmental authority. That required Commerce to do more than identify a controlling shareholder; it did not exempt state-owned suppliers from subsidy investigations. The distinction mattered immediately: China succeeded against the ownership-based findings concerning input suppliers, but failed to establish the same inconsistency in the finding concerning state-owned commercial banks, which rested on a broader record.[^5][^6]

On double remedies, the appellate tribunal rejected Washington's interpretation and required investigation of whether, and to what extent, the same subsidy had been offset twice. It described duplication under the method as likely, rather than inevitable in every instance. It did not tell Commerce to subtract every subsidy dollar automatically.[^8]

In Washington, the favorable interpretation therefore became another investigation. Commerce's account records the resulting dispute with China. The agency asked about Chinese laws, the conduct of enterprises and their relationships with government. China supplied material but objected that the requests were excessive and that the agency had not adequately explained what evidence would matter. Commerce replied that the fuller inquiry followed from China's successful objection to treating ownership as sufficient. The winner had obtained a more demanding test; it now faced demands for information under that test.[^DS-new1]

The reopened land inquiry shows how this could work against an exporter. The WTO panel had rejected reasoning that made land-use rights regionally specific simply because land occupied a designated place. It explicitly allowed that better evidence of a distinct regime might justify another finding.[^DS-new5] During implementation, China supplied a 1992 Huantai County circular setting land prices in different districts. It argued that Aifudi's price reflected applicable law and transactions in a neighboring county. Commerce saw a different implication. It said the circular, absent from the original investigation, helped establish a distinct pricing regime inside the county. Prices remained fixed over a long period, the lowest rate also applied to foreign-invested enterprises, and other material linked preferences to investment. China disputed aspects of the evidence and its applicability. Commerce maintained that the land benefit was specific enough to countervail.[^DS-new1]

This explains how an adverse WTO finding could produce a renewed American determination. It does not explain the 53.80-point rise. For the revised countervailing rates, the final memorandum points to a separate calculation concerning petrochemical inputs and the public-body inquiry. That calculation's detailed attachment has not been recovered. The defensible conclusion is narrower: the ruling did not prevent Commerce from reaching another subsidy finding, and the final table cannot by itself establish the precise contribution of changed supplier classifications, valuation or other inputs.[^DS-new1]

## An adjustment opposed from both sides

The anti-dumping calculation offers a more visible result. American producers urged Commerce to grant no double-remedy adjustment. They argued that China and the respondent companies had not shown that subsidies reduced export prices in the relevant industries. Commerce disagreed. Information about how subsidized raw materials entered the companies' accounts, combined with Chinese submissions about industry conditions and broader manufacturing data, gave it a basis for an adjustment.[^DS-new1]

This was a consequential administrative choice. Commerce was implementing a new statutory provision under a compressed timetable. It acknowledged that its manufacturing-sector estimate was an imperfect substitute for measuring how particular subsidies affected particular prices. It nonetheless accepted the evidence as sufficient for these proceedings, over the objections of the American petitioners.

China wanted more. It disputed the restriction of the analysis to input subsidies, challenged the comparison of percentage changes in costs and prices, and argued for a full offset. Commerce rejected those arguments. A subsidy that reduced a producer's costs dollar for dollar did not necessarily lower its selling price by the same amount, the agency reasoned. Changes in output, demand for inputs and market conditions could intervene. China contested the agency's economic framework as well as its legal interpretation.[^DS-new1]

The resulting table placed Aifudi's original weighted-average dumping margin of 64.28 percent beside a revised anti-dumping cash-deposit rate of 20.19 percent. These are different measures; subtracting them does not establish how much less an importer actually paid. The new instructions applied prospectively to covered entries from August 21, 2012, and intervening administrative reviews could supersede the listed rates. Still, the memorandum records a real decision to recognize an adjustment, not merely a promise to study one.[^12][^DS-new1]

Expertise had mattered at more than one stage. Before the appeal, China's proposal for an inflation adjustment had persuaded Commerce to alter a loan benchmark used in the tyre investigation, although other Chinese suggestions failed and the benchmark's overall legality remained unresolved. After the appeal, submissions helped support an anti-dumping adjustment that American producers opposed. Neither result depended on persuading Washington to accept China's whole description of its economy.[^7]

The practical difficulty was preserving that gain. In 2016, after American domestic litigation and a voluntary remand, Commerce withdrew the sacks anti-dumping adjustment; the Court of International Trade sustained the redetermination. A new notice listed 64.28 percent for the Aifudi exporter–producer combination, applicable from April 11 where an intervening review had not superseded it. This was a domestic proceeding, not a WTO judgment deciding whether the United States had complied with DS379. It demonstrates why a favorable interpretation and an initial implementation decision could leave further work for the exporter and its government.[^DS-new4]

## Keeping another route open

Four years earlier, China had prepared for continuing disagreement. On May 11, 2012, ambassadors Yi Xiaozhun and Michael Punke signed an agreement specifying how a dispute over compliance would proceed.[^1] China could request a compliance panel without repeating consultations; if it chose consultations, the governments would meet within fifteen days. The United States would accept establishment of the panel at the first relevant Dispute Settlement Body meeting. Later authorization to suspend concessions depended on a further adverse compliance finding and other specified steps. Washington waived a timing objection while preserving rights to contest the proposed suspension.[^11]

The arrangement gave China a defined procedural option without requiring the Americans to admit noncompliance. It also required China to pursue review before the contemplated retaliation. Precision served both parties: it reduced a future argument over sequence while preserving their opposing substantive positions.

At the WTO on September 28, China's representative put those elements together. She rejected the American claim of full compliance, welcomed its offer to discuss Chinese concerns, recalled the May agreement and reserved China's right to take further steps. The American representative repeated the compliance claim and the offer to talk. Discussion, legal objection and a reserved route to adjudication coexisted in the same exchange.[^DS-new2]

Chinese legal scholar Chen Weidong drew attention to the strategic problem soon afterward. A favorable interpretation could be valuable even when commercial relief remained limited; further litigation demanded an assessment of its likely return, its evidentiary burden and the time it would consume. His account was an argument about how China should act, not evidence of why officials chose a particular course.[^DS-new3]

For a negotiator, the preparation begins before victory: identify what the counterpart would have to change if the argument succeeded, what it could lawfully decide again, and what evidence would be needed in that second proceeding. A demand for fuller explanation can produce useful disclosure and expose weak reasoning. It can also produce a stronger rationale for substantially the same restriction. The advantage of a review procedure is that the counterpart must defend a decision under an agreed standard; its cost is that another defensible decision may still leave trade obstructed.

China was using membership to contest the interpretation of rules and to preserve procedures for further challenge. That is a substantial development from negotiating entry. It is not proof that legal skill had displaced political bargaining, nor that the legal victory had restored profitable access for Aifudi. It had changed what the United States was required to answer, while leaving both governments decisions about how far to carry the dispute.

The route through the WTO has itself become a bargaining question. As of September 2026, its Appellate Body had no members, and an appeal could prevent a panel report from being adopted. China and the European Union participate in an interim appeal-arbitration arrangement; the United States does not. Arbitration under Article 25 requires agreement between the parties. Chinese membership alone therefore cannot give it that route against Washington. The American appeal in the tariff dispute DS543, filed in October 2020, illustrates the obstacle.[^appeal-1] With Europe, the alternative has been used. China and the EU agreed procedures for their intellectual-property dispute in 2023; a final award followed in July 2025. Their agreement required them to abide by it.[^appeal-2] Even then, legal finality did not settle implementation. China announced domestic steps while criticizing the award; the EU continued to assess compliance.[^appeal-3] A negotiator considering litigation must therefore ask two separate questions: what procedure has the other government accepted for reaching a final ruling, and what will still be needed to turn that ruling into changed conduct?

The climate negotiators faced the complementary problem. They were trying to agree on procedures that did not yet exist in their final form. Their task was to make a proposed obligation acceptable to governments before anyone could use it to demand an answer.

## Making two different promises together

In January 2014, John Kerry asked Todd Stern what the United States should attempt next with China on climate change. Stern's team proposed a joint announcement of national targets when Barack Obama visited Beijing that November, a year before the Paris climate conference. Stern liked the idea but doubted whether China would want to associate itself so closely with Washington when it also had developing-country partners to consider. In his later account, he approached Xie Zhenhua in February; Kerry raised the proposal with Xi Jinping and other Chinese leaders. An opening existed. Agreement did not.[^20]

The proposal gave the specialists a problem that neither atmospheric science nor personal rapport could solve by itself. They had to find national actions their own governments would authorize and the other government would accept as sufficient for a public partnership. Each president would be lending political weight to the other's effort. A domestic target could be defensible at home yet too weak for a counterpart to endorse. Conversely, a target impressive abroad could be impossible to authorize or deliver domestically. Technical work had to make those judgments better informed.

Stern and Xie had been building their working relationship since 2009. Stern recalled that Chinese officials initially wanted to know whether he really spoke with authority. Hillary Clinton's treatment of him on her first Asian trip helped establish his standing.[^20] That was a practical matter. Time spent exploring an option with a well-informed official would be wasted if someone else could simply disown it. The question for each envoy was whether the other could carry a proposal into the decisions of his government.

## What Copenhagen left unfinished

They had already experienced a more disorderly way to reach agreement. At Copenhagen in December 2009, Obama negotiated with Wen Jiabao and the leaders of Brazil, India and South Africa. Michael Froman, an American participant, recalled that he and Stern thought they had an agreement but were unsure whether China had accepted the listing of its commitments. A note passed through Clinton to Obama, who asked Wen to confirm. Wen agreed. Xie then loudly protested to his premier. Froman took this to mean that Wen had exceeded his mandate, but acknowledged that the Chinese interpreter would not translate the exchange.[^copenhagen-froman]

Shyam Saran, India's climate envoy, remembered the immediate issue differently: Wen was accepting international consultation and analysis. Saran understood Mandarin and recalled Xie objecting to the demands on China and the inadequacy of what Obama offered. In both accounts, Wen let his acceptance stand.[^copenhagen-saran] The witnesses had seen a disagreement inside the Chinese delegation. Neither supplied its written instructions. What the encounter established was narrower and more useful: the specialist's objection did not prevent the premier from accepting the compromise.

The compromise also had to survive explanation at home. Four days later, India's environment minister, Jairam Ramesh, faced questions in Parliament about what his government had conceded. He acknowledged moving beyond the information reporting he had previously promised to accept. His defense rested on the protection of sovereignty in guidelines still to be negotiated, and on a collective decision by Brazil, South Africa, India and China—the BASIC group—to avoid responsibility for the conference's failure. He described pressure from countries seeking climate finance after the Americans linked funding to a transparency agreement.[^copenhagen-ramesh] China was negotiating alongside partners with their own limits and audiences. Holding the coalition together required deciding which concession its members could defend.

The Chinese government's published account likewise distinguished a national target kept outside bargaining from flexibility over transparency.[^14] That substantive distinction matters alongside the institutional explanation. Björn Conrad's later interview study describes foreign-policy advisers displacing climate specialists in the handling of the summit, but also identifies reasons to resist the proposed distribution of emissions cuts. The presence of an expert could improve the advice available without settling either the allocation of burdens or who would choose the final position.[^17]

The Copenhagen Accord nevertheless provided for international consultation and analysis of information about developing-country actions. It distinguished that process from the international measurement, reporting and verification attached to actions receiving international support. He Jiankun, a senior Chinese climate expert, publicly described acceptance of consultation and analysis as a flexible adjustment while maintaining the objection to equivalent international verification. He also acknowledged that the guidelines remained to be negotiated. The choice was already more precise than accepting or rejecting scrutiny altogether.[^16]

Copenhagen supplied a further warning for any bilateral initiative: agreement among major leaders could fail to secure assent from the whole conference. The COP took note of the accord rather than adopting it by consensus. Subsequent conferences carried the work forward. Cancun in 2010 specified a facilitative, non-punitive consultation process that excluded judgment on the appropriateness of domestic policies. Durban in 2011 authorized negotiation of an outcome with legal force applicable to all parties. Stern and Xie's later work took place within that developing multilateral framework.[^18]

Stern and Xie were also using machinery with a history. A memorandum released in July 2009 had provided for regular policy consultations and ministry involvement; the governments reported a climate policy dialogue in 2011. In April 2013 they gave the envoys a more specific assignment: chair a Climate Change Working Group, review existing cooperation and prepare proposals for the Strategic and Economic Dialogue. The July program identified sectoral tasks, required implementation plans defining agency responsibilities, and specified at least two group meetings and one report to the dialogue each year. Senior representatives of both presidents approved it. Stern later described the annual dialogue as a deadline for producing agreements.[^19] The envoys had a program to prepare and officials responsible for its parts. They still needed an acceptable proposal to put before their leaders.

## National choice and reciprocal acceptance

The 2014 proposal asked the two envoys to use that relationship for a larger purpose. Stern recalls roughly nine months of confidential preparation and a shared understanding that neither president would endorse a joint announcement unless comfortable with the other's ambition.[^20] Confidentiality allowed discussion before either government had publicly committed itself to the package. It did not give the envoys authority to choose the national numbers on their own.

Xie's contemporary account supplies the Chinese side of that distinction. Interviewed by *Caixin* three days after the announcement, he foregrounded Zhang Gaoli's September message to Obama and the teams' subsequent exchanges. He said they had agreed not to negotiate one another's targets, then acknowledged that some discussion was unavoidable. Both sides' experts assessed the other's effort as well as their own. A joint release required mutual acceptability. The statement was finalized on the announcement day, he said.[^climate-xie2014]

Xie also rejected the idea that a Chinese target emerged from a single technical calculation. Researchers had spent nearly two years examining the emissions peak and disagreed: some put it in 2025, others around 2030, and others thought 2030 unattainable. The central leadership and State Council chose among competing considerations. In his explanation, the target was also intended to push domestic economic restructuring. International bargaining operated alongside a Chinese policy project with its own reasons for action.[^climate-xie2014]

The announcement released in Beijing on November 12 paired visibly different intentions. The United States intended an economy-wide greenhouse-gas reduction of 26–28 percent below its 2005 level by 2025, with best efforts toward 28 percent. China intended carbon dioxide emissions to peak around 2030, with best efforts to peak earlier, and non-fossil fuels to supply about 20 percent of primary energy consumption by 2030. Neither the gases covered nor the form and date of the targets were identical. The document did not convert them into a common percentage reduction. It joined them to a commitment to work toward a successful Paris outcome.[^21]

The arrangement let both governments preserve national choice while putting their intentions before the other participants well before Paris. Its diplomatic value lay partly in this sequencing: a counterpart could see a stated course of action instead of waiting for a final summit to discover what the other might offer. The announcement also connected ambition to further technical cooperation. Whether either country would achieve its target was a later question; announcing it together was an immediate political act.

The record does not show a numerical trade in which an American increment purchased a specified Chinese increment. It shows an agreement to announce nationally chosen actions together, conditional on finding each other's effort acceptable. For a negotiator, it identifies a possible transaction even when the counterpart insists that a particular domestic decision is not itself for negotiation.

## Taking a bilateral formula into a multilateral negotiation

An immediate test came within weeks at Lima. Observers who expected the presidential announcement to dissolve established divisions were disappointed. The *Earth Negotiations Bulletin* found that high-level signals had not immediately produced fundamental changes in negotiating positions. Differentiation—how responsibilities should vary among countries—remained disputed, as did finance. The bilateral announcement had created a resource for negotiation; it had not instructed every other government to agree.[^22]

Stern and Xie did find a use for its language. Reporting from the close of the conference, Ed King described a late meeting between the two envoys and quoted Stern saying they had offered the formulation that reached the final text. It coupled common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities with consideration of different national circumstances. Stern did not expect the dispute to disappear. UN climate chief Christiana Figueres saw an important opening in the formulation; India's Prakash Javadekar emphasized the preservation of differentiation and expected further accommodation from developed countries. The same words could support different expectations about the next bargain.[^climate-king2014]

The adopted Lima decision confirms the language but leaves consequential matters open. Its arrangements for intended national contributions were without prejudice to their legal nature and content. That reservation explains why an apparent success over a general principle still required work. Countries had accepted a formula through which responsibilities could be discussed; they had not agreed every obligation that would follow from it.[^22]

By September 2015 Obama and Xi could endorse a more specific approach to transparency. Their second joint climate statement supported reporting and review of both action and support, with flexibility for developing countries needing it in light of their capacities. This made capacity and need relevant to flexibility, while retaining differentiation. It gave negotiators a more definite proposition than either identical treatment for every country or a general developing-country exemption.[^23]

Finance remained differently structured. The same statement urged continued support from developed countries and encouraged other willing countries to contribute. The United States reiterated its pledge to the Green Climate Fund; China announced a separate South–South cooperation fund. The countries could both offer assistance without accepting identical responsibilities or using the same institution. A useful compromise on transparency did not have to settle finance on precisely the same terms.[^23]

At Paris, those distinctions were still being fought over. China and Saudi Arabia objected to a proposed facilitative dialogue that they feared could become advance review of national contributions. The United States and European Union presented it as information for the next round. China's broader position also insisted on balance across the negotiations' components and leadership by developed countries. Agreement with Washington on a presidential statement had not eliminated concern about what a new procedure might authorize.[^24]

The conference required other negotiators to work through these problems. Under the French presidency, Brazil's Izabella Teixeira and Singapore's Vivian Balakrishnan facilitated consultations on differentiation across mitigation, transparency and finance. On December 9 Balakrishnan reported that parties were still withholding their final positions. The task was not simply to transmit a bilateral bargain into a global text. Different governments had to judge how the obligations fitted together, and whether their own priorities had sufficient protection.[^24]

The treaty text adopted on December 12 made that fit more explicit. Parties would have to prepare, communicate and maintain successive nationally determined contributions and pursue domestic measures aimed at their objectives. Achievement of each announced national result was not itself an internationally binding obligation. Reporting and review requirements made a different kind of demand, with capacity-related flexibility; the compliance mechanism was facilitative and non-punitive. Developed countries retained a distinct obligation to provide financial resources, while other parties were encouraged to provide support voluntarily.[^25]

Those distinctions answer the problem Stern and Xie had been working on. National discretion over ambition could coexist with international obligations about stating, pursuing and reporting action. A negotiator could recommend accepting a review procedure without conceding that other governments would set the country's emissions target. Another could obtain more information without securing an enforceable promise that every target would be met. The value of the agreement depended on knowing which of those results had actually been obtained. Adoption of the text was itself only a stage; consent to be bound and delivery of national action followed through separate decisions.

The comparison with the trade dispute is practical. In DS379, a Chinese argument could reach an adjudicator despite American opposition. Stern and Xie were negotiating rules whose acceptance still depended on governments with different interests. Their specialists could estimate effort, identify feasible policies and draft a procedure that preserved a political limit. Their leaders could authorize the package. Neither technical proficiency nor a strong relationship could supply the assent of the rest of the conference. What they made possible was a joint proposal for cooperation—and a way to keep negotiating the points it had not resolved.

## What a specialist can change

An official could use a legal standard to demand a new answer, or technical work to recommend a proposal that another government might endorse. The first required an available institution; the second required political assent.

Before investing in another round of expert work, a negotiator needs to identify the decision it could alter. Better evidence might change an administrative calculation, reveal that a proposed target was credible, or show why an apparent concession would be unusable. Where the disagreement concerned what a government was willing to accept, further calculation could clarify the price without supplying the authority to pay it. In a crisis, that distinction could become urgent. Prueher knew a great deal about aircraft; getting his crew home required another kind of decision.
