# 5. A partial settlement under pressure

In the Oval Office on February 22, 2019, Liu He found himself agreeing with the American trade representative while the American president disagreed. The subject was the document they were negotiating. Robert Lighthizer explained that a memorandum of understanding was a contract. Liu said yes. Donald Trump objected: the memorandum would have to become a final contract before it meant much to him. Lighthizer offered a solution. They would keep the document and call it a trade agreement. Liu accepted the new name too.[^25]

The exchange was comic, but the problem beneath it was serious. What would make another promise worth accepting? To Trump, the title was inadequate. To Lighthizer, the document needed a way to make China perform. To Beijing, an acceptable agreement had to offer relief from American tariffs while preserving a defensible measure of control over China's own affairs. The negotiators were bargaining over the authority each government would retain after they signed.

Lighthizer made his concern concrete five days later, before the House Ways and Means Committee. Companies would bring complaints to his office, sometimes anonymously because they feared the consequences of being identified. Officials would meet monthly; unresolved matters would rise through quarterly meetings of deputies to the trade representative and a Chinese vice premier. If the principals could not agree, he wanted the United States to be able to act proportionately and unilaterally. He was proposing a means of dealing with the next dispute before the present one was over.[^26]

It took most of the year to reach an agreement containing such a procedure. In May the negotiation broke down over a draft whose disputed provisions have never been made available to this project. In October Trump accepted a first phase. In January 2020 he and Liu signed it. China undertook specific changes and substantial additional purchases; the United States accepted a limited settlement and left substantial tariffs in place. Each government kept instruments with which to press the other. Their success lay in finding a bargain they could accept without settling the conflict that had brought them to the table.[^2]

## The pause that did not hold

The problem of what had been promised was already a year old. On May 19, 2018, Liu, acting as Xi Jinping's special envoy, concluded talks with Treasury Secretary Steven Mnuchin, Commerce Secretary Wilbur Ross and Lighthizer. Their joint statement described work on purchases, market access, investment and intellectual property. It expressly contemplated amendments to Chinese laws, including the Patent Law. It supplied neither an overall purchase figure nor a tariff suspension.[^3]

The next day, both sides publicly described a pause. Xinhua reported Liu saying they had agreed against a trade war and additional tariffs. Mnuchin told an American television audience that the trade war was on hold while the governments developed the framework. Other American officials emphasized what remained to be done: Larry Kudlow denied that a completed trade deal existed, and Lighthizer stressed structural reform.[^4]

Liu had an American statement to point to, but no published common text specifying how long the pause would last or what would keep it in force. That left room for the next disagreement. On May 29 the White House announced that it would impose 25 percent tariffs on $50 billion of Chinese goods. After Ross returned to Beijing, China's June 3 statement reported progress while reserving final confirmation of the details. It then attached an explicit condition: if the United States imposed trade sanctions, including tariffs, the negotiated outcomes would not take effect. On June 15 Washington confirmed its tariff decision and threatened further action if China retaliated.[^5]

For an official preparing the next Chinese offer, this sequence created a problem that a cordial relationship with Mnuchin could not solve. Would an understanding reached with one American official survive the president's decision and the demands of the rest of his administration? For the American side, accepting a pause without a completed structural bargain risked surrendering pressure for purchases and further discussion. These were different tests of whether the talks were succeeding.

Beijing's September white paper made American changes of position part of its indictment. It paired a case against tariff pressure with a declaration that China would continue opening its economy at its own pace. China was claiming both willingness to negotiate and the right to control the terms of its reform.[^6]

Neither the later indictment nor the published May statement settles the full scope of the oral understanding. The practical weakness is nevertheless visible. Both sides had announced restraint without publishing the conditions under which it would endure. The June Chinese statement tried to remove that ambiguity from the package then under discussion: the additional goods and the absence of new American sanctions were to travel together.

## Permission to negotiate

Trump and Xi reopened the process over dinner in Buenos Aires on December 1. The American account described postponing a scheduled tariff increase while the teams negotiated structural issues for approximately ninety days. It promised substantial Chinese purchases but said the amount remained unagreed. Wang Yi's account presented the leaders' understanding as principles from which negotiators would develop a concrete agreement. He described a pause on additional tariffs, purchases responsive to China's needs, and talks toward removing the additional duties. He gave no purchase figure or ninety-day deadline.[^7]

The leaders had supplied political permission to try again. Their teams still had to decide what China would change, what the United States would lift, and how either side could respond to a failure. Lighthizer continued to stress enforceability in his February testimony. As late as April 23, the White House's announcement of another negotiating visit listed purchases and enforcement among the subjects still to be discussed.[^8]

The tariff deadline itself was negotiable. In February, with Liu present, Trump said he could extend it if the talks made sufficient progress. The subsequent tariff notice records the postponement to March 2 and then until further notice.[^25][^9] Chinese officials could see that the clock was under presidential control. That knowledge gave them a reason to test what an extension would cost; it did not make the threat of higher duties harmless.

Solomon taught American negotiators to look for the moment when a Chinese leader entered a negotiation and detailed drafting began. Both were visible here, yet neither answered the question that would destroy the spring bargain: had the governments accepted the same obligations, or were their delegates still working on terms that one principal would refuse? A senior encounter can authorize more bargaining. An agreement still requires an authorized text.

In March 2018, Beijing had converted its central foreign-affairs leading group into a commission, chaired by Xi and charged with coordinating policy and supervising implementation. The published arrangements did not disclose which terms Liu could accept or what approvals his draft required.[^authority-2018]

## The missing agreement

On May 8 Reuters reported that Beijing had sent back extensive revisions to a draft approaching 150 pages. Its sources described changes across seven chapters, especially the removal of commitments to change Chinese laws. Liu, they said, proposed administrative and regulatory action instead. Mnuchin and Lighthizer considered that inadequate. A Chinese official familiar with the talks disputed the accusation of backtracking, emphasized the procedures required to amend laws, and said American demands had become harsher. Subsidies and biotechnology approvals were still sticking points.[^10]

The domestic form of a promise was worth fighting over. If a concession depended on an administrative instruction, a foreign company would need to know who had received it and whether another official could reverse or disregard it. A published legal change could offer a different basis for challenging an adverse decision. For Chinese negotiators, an American demand to prescribe changes to domestic law could carry costs that the same substantive commitment, implemented through Chinese procedures, did not. Neither concern was answered by renaming the document.

Washington treated the revisions as a retreat from commitments already made. Its May 9 tariff notice put that accusation into an operative decision: the additional rate on the specified goods would rise from 10 to 25 percent, under the notice's entry and export conditions.[^9] Liu continued to Washington for the May 9–10 round. Afterwards, he identified three unresolved Chinese concerns: all additional tariffs should be removed; purchases should be realistic; and the text should be balanced and respect national dignity. He also disputed the legitimacy of accusing a side of retreat while negotiations were still in progress.[^11]

Each of Liu's concerns affected the price of the other two. Beijing was being asked to accept domestic changes and substantial purchases while Washington sought to retain the means of coercion. Beijing wanted the existing additional tariffs removed; Washington wanted a means of imposing costs if China failed to perform. The parties were disputing the allocation of risk as much as the wording of reforms.

China's June white paper defended revisions to an unfinished agreement as normal negotiating practice. It alleged changes in American demands and rejected what it described as interference in sovereign affairs.[^12] That defense did not require denying every deletion reported by Reuters. A negotiator can alter a draft and maintain that no promise has been broken. The answer depends on whether the earlier language was accepted, conditional or awaiting clearance.

Here the documentary history has a real break. Reuters reported from government sources and people briefed on the talks; it did not publish the draft. The record available to this book cannot settle whether Liu had exceeded his instructions, Xi had reversed an authorized bargain, or American officials had mistaken provisional agreement for final assent. It does establish a serious conflict over obligations, domestic implementation and the compensation offered for accepting them. Treating the episode as a demonstration of Chinese dishonesty would conceal those unresolved choices. Treating it as mere misunderstanding would minimize the costs of the breakdown.

Before pricing the whole package, an official can ask both delegations to record which provisions they regard as accepted, which depend on the rest of the bargain and which await a decision in their capitals. Such a record cannot verify private instructions or prevent reversal. It can expose a difference over the status of the draft while there is still time to negotiate its consequences. A refusal to give that account leaves a risk to be priced; it does not by itself prove deception.

## The bargain becomes smaller

By September, according to reporting in the *Wall Street Journal*, Chinese officials were seeking to separate the trade negotiation from national-security disputes. The public result emerged in the Oval Office on October 11. Trump was now defending a deal in phases. Lighthizer told reporters that Huawei was outside the agreement.[^13]

The details of the meeting revealed both a concession and an unfinished bargain. Mnuchin said Liu had requested, and Trump had approved, suspension of the increase from 25 to 30 percent scheduled for the following week. The December tariffs remained a separate presidential decision. Asked about enforcement, Lighthizer described an agreed structure for consultations and escalation, but said the consequences of an unresolved dispute still needed final work. Mnuchin added that Liu had work to do with his own team. The president's announcement had not disposed of those questions.[^1]

In December Chinese officials presented the first-stage agreement as a useful exchange. Some planned and existing American duties would be removed. Stronger intellectual-property protection, financial opening and imports could serve Chinese development; the agricultural package also contained benefits for Chinese exports. The officials defended the bargain as compatible with domestic reform, rather than a surrender of the right to direct it.[^14]

This was less tariff relief than Liu had demanded in May. But China was also accepting a different package: a first stage with further disputes left outside it. Washington had changed its demand for closure too. Trump defended division into phases and kept tariffs as pressure for performance and subsequent negotiation.[^15] The two governments had reduced the number of disagreements that had to be settled at once.

The relief came in separate acts. The December notice suspended the duties planned for December 15. The January 22 notice then prescribed a cut from 15 to 7.5 percent on the relevant List 4A goods, effective February 14. Announcing relief, suspending a future charge and reducing an existing rate were different concessions, delivered on different dates.[^16]

Partial agreement was familiar territory for Solomon. The lesson from 2019 is more particular than an injunction to be flexible. A position presented as indispensable to a comprehensive bargain may be negotiable in exchange for a smaller set of obligations. The test is to change the package and ask for a price. Demanding that the other side first renounce its principle can make it harder to discover which transactions remain possible within it.

## Whose reform?

The signed agreement offered a way to combine Chinese domestic control with specified changes. Article 1.34 left each party to choose appropriate implementation methods within its own system and practice, including proposing legal amendments where necessary. It also required full implementation. China had discretion over means, not a general exemption from the promised obligations.[^17]

The intellectual-property chapter shows what that meant. It addressed evidentiary and criminal-enforcement rules, confidentiality and penalties. Many provisions required Chinese changes while affirming that existing American measures already provided equivalent treatment. Equal standing as a party did not mean an equal number of new domestic burdens.[^18]

The agreement therefore required China to prepare an intellectual-property action plan specifying measures and effective dates within thirty working days after entry into force. Its English and Chinese verbs differ over promulgation and formulation; the shared requirement for a dated plan is clear. The plan would give officials a schedule against which to pursue implementation.[^17]

After the signing, Liu described the deal to Chinese reporters as consistent with China's reform program. In the same briefing, he said that reform would proceed under Communist Party leadership and the socialist system. This was his public explanation of how the concession fitted the political order he represented.[^27] The foreign negotiator should take both parts seriously: a reform may be useful to Chinese leaders without entailing acceptance of the foreign government's preferred economic system.

Lighthizer had described a similarly specific boundary to Congress. When Linda Sánchez asked about obtaining a larger share of Chinese box-office receipts for American studios, he called revenue-sharing a priority. Distribution was more complicated. He said the negotiation was not taking on “challenging control in China.” The terms remained unresolved. His answer described a commercial objective to pursue within a political limit; it did not announce a film concession secured in the subsequent agreement.[^28]

Lighthizer had himself told Congress that the American approach should assume there were Chinese reformers with whom it could work. He also insisted on an enforceable process.[^26] Sympathy for a counterpart's stated objectives and protection against failure were compatible. The unresolved historical question is how much domestic support particular provisions commanded. The practical obligation is to design an agreement that does not depend entirely on guessing the answer.

## The right to complain

The enforcement problem had a history. On March 2, 1995, Congressman Toby Roth pressed Charlene Barshefsky on whether Beijing could enforce the intellectual-property bargain just reached with Washington. He then distinguished ability from will. Barshefsky was confident in Beijing's ability and cited factory closures as evidence of its will. That will would have to be tested continually. Her proposed response combined pressure and vigilance with technical assistance and an American presence on the ground.[^ip-before]

The agreement already prescribed domestic enforcement machinery. Right holders were to have published contacts, access to progress information and a fifteen-day deadline for a task force to accept a petition or give written reasons for refusing it. That was a deadline for taking up the complaint, not for deciding its merits. Both governments undertook to supply enforcement statistics and consult regularly. Washington rescinded its scheduled tariff action while expressly retaining monitoring and the possibility of further Section 301 action if implementation proved unsatisfactory. Detailed administration, private complaints and the prospect of renewed pressure were part of the earlier bargain.[^ip-before]

Lighthizer's concern about frightened companies reached the final text in a specific form. An appeal could omit identifying information about a company, and appeals and related material were to remain confidential unless the governments agreed otherwise. A written complaint still had to contain enough information for an assessment. These provisions acknowledged that the government seeking enforcement might need to protect the firm whose experience supplied the evidence.[^19]

That is a concrete difficulty for anyone negotiating commercial access in China. An enterprise seeking permission from Chinese authorities may also be the enterprise expected to document their failure. A foreign government cannot assume that every beneficiary will volunteer a complaint, or that omitting its name will make it unrecognizable. Before treating a new obligation as enforceable, the negotiator needs a feasible way to learn what happened to the firms meant to benefit. Confidentiality can help; it cannot, by itself, remove their exposure.

The agreement gave the complaints a route. Bilateral offices would attempt resolution, then deputies, then the US trade representative and a designated Chinese vice premier. Working-level and deputy-level deadlines ran from receipt of an appeal—twenty-one and forty-five calendar days—not as consecutive allowances. Urgent matters could go directly to the principals. If an urgent meeting could not be scheduled in time, the complainant could proceed under the remedial provision. Refusing a meeting could not indefinitely hold that route closed.[^19]

The route ended in political judgment. After failed consultations over a response to losses, the complaining party could suspend an obligation or adopt a proportionate remedial measure. If the respondent considered the action taken in good faith, it could not counterrespond or otherwise challenge it. If it considered the action taken in bad faith, the specified remedy was withdrawal by written notice. A separate provision allowed termination by notice, normally after sixty days. Neither government had entrusted the good-faith question to an independent judge.[^20]

The 1995 American notice had reserved Washington's return to pressure under American law. Phase One added a bilateral agreement about the respondent's next move: no counterresponse to a measure it judged taken in good faith, or withdrawal over one it judged taken in bad faith. The earlier published letters and action plan contained no equivalent provision. This was the particular change the comparison establishes; neither confidentiality nor pressure-backed enforcement was itself new.[^ip-before]

Compared with the WTO procedure examined earlier, Phase One left its bilateral dispute in the hands of the governments imposing and bearing its costs. It could permit action without waiting for an independent ruling. It could also turn a dispute about one obligation into a choice about the survival of the agreement. Those were consequential terms to settle before accepting the underlying duties.

Both settlements formalized the continuation of negotiation after signature. Phase One's procedure was bilateral, and the United States had publicly pressed for it. Its existence does not establish his stronger claim that China habitually insists on the other's adherence while seeking indulgence for itself. That requires the record of actual complaints and responses. The confidentiality clause also prevents silence in the public record from establishing that the procedure went unused.

## What the numbers promised

Purchases gave the agreement its most conspicuous test. Trump had spoken of $40–50 billion in annual agricultural sales in October. At China's December briefing, a Reuters correspondent asked about $50 billion; the Chinese answer deferred disclosure of the figures. That question was not a Chinese undertaking.[^21]

The final schedule required covered purchases and imports in 2020–2021 to exceed the corresponding 2017 baselines by at least $200 billion. The number was an increment across the two years. Agriculture's scheduled increases were $12.5 billion in 2020 and $19.5 billion in 2021. A separate note recorded an undertaking to strive for another $5 billion annually. Projected growth in 2022–2025 did not renew the same quantified annual minima.[^22]

The text also acknowledged market prices and commercial considerations, American facilitation of supply, and consultation over conflicting data and circumstances in the United States affecting Chinese performance. Those qualifications did not expressly erase the numerical requirements. They identified questions that would matter when either government tried to explain a shortfall.

Here the negotiators had promised a result dependent on many transactions. A regulator could issue a permission; it could not make a soybean price, an American shipment and a Chinese buyer's demand interchangeable. A purchase commitment might justify asking the government to remove an obstacle or arrange additional buying. Judging its performance required knowing which goods and services counted, which benchmark applied, and what the government had undertaken to do when commercial conditions changed. The impressive number left those tasks to the officials who would live with it.

## Bargaining for the next day

Was Phase One evidence that tariffs had taught China to take American demands seriously? Cathy Wu's bargaining account makes learning about resolve a candidate explanation. Angela Huyue Zhang's contract analysis asks a different question: could the parties observe performance and credibly punish a failure? Both help identify what needs explaining. Neither supplies the private instructions missing from the May breakdown.[^23][^24]

The public sequence establishes changes in the bargain. China accepted partial tariff relief for a partial settlement. Washington accepted phases and provisions allowing domestic choice over implementation. The parties gave complaints a procedure and preserved room to contest the response. Those findings are more precise than a declaration that pressure either worked or failed. Pressure helped define the choices they faced; the documents do not isolate its contribution from the narrower package, the value of the concessions or changing political calculations.

For the next negotiator, the demanding part begins before signature. Identify which domestic act will make the concession useful, what evidence would show it has occurred, and how the affected firm can report a failure. Price the enforcement mechanism as part of the exchange. A threat costly enough to command attention may also be costly enough that one's own government hesitates to use it. A faster remedy can sacrifice independent judgment. A partial bargain can deliver something valuable while allowing the hardest dispute to persist.

Lighthizer could seek cooperation from reformers while demanding a way to act if cooperation failed. Liu could defend China's political system while accepting specific changes within it. Their bargain would reach officials and enterprises with choices of their own. The next chapter follows an opening that became an operating business, approvals that still required years of administrative work, and purchases that fell far short.
