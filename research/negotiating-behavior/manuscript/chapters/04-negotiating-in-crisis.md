# 4. Negotiating in a crisis

Joseph Prueher tried to explain the collision as a pilot. The American ambassador had thirty-five years of flying experience. Across from him on April 4, 2001, Foreign Minister Tang Jiaxuan insisted that an American reconnaissance aircraft had rammed a Chinese fighter. Prueher challenged the mechanics. A large, slow EP-3 could not have struck a more maneuverable aircraft in the way Tang described. The Chinese officials might have been given that account, he said, but the physics did not support it.[^draft-impasse]

Tang held his ground. China had facts and evidence. The United States owed an explanation and an apology. Prueher wanted to speak fully with the twenty-four Americans whose damaged aircraft had landed on Hainan three days earlier. China was keeping them there. He argued that understanding the collision was central to resolving the dispute. Tang said this was a diplomatic matter, not a technical inquiry into the pilot's state of mind.

Both men said they wanted a prompt resolution. Tang repeatedly said China sought a prompt resolution, while maintaining the condition that made one difficult. Prueher feared the damage each further day would do to relations. Their shared call for an end to the crisis did not establish its terms. They were still arguing over what the other government had to admit.

Prueher later recalled a visitor helping him escape that argument. Kenneth Lieberthal, a former Clinton administration official then in Beijing, advised him to stop trying to settle fault. Changing his approach required going back to Colin Powell, who had approved it. Prueher remembered reluctance followed by permission to proceed. Zhou Wenzhong, his principal Chinese counterpart, later described a leadership instruction to exchange an apology for the people. Neither recollection supplies the original instruction. The contemporary cables show the work of finding an acceptable exchange.[^7][^31]

## What could be conceded

The initial public demands had been broad. On April 3, China's Foreign Ministry called for acceptance of responsibility, an explanation, an apology and prevention of repetition. Bush demanded the return of both crew and aircraft and warned of damage to the wider relationship.[^2] Privately, Chinese officials also argued about the aircraft's movements, the evidence and the law. The disagreement cannot be reduced to American concern with facts and Chinese concern with appearances. The two governments disputed both the facts and what should follow from them.

For Washington, sympathetic language posed a particular problem. What could the United States say without accepting the Chinese legal argument? On April 6, acting Defense General Counsel Daniel Dell'Orto sent the defense secretary and deputy secretary guidance developed by Defense and Joint Chiefs lawyers in consultation with State's Legal Adviser. Its talking points expressed regret for the Chinese loss while defending the flight, emergency landing and aircraft immunity. The responsibility argument was partly conditional on the reported conduct of the Chinese fighter.[^3]

The lawyers also considered where their position should be stated. One passage specified an oral, off-camera response if pressed. A clearance sheet recorded agreement among State, Defense, the Joint Chiefs and the relevant regional office on aircraft-immunity guidance. A government could prepare a firm legal defense without putting every point into its most visible statement. This packet still demanded immediate aircraft release. It did not supply the arrangement that eventually brought the crew home first.

By the morning of April 7, the negotiators were discussing a draft letter from Prueher to Tang. Zhou presented Chinese revisions to the text delivered the previous evening. They had high-level clearance, he said; they were more than the Foreign Ministry's position. Prueher objected especially to changes that made the timing of the crew's release and the plane's return less clear. He would consult Washington immediately. At noon, he delivered a new American text that had been cleared there.[^draft-seven]

Approval had not ended the bargaining. It identified the standing of the proposal Prueher was being asked to accept. He still had to decide whether its words would obtain what his government needed.

The standing of the American negotiator was also at issue. Qian Qichen had suggested sending a special envoy. Zhou told Prueher that this was optional and should not be linked to the crew's release. On April 8, Prueher said Washington did not plan to send one. Zhou then requested a brief letter from Powell to Qian confirming the ambassador's full authority. Prueher agreed. The arrangement would allow the existing negotiator to finish the work with written evidence of his mandate.[^draft-eight]

At five that afternoon, Zhou again said China's leaders wanted the word *apologize*. Three hours later, after speaking to Powell, Prueher rejected it. He supplied a revised draft incorporating other Chinese changes. Zhou would report to his superiors immediately. Discussion of departure details, he said, required leadership approval of the package; the meeting with Tang would follow approval by leaders who were traveling.

These explanations gave Prueher a description of the process, but no control over its pace. The embassy thought China's leadership wanted another day or so for internal reasons. Its cable expressly presented that as an impression. To turn it into a demonstrated cause of delay would be to give the historian knowledge the negotiator did not possess.

On April 9, Zhou brought another request to strengthen the text, including an apology for entering Chinese airspace without permission. He also conveyed the leadership's anger at American discussion of consequences for China's Olympic bid, trade relations, WTO accession and Taiwan arms sales. Prueher replied that delay made the political damage worse. He warned that even the existing text could become harder to sustain and suggested that the agenda for future talks might mention aggressive Chinese interception tactics.[^draft-nine]

After another consultation with Powell, he accepted one minor change and rejected the others. The cable does not identify that change. Zhou acknowledged that the draft had improved considerably from the first American version, although it remained unsatisfactory. He would report and return with a decision. China's stated objection had survived several rounds; so had America's refusal of the requested word. Other revisions had continued around them.

## The letter and the assurance

An agreed text would leave another problem: how to exchange it for the crew. The American letter could be signed and handed over. China's assurance would be spoken. Prueher wanted to know what Tang would say before giving him the document.

On April 11, the embassy and the Foreign Ministry negotiated the sequence in three preparatory meetings. Lu Shumin first requested an advance photocopy of the signed letter, explaining that it would prevent misunderstanding at the ministerial encounter. The deputy chief of mission objected that providing it could amount to delivering the letter. At three in the afternoon, James Moriarty, the embassy's political minister-counselor, supplied an unsigned copy. Zheng Zeguang asked again for a signed one. Moriarty declined: even a copy of a signed document could acquire a life of its own.[^draft-transfer]

The Americans were also resisting uncertainty about the other half of the exchange. Moriarty warned that Prueher could leave Tang's meeting without a deal if the release assurance proved insufficient. Zheng said he could not disclose the minister's exact words. At a further meeting at 4:50 p.m., forty minutes before the scheduled encounter, he supplied more practical detail. The charter plane could arrive within twenty hours of delivery, perhaps fourteen or fifteen. The necessary procedures involved identifying the twenty-four crew members and signing a transfer memorandum.

That memorandum had already presented a difficulty of its own. Lu had described it as simple and said there was no time to negotiate changes. The deputy chief of mission nevertheless withheld agreement. After lunch, he and Zheng revised it by telephone, inserting *verbal* in the reference to the missing landing clearance and removing language thanking China for appropriate treatment of the crew. An administrative document could carry implications the main negotiation had carefully limited. The claim that there was no time to revise it did not survive the American objection.

At 5:30 p.m., Prueher met Tang. He first handed over Powell's letter to Qian, citing presidential authorization for his role. Then he asked Tang to confirm that the crew would be free to leave once he delivered his own letter. Tang requested the signed original first. Zhou said the assurance was included in Tang's prepared response. Prueher stated his understanding that the crew would be free and handed over the letter.[^draft-delivery]

Tang read his statement. It maintained American responsibility for the collision and said China would permit departure after the necessary procedures. At one point, the embassy reported, he used *daoqian* in Chinese while his interpreter said *very sorry* in English. The two expressions' different force was present inside the delivery meeting itself. Prueher expressly said that Washington did not share China's account of the incident.

He also asked what the departure procedures would require. Tang said they should not take long and expected the crew to leave the next day. As the meeting ended, Zheng confirmed that the charter could land at Haikou at six the next morning. The assurance had acquired a flight time.

The delivered letter preserved the American emergency-landing explanation and expressed sorrow for the Chinese loss and for the absence of verbal clearance. It recorded agreement to meet and Washington's expectation of prompt crew departure. April 18 was set for talks. A plan for returning the aircraft remained to be developed; acknowledging China's intention to raise reconnaissance was not a promise to end the flights.[^1][^4] The crew's departure had been separated from questions that neither government had conceded.

## An agreement that had to be explained

The Chinese government called the letter an apology and published selected passages using 深表歉意, an expression of being deeply sorry. It did not publish a complete Chinese counterpart to the English release. Omitting the American emergency-landing explanation was an act of selection, not simply an error in translating a parallel document.[^5]

At Sun Yuxi's April 11 briefing, a journalist asked the difficult question directly. China had demanded an apology—道歉—but had allowed the crew to leave after an expression of deep sorrow. Had Beijing compromised? Had Washington given other undertakings? Sun defended the official character of the American letter, invoked humanitarian considerations and emphasized that negotiation would continue. The government's account of success was being questioned even as it announced the decision.

In Washington, Ari Fleischer said Bush had approved everything in the letter.[^6] The public statement completed a picture partly visible in the cables: Prueher had returned repeatedly to Powell, and his final delivery included a separate letter attesting to presidential authority. There were two documents with different jobs. One established who could act; the other specified what the United States would say and do.

The Chinese approval process remains harder to reconstruct. Zhou repeatedly referred to instructions, reporting and leadership decisions. Those were contemporary assurances made to the American negotiator. They do not reveal the complete internal argument. Prueher later recalled using other Chinese channels when he doubted that Foreign Ministry reports were reaching the leadership; he did not know which had helped.[^7] Meanwhile, in Washington on April 10, State Department official James Keith declined to pursue a separate argument with a Chinese diplomat, explicitly preserving Prueher's role as the authorized channel.[^draft-channel]

Tyler Jost's institutional study argues that separated military and diplomatic information channels impaired Chinese crisis decisions.[^8] The distinction matters to a negotiator confronting an apparently immovable account: officials may be defending a deliberate position, working from information they cannot readily test, or doing both. The settlement does not resolve that causal question. Tang still asserted American responsibility as he announced the decision to permit departure. What changed demonstrably was the action China would take while maintaining that assertion.

## The aircraft followed a different clock

At the April 18 talks, China continued to press its account of responsibility and its objections to reconnaissance. The repetition did not itself repudiate the letter: the earlier arrangement had reserved those questions. The Americans had obtained the crew's release without obtaining the return of their aircraft.[^9]

Getting the aircraft back required another bargain. On April 24, Prueher suggested that an assessment might show flying it out to be easier than shipping it. According to the embassy's cable, Zhou called a flyout symbolically difficult, but said that was not the core issue. How and when the plane would return still had to be settled.[^aircraft-opening]

Washington gave Prueher a tightly bounded proposal the next day. Deputy Secretary Richard Armitage approved discussion of a document that would be politically rather than legally binding. There could be no apology, compensation for the lost Chinese aircraft or pilot, or side verbal agreement on reconnaissance flights. Washington reserved approval of the complete text before any commitment to sign. The proposed first step was inspection; the end of May was a target for return.[^aircraft-mandate]

Zhou wanted a broader record of responsibility, payments and preventing another incident. A jointly accepted account proved unnecessary for the next step. On April 29 he delivered a Chinese non-paper, acknowledged that it represented Beijing's view, and said he did not require an American response. It recorded disagreement over blame. He also announced permission for an assessment team to visit the aircraft. The paper preserved China's position while the permission made an inspection possible.[^aircraft-paper]

Permission remained specific. By April 30, the embassy reported that China had allowed an accompanying American defense attaché officer and room to extend the team's working time. But Chinese officials expressly ruled out flying the EP-3 from Lingshui. Even electrical power required argument. The Foreign Ministry first suggested local flexibility, then prohibited powering up. The Americans explained that assessment required power without running the engines. After repeated requests, the ministry returned the question to discussion in Hainan; a senior official said the ministry did not understand the technical issue and the relevant PLA specialists had already left for Haikou. Approval was still pending when the embassy reported.[^aircraft-power]

Prueher later remembered an opportunity to fly the repaired aircraft out in May that was lost after the American approach changed. Zhou remembered refusing it.[^31] The April cables establish an objection that became explicit, alongside accommodation over inspection. They do not settle whether permission was offered later. On May 29, China announced agreement in principle on removing the disassembled aircraft aboard a commercial An-124. Technical arrangements remained to be made.[^9]

The Pentagon's June 7 briefing gives some of that work a concrete form: runway loads, loading and flight requirements, technicians, equipment and local support. Rear Admiral Craig Quigley expected component removal to take weeks and reported no communications hindrance under the arrangements reached. China's July 3 statement then described completed disassembly and removal while payment negotiations remained open.[^10]

## In 2023, the channel became part of the dispute

Twenty-two years later, a Chinese balloon over the United States produced another confrontation about surveillance, sovereignty and responsibility. There was no detained crew to negotiate out. One of the things Washington wanted was communication itself: a military contact that would remain usable when relations deteriorated. Solomon had limited the transfer of his findings from the construction of cooperative relations to confrontation. Here the distinction was concrete. A channel for managing danger had become something governments could withhold.[^30]

The balloon episode began against an already damaged institutional background. In August 2022, after Nancy Pelosi's visit to Taiwan, the Chinese Foreign Ministry announced countermeasures cancelling arrangements for theater-commander calls, Defense Policy Coordination Talks and Military Maritime Consultative Agreement meetings. It also suspended cooperation in five other areas, including counternarcotics and climate. These were identified mechanisms, not the severance of all diplomatic relations.[^11]

The use of political conditions was older than these cancellations. In April 2001, Tang had questioned the purpose of maritime consultations if the Americans began by denying what China called facts.[^draft-impasse] By 2022, Beijing was publicly withholding a specified set of mechanisms. Their subsequent restoration would involve more than undoing the balloon incident.

On February 3, Beijing acknowledged that the airship was Chinese and expressed regret. It described a civilian research craft, limited in its ability to steer, that winds had taken off course. Its explanation invoked force majeure and unintended entry. It did not concede a surveillance mission. In Washington's account of Blinken's call with Wang Yi that day, the secretary acknowledged the regret but characterized the presence as an irresponsible violation that undermined his planned visit. He postponed travel while affirming the intention to engage when conditions allowed.[^12]

The American postponement is a necessary counterpart observation. Washington too could make a particular encounter conditional on political circumstances. The important difference was which encounters each side sought to insulate from the deterioration. The United States wanted military contact during a crisis even when a diplomatic visit had become untenable. Beijing publicly linked acceptance of a ministerial military call to the atmosphere the American response had created.

American aircraft destroyed the balloon on February 4. Austin's statement characterized it as a surveillance platform and explained the delay in shooting it down by danger to people on the ground. China's response protested the use of force, repeated the civilian and accidental-entry account and reserved further action. These were incompatible public justifications, not a mutually accepted description of an incident awaiting only a suitable apology.[^13]

The military refusal is directly acknowledged by China. On February 9, Defense Ministry spokesman Tan Kefei said the American use of force had failed to create the proper atmosphere for dialogue, and China had therefore not accepted the proposed call between defense ministers. This connects the refusal to a stated condition. It does not specify an apology, payment or operational concession that would satisfy that condition. The evidence establishes conditional access more securely than it establishes its price.[^14]

Nor was this an unanswered ordinary telephone in the simple sense sometimes suggested by the word *hotline*. Contemporary AP reporting described a process for requesting an encrypted call or video conference. A much-cited recollection of a phone merely ringing referred to an incident in 2009. The distinction matters: a technical connection can exist while political permission to use it is withheld. Building more reliable communication requires knowing which of those failures has occurred.[^15]

Diplomacy continued in February. Wang and Blinken met in Munich on February 18. The Chinese account described informal contact at American request and demanded correction of the damage caused by the use of force. The American account recorded demands about the airspace violation, concern about support to Russia and an insistence on keeping diplomatic dialogue open. The accounts corroborate the encounter while preserving disagreement. They rule out a chronology in which all senior communication stopped until June.[^16]

## Reopening one channel while another remained closed

Before Sullivan and Wang could use a strategic channel, their officials had to negotiate how it would work. Former Biden officials interviewed by Christopher Chivvis and Senkai Hsia described months of preparation: which subjects could be raised, how meetings would stay out of public view and what would be said afterward. Sullivan's ability to travel discreetly helped recommend him for the task. From Washington's perspective, predictable arrangements could do some of the work that missing trust could not.[^18]

The American account describes an initial small dinner in Vienna, followed by extended conversations. Within the US government, officials also had to decide which messages belonged in this channel and which should go through other departments. That choice mattered: a senior encounter could be consumed by subjects better handled elsewhere, while the issue requiring high-level attention went unresolved. These recollections make the American preparation visible; they do not supply Beijing's private assessment of the same arrangements.

The May 10–11 Sullivan–Wang meeting in Vienna supplies an observable result. Both governments recorded substantive exchanges and agreement to maintain the strategic communication channel. The American account emphasized responsible management of competition; the Chinese one emphasized removing obstacles and arresting deterioration. They could agree to continue a channel while differing over the purpose of the relationship it was meant to manage.[^19][^17]

Later that month, the proposed Austin–Li Shangfu meeting in Singapore remained unsuccessful. Li was under American sanctions, and the two governments disagreed over what that meant for contact. Answering a question about the proposed meeting on May 22, Foreign Ministry spokeswoman Mao Ning demanded withdrawal of sanctions to remove obstacles. In Washington, State spokesman Matthew Miller denied that lifting Li's sanctions was being considered. American officials maintained that the sanctions did not prevent official meetings. Washington offered contact without delisting Li; Beijing called for sanctions relief to create the conditions for dialogue.[^sanctions-may]

The Chinese Defense Ministry's May 31 answer did not give a formula in which removing Li's sanction would guarantee the meeting. It emphasized principles, corrected American conduct and an appropriate atmosphere. It also stated that military contacts had not been interrupted altogether. The objects being withheld remained specific.[^20]

Blinken's June visit made the distinction still clearer. He met Qin Gang on June 18 and Wang and Xi on June 19. The Chinese account of the Qin meeting listed agreements to maintain senior exchanges, pursue consultations and working groups, encourage exchanges and discuss additional flights. Qin accepted an invitation to visit the United States at a suitable time. The practical work of arranging visits, flights and further consultations remained ahead.[^21]

On the military question, Blinken was explicit. Asked at his Beijing press availability whether the Chinese side had agreed to crisis or military communication channels, he said it had not and that there had been no immediate progress. He nevertheless defended continuing diplomacy. Access to Xi was compatible with failure to obtain this particular requested outcome.[^22]

The June statements also prevent a broader exaggeration. Wang called for an end to unilateral sanctions and suppression of Chinese technological development. Xi disputed the American conception of great-power competition. Blinken reaffirmed targeted national-security restrictions and the American Taiwan policy, including the Taiwan Relations Act, the three communiqués and the Six Assurances. Repeating opposition to Taiwan independence did not amount to accepting all of Beijing's demands. The encounter supported further engagement while leaving those positions in conflict.

## What an old friend could do

Henry Kissinger arrived the next month. Li Shangfu received him on July 18, Wang Yi on July 19 and Xi on July 20. Xi's welcome invoked his hundredth birthday, his many visits and the opening of relations more than fifty years earlier. Kissinger thanked Xi for choosing the Diaoyutai building associated with his first visit. The meeting offered a conspicuous example of personal recognition persisting across generations of Chinese leaders.[^23]

There was also a practical appeal. Xi asked Kissinger and other Americans favoring better relations to contribute to improving them. Wang called for diplomatic judgment and political courage associated with Kissinger and Nixon, while repeating demands on Taiwan. A longer account of the Li meeting, republished from the Defense Ministry, attributed support for better military communication to Kissinger. It did not announce that Li had authorized official talks. Access and advocacy were visible; a resulting instruction was not.[^24]

Kissinger reached a defense minister whom Austin had been unable to meet through the desired formal channel. Yet the official diplomatic track was already active: Blinken had met Xi in June, and Wang and Blinken met in Jakarta before Kissinger's arrival. The visit's meaning depended on what his particular access could accomplish, beyond the recognition on display.

Washington said he was not acting on the government's behalf. John Kirby nevertheless expressed interest in hearing what he had learned, and Blinken later acknowledged seeking his counsel in connection with China travel and work on artificial intelligence.[^25][^26] Private status did not make him irrelevant to serving officials. Nor does that acknowledgment identify a message from July that changed an instruction or produced the November agreements.

Kissinger's reception cannot stand for the access available to an ordinary counterpart. During research in the early Xi years, Dylan Loh encountered reports of tighter restrictions on informal contact with Chinese diplomats. A European diplomat described an intended private dinner becoming an encounter with the appearance of an official meeting. Other foreign diplomats reported Chinese counterparts arriving with colleagues; Loh observed accompanied meetings himself. Such constraints could change the way a relationship developed without demonstrating that the Chinese participant disliked the visitor or rejected the proposal. Loh also found room for disagreement before a decision, followed by discipline in defending it. The practical difficulty was to find a format the official could use and a proposal worth carrying through that process.[^loh-access]

An official considering an intermediary like Kissinger must decide what the person is being asked to do. Explain a proposal, test a warning, seek information and negotiate a commitment are different assignments. Kissinger's reception demonstrated access and a Chinese effort to enlist his help. The public record does not trace that help through to a particular decision. A valuable relationship remains a resource whose usefulness depends on the task it can perform.

## A summit agreement, then implementation

By autumn, the sanctions objection was explicit in the Defense Ministry's own account. On August 31, spokesman Wu Qian listed sanctions on Central Military Commission leaders alongside Taiwan arms support and American military activity near China. He demanded changes in American conduct while again denying that all military contact had stopped. These were stated obstacles; his answer did not identify which had to be removed before any particular mechanism could resume.[^sanctions-august]

Li's removal as defense minister on October 24 changed one part of the problem. The decree named no successor and gave no reason for his dismissal. Two days later, Wu announced planned exchanges with a visiting American defense official while continuing to invoke sovereignty, dignity and core interests. Working-level contact could be arranged while the ministerial seat was vacant. Li's departure did not itself announce a new policy on the suspended mechanisms.[^vacant-ministry]

At the November 15 Woodside summit, the two public accounts finally converged on named military mechanisms: high-level military communication, Defense Policy Coordination Talks, Military Maritime Consultative Agreement meetings and telephone conversations between theater commanders. The Chinese account specified equality and respect. This was more definite than a favorable description of the atmosphere, but the readouts supplied no guaranteed response time or unconditional promise that every mechanism would remain available during the next confrontation.[^27]

The following day, the Pentagon said Austin would meet his counterpart when one was named. The broader agreement had preceded the appointment Washington awaited. Practical work on meetings and calls still lay ahead. Removing an awkward interlocutor may have helped, but the public record does not establish that Li's dismissal caused the wider decision to resume communications.[^vacant-ministry]

The summit also produced commitments on counternarcotics and government AI discussions. One adjacent American concession can be documented particularly well. A Commerce rule removed the Ministry of Public Security's Institute of Forensic Science from the Entity List, effective November 16. The rule establishes the act and its date. Separately, a named State spokesman, Matthew Miller, explained that the listing had obstructed precursor-chemical cooperation and that China's proposed steps justified removal.[^28]

The documented exchange concerned counternarcotics; it does not establish the price of the military-channel agreement. Human-rights advocates still objected to lifting the restriction. Washington had accepted a policy cost for prospective cooperation whose value would depend on subsequent Chinese action.

The paired summit readouts continued to register disagreement over Taiwan and technology restrictions. Their value lies partly in that coexistence: a commitment to specified communication was compatible with unresolved rivalry. The parties had identified things they were prepared to do, rather than reached a comprehensive common understanding of each other's conduct.

On November 30, China's defense spokesman said the departments were communicating and coordinating implementation. Both the Chinese and official English formulations placed the resumption of the mechanisms ahead of them. An agreement to restore a channel had reached a follow-up stage; the statement did not establish that all the mechanisms had already convened.[^29]

## The next meeting still required a decision

Austin met China's new defense minister, Dong Jun, in Singapore on May 31, 2024. Both official accounts recorded the encounter and their opposing positions over Taiwan. The disagreement had not prevented this use of high-level military communication.[^channel-may]

In November, China declined a proposed ministerial meeting at the ASEAN defense gathering in Laos. Austin regretted the decision but did not read it as evidence of a broader future breakdown. China's defense spokesman assigned responsibility to Washington: the Americans could not damage China's core interests over Taiwan and expect military exchanges to proceed normally. An American official said Beijing had cited arms sales to Taiwan. The public accounts did not disclose the internal decision or establish what would have secured a meeting.[^channel-refusal]

Another mechanism had operated earlier that month. Both militaries confirmed maritime-safety meetings in Qingdao on November 6–8. These preceded the refused ministerial encounter; they could not guarantee that the next request would be accepted.[^channel-mmca] Woodside had produced contact without securing every subsequent appointment. For an official needing to address a particular problem, it mattered which level was available and what that level could do. Treating military communication as simply open or closed would miss that distinction.

Once the EP-3 crew had left, no later dispute could undo their departure. The military mechanisms restored after Woodside were different: each use still required cooperation. The 2024 record contained both successful contact and a refused meeting. In 2019, trade negotiators addressed a related problem by specifying what could happen when consultation failed. Their answer would put the survival of the agreement at stake.
