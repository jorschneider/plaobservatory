# 4. Negotiating in a crisis

On April 11, 2001, the Chinese government accepted an American ambassador's letter and authorized the departure of twenty-four American aircrew. It did not accept the American account of the collision that had brought their damaged reconnaissance aircraft to Hainan. Nor did Washington accept China's demand to stop reconnaissance. The letter left the aircraft's return to a further discussion and acknowledged that China intended to raise the flights. A crisis had produced an arrangement whose immediate value depended on separating decisions that the original demands had joined together.[^1]

Twenty-two years later, the presence of a Chinese balloon over the United States produced another confrontation involving disputed responsibility, sovereignty and surveillance. This time, no detained crew supplied the central object of a bargain. Instead, the availability of official communication became one of the disputed objects. Diplomatic contact continued while particular military contacts remained unavailable. A meeting with Xi Jinping in June did not deliver the military-channel agreement Washington sought; the November summit identified specific mechanisms to restore. The two episodes ask different questions about what an agreement can accomplish before the underlying dispute is resolved.

Solomon explicitly limited the transfer of conclusions drawn from building a cooperative relationship to bargaining under confrontation.[^30] Taking that qualification seriously requires more than finding familiar words about respect or friendship in a crisis. It requires reconstructing the actions each government sought, the conditions attached to them and the authority behind an eventual arrangement. These cases show why trust, access, communication and agreement cannot be treated as successive steps on one automatic path. Governments may communicate to repeat incompatible demands. They may authorize one action while reserving another. They may cultivate a private intermediary while declining the official contact the other government wants.

## A letter that did several different jobs

The April 1 collision between an American EP-3 and a Chinese fighter created multiple issues at once. The Chinese pilot was lost; the damaged American aircraft landed in China; the crew and plane remained there. Responsibility for the collision, permission for the landing, aircraft immunity, reconnaissance practices, return of the people and return of the machine did not have to receive the same answer. Initially, however, the public demands made them look like parts of a single confrontation.[^2]

On April 3, China's Foreign Ministry called for acceptance of responsibility, an explanation, an apology and prevention of repetition. Its statement advanced a specific account of the aircraft's movements and legal arguments about reconnaissance, entry and immunity. President George W. Bush demanded the return of both the crew and aircraft and warned of damage to the wider relationship. Neither side confined its argument to the emotional meaning of the incident. Both attached practical demands to an account of responsibility.

A contemporaneous American legal packet shows how conciliatory language could coexist with the defense of those positions. On April 6, acting Defense General Counsel Daniel Dell'Orto sent guidance to the defense secretary and deputy secretary. The cover memorandum described work by Defense and Joint Chiefs legal offices in consultation with State's Legal Adviser. The enclosed talking points expressed regret for the Chinese loss while defending the flight, emergency landing and immunity. Their attribution of responsibility was partly conditional on the reported conduct of the Chinese fighter.[^3]

The packet also makes one piece of interagency coordination visible. A clearance page recorded agreement by State, Defense, the Joint Chiefs and the relevant regional office on aircraft-immunity guidance. Another passage specified an oral, off-camera response if pressed. Officials were deciding not only which legal position to maintain, but where and how to state it. Sympathy and legal concession were separate choices within the American apparatus before the final letter was delivered.

This is evidence of preparation, not the missing sequence of negotiated drafts. The packet still demanded immediate aircraft release. Its separate legal sections on people and aircraft do not establish that its authors proposed releasing the crew first. To move from the packet to the final arrangement, the historian still needs to distinguish a legal defense from a bargaining instruction and a later accepted instrument.

The released April 11 letter, from Ambassador Joseph Prueher to Foreign Minister Tang Jiaxuan, performed several jobs. It conveyed sorrow for the Chinese loss. It expressed regret about the absence of verbal clearance for entry and landing, while describing a severely damaged aircraft following emergency procedures and acknowledging uncertainty about the full incident. It recorded agreement to meet, stated the American expectation that the crew would leave promptly, set April 18 for further talks and identified subjects for those talks.[^4]

The wording of the agenda was consequential. Developing a plan to return the aircraft was a future task, not a declaration that return had been completed. Acknowledging China's intention to raise reconnaissance was not an American promise to end it. Tang's acceptance announcement supplied the corresponding immediate action: China would allow departure after necessary formalities while maintaining that the incident was unfinished. The arrangement could be acted upon because it identified an action that did not require either side publicly to accept the other's full account.

The Chinese presentation added another distinction. The Foreign Ministry described a letter of apology and reproduced selected expressions as **深表歉意**. But this was a report selecting passages from the letter, not a complete Chinese counterpart to the entire English release. The missing emergency context therefore cannot simply be called a mistranslation of a full parallel text. Selection and interpretation were doing work alongside translation.[^5]

Chinese journalists recognized the issue. At Sun Yuxi's April 11 briefing, a question contrasted China's demand for **道歉** with **深表歉意** and asked whether China had compromised. Sun emphasized the official character of the letter, a humanitarian decision and continuing negotiations. The public record thus contains both the government's presentation of an acceptable American statement and a question challenging whether it satisfied the original demand. China's audience did not receive only one uncontested interpretation.

This supports a limited account of politically acceptable presentation. It does not prove that a particular translation caused release. The leaderships might already have decided to end a damaging detention dispute and then accepted language they could defend; alternatively, a change in language or package might have made authorization possible. The observed instrument identifies the compromise. The order of the private decisions would distinguish those explanations.

## Authority and the evidence of institutional friction

The American ambassador signed the letter, but the published chronology places approval above him. On April 11, White House spokesman Ari Fleischer said Bush had approved everything in it. He described Prueher delivering a signed copy and receiving verbal assurances of release early that morning in Washington time. The Chinese delivery report's Beijing time is consistent with that sequence. These are contemporary public accounts of authorization, not the original presidential clearance message.[^6]

Prueher's later interview gives the process more texture. He described rapid approvals from Colin Powell, advice from Kenneth Lieberthal to move beyond the dispute over fault, and additional Chinese channels. Yet his remembered wording about China not hearing a landing request differs from the released letter's reference to absent verbal clearance. The difference illustrates why a participant's recollection is valuable for locating decisions and witnesses, while the delivered text remains the better evidence for the words the government accepted.[^7]

There is a further complication for a study built around Solomon. Prueher explicitly recalled studying Solomon's work. His interpretation is therefore not an entirely independent confirmation of that framework: the framework helped the participant understand and perhaps conduct the negotiation. That possibility makes the interview more interesting, but changes what it can prove. Recollection organized through a theory cannot then be counted without qualification as an unprompted observation confirming it.

Tyler Jost's institutional explanation asks a different question: could Chinese military and diplomatic information be brought together and challenged before a decision? His EP-3 analysis attributes early miscalculation to separated channels and limited scrutiny. The acquired article and appendix supply a serious hypothesis, but not all the underlying cables, coded interviews or intelligence documents. A cited April 17 CIA assessment cannot explain the April 11 decision without establishing when the underlying information reached those who authorized release.[^8]

Three explanations remain distinguishable in principle. An information failure would require evidence about what reached the leadership and whether a corrected assessment changed its decision. Deliberate bargaining pressure would require evidence that officials understood the limits of their claims but withheld the crew to secure a particular concession. Concern for the relationship would require a decision connecting the costs of continued detention to acceptance of a limited settlement. A forceful public demand is compatible with all three. It cannot select the cause by itself.

The American legal packet provides a useful counterpart discipline. We can demonstrate coordination of a particular piece of guidance because we have its clearance record. We cannot infer either universal American coherence or universal Chinese incoherence from that fact. Institutional explanations become strongest when they trace a piece of information, an objection or an instruction through identifiable offices to a decision. Simply giving the bureaucracy a place in the story is not yet an explanation of the outcome.

## The aircraft followed a different clock

After the crew arrangement, China continued to press its account of responsibility and its objections to reconnaissance in the April 18 talks. Those demands show the limited scope of the earlier settlement. Their repetition did not itself repudiate a letter that had explicitly reserved further discussion.[^9]

Aircraft return then moved through several states. On April 29, China announced that it had completed evidence collection and would permit American personnel to inspect the plane. On May 29, it announced agreement in principle to remove the disassembled aircraft aboard a commercial An-124. Technical arrangements remained. Permission to inspect, agreement on a transport method and the completion of transport were distinct events.

The Pentagon's June 7 briefing gives part of the delay a concrete explanation. Runway load assessment, loading and flight requirements, technicians, equipment and local support all needed attention. Rear Admiral Craig Quigley estimated that component removal was still weeks away and reported no communications hindrance under the arrangements his team had reached. On July 3, the Chinese announcement described completed disassembly and removal, while payment negotiations remained open.[^10]

Solomon's attention to time and urgency remains pertinent: the crew's detention plainly gave the Americans a pressing objective. But the later aircraft timetable cannot be read simply as a national tactic of exhausting the other side. Here there were recoverable engineering tasks and an explicit American statement about cooperation on the working arrangements. Those observations do not exclude bargaining pressure elsewhere. They prevent elapsed time from serving as sufficient proof of it.

The case also places a limit on the proposition that China keeps negotiating after an agreement. It certainly did, as did the United States. But deciding an expressly deferred transport method is different from reopening an accepted promise. An account of implementation bargaining must first identify the obligation being implemented. Otherwise every later conversation becomes apparent evidence of revision, even when the original document authorized it.

## In 2023, the channel became part of the dispute

The balloon episode began against an already damaged institutional background. In August 2022, after Nancy Pelosi's visit to Taiwan, the Chinese Foreign Ministry announced countermeasures cancelling arrangements for theater-commander calls, Defense Policy Coordination Talks and Military Maritime Consultative Agreement meetings. It also suspended cooperation in five other areas, including counternarcotics and climate. These were identified mechanisms, not the severance of all diplomatic relations.[^11]

That baseline matters. Military channels were not all lost in a single February 2023 rupture, and their later restoration cannot be described simply as undoing the balloon incident. China had already publicly made some mechanisms contingent on American political conduct. An institution designed to manage danger could itself be withheld as a response to an objectionable act.

On February 3, Beijing acknowledged that the airship was Chinese and expressed regret. It described a civilian research craft, limited in its ability to steer, that winds had taken off course. Its explanation invoked force majeure and unintended entry. It did not concede a surveillance mission. In Washington's account of Blinken's call with Wang Yi that day, the secretary acknowledged the regret but characterized the presence as an irresponsible violation that undermined his planned visit. He postponed travel while affirming the intention to engage when conditions allowed.[^12]

The American postponement is a necessary counterpart observation. Washington too could make a particular encounter conditional on political circumstances. The important difference was which encounters each side sought to insulate from the deterioration. The United States wanted military contact during a crisis even when a diplomatic visit had become untenable. Beijing publicly linked acceptance of a ministerial military call to the atmosphere the American response had created.

American aircraft destroyed the balloon on February 4. Austin's statement characterized it as a surveillance platform and explained the delay in shooting it down by danger to people on the ground. China's response protested the use of force, repeated the civilian and accidental-entry account and reserved further action. These were incompatible public justifications, not a mutually accepted description of an incident awaiting only a suitable apology.[^13]

The military refusal is directly acknowledged by China. On February 9, Defense Ministry spokesman Tan Kefei said the American use of force had failed to create the proper atmosphere for dialogue, and China had therefore not accepted the proposed call between defense ministers. This connects the refusal to a stated condition. It does not specify an apology, payment or operational concession that would satisfy that condition. The evidence establishes conditional access more securely than it establishes its price.[^14]

Nor was this an unanswered ordinary telephone in the simple sense sometimes suggested by the word *hotline*. Contemporary AP reporting described a process for requesting an encrypted call or video conference. A much-cited recollection of a phone merely ringing referred to an incident in 2009. The distinction matters: a technical connection can exist while political permission to use it is withheld. Building more reliable communication requires knowing which of those failures has occurred.[^15]

Diplomacy continued in February. Wang and Blinken met in Munich on February 18. The Chinese account described informal contact at American request and demanded correction of the damage caused by the use of force. The American account recorded demands about the airspace violation, concern about support to Russia and an insistence on keeping diplomatic dialogue open. The accounts corroborate the encounter while preserving disagreement. They rule out a chronology in which all senior communication stopped until June.[^16]

## Reopening one channel while another remained closed

The leadership's knowledge of the balloon mission remains a different evidentiary problem. Contemporary reporting conveyed an American assessment that Xi and other senior Chinese officials might not have known about it over the United States. The underlying intelligence was not disclosed. Later interviews with former American officials support treating leadership awareness as an important question; they do not provide this project with an independently established account of Xi's information.[^17]

The March 2026 Carnegie study by Christopher Chivvis and Senkai Hsia adds useful American process evidence. Based on interviews with thirty-three former Biden officials, it describes negotiation over the strategic channel's agenda, confidentiality and public statements. The report expressly lacks Chinese internal records. Its value here is to make the work of preparing a channel visible, with the participants' American perspective retained. Their account should not be converted into a complete explanation of why Beijing accepted it.[^18]

The May 10–11 Sullivan–Wang meeting in Vienna supplies an observable result. Both governments recorded substantive exchanges and agreement to maintain the strategic communication channel. The American account emphasized responsible management of competition; the Chinese one emphasized removing obstacles and arresting deterioration. They could agree to continue a channel while differing over the purpose of the relationship it was meant to manage.[^19]

Later that month, the proposed Austin–Li Shangfu meeting in Singapore remained unsuccessful. Li was under American sanctions. Contemporary reporting placed those sanctions in the dispute, but the Chinese Defense Ministry's May 31 answer did not offer a simple formula in which removing one sanction would guarantee a meeting. It emphasized principles, limits, corrected American conduct and an appropriate atmosphere. It also stated that military contacts had not been interrupted altogether. The objects being withheld remained specific.[^20]

Blinken's June visit made the distinction still clearer. He met Qin Gang on June 18 and Wang and Xi on June 19. The Chinese account of the Qin meeting listed agreements to maintain senior exchanges, pursue consultations and working groups, encourage exchanges and discuss additional flights. Qin accepted an invitation to visit the United States at a suitable time. Each was a commitment at its own stage: a visit invitation, a discussion of flights and an agreement to continue consulting were not completed visits, operating services or settled principles.[^21]

On the military question, Blinken was explicit. Asked at his Beijing press availability whether the Chinese side had agreed to crisis or military communication channels, he said it had not and that there had been no immediate progress. He nevertheless defended continuing diplomacy. Access to Xi was compatible with failure to obtain this particular requested outcome. The observation belongs in a test of Solomon's strong forecast about senior intervention; it does not alone establish how reliable that signal is compared with others.[^22]

The June statements also prevent a broader exaggeration. Wang called for an end to unilateral sanctions and suppression of Chinese technological development. Xi disputed the American conception of great-power competition. Blinken reaffirmed targeted national-security restrictions and the American Taiwan policy, including the Taiwan Relations Act, the three communiqués and the Six Assurances. Repeating opposition to Taiwan independence did not amount to accepting all of Beijing's demands. The encounter supported further engagement while leaving those positions in conflict.

## What an old friend could do

Henry Kissinger arrived the next month. Li Shangfu received him on July 18, Wang Yi on July 19 and Xi on July 20. Xi's welcome invoked his hundredth birthday, his many visits and the opening of relations more than fifty years earlier. Kissinger thanked Xi for choosing the Diaoyutai building associated with his first visit. The meeting offered a conspicuous example of personal recognition persisting across generations of Chinese leaders.[^23]

There was also a practical appeal. Xi asked Kissinger and other Americans favoring better relations to contribute to improving them. Wang called for diplomatic judgment and political courage associated with Kissinger and Nixon, while repeating demands on Taiwan. A longer account of the Li meeting, republished from the Defense Ministry, attributed support for better military communication to Kissinger. It did not announce that Li had authorized official talks. Access and advocacy were visible; a resulting instruction was not.[^24]

The contrast must be drawn carefully. Kissinger reached a defense minister whom Austin had been unable to meet through the desired formal channel. He did not uniquely gain entry to Xi when all serving Americans were excluded: Blinken had met Xi a month earlier, and Wang and Blinken met again in Jakarta before Kissinger's arrival. The formal diplomatic track was already working. His visit cannot be made the origin of the entire reopening merely because it supplied the most evocative photograph.

Washington's public account also distinguished the private visitor from an envoy. State said Kissinger was not acting on behalf of the government and that the Chinese side had mentioned his plans during Blinken's June visit. Reuters reported John Kirby expressing interest in hearing what Kissinger learned when he returned. That was an anticipated opportunity for a debrief, not evidence that one had occurred or changed an instruction.[^25]

There is positive evidence of consultation too. In his November statement after Kissinger's death, Blinken acknowledged seeking his counsel in connection with travel to China and work on artificial intelligence. That is a serving official's retrospective acknowledgment of advice. It supplies neither the content and precise timing of the advice nor a link from the July visit to a policy decision. But it rules out treating the private status of the visit as proof that Kissinger had no access to American policymakers.[^26]

The case therefore separates several roles that the phrase *old friend* tends to bundle together. A person can be honored, consulted, encouraged to advocate, asked for information and authorized to negotiate. The roles can overlap, but one does not prove the next. China's public appeal establishes an effort to recruit advocacy. Establishing effective leverage over the recipient would additionally require his response and some consequence for an actual decision. Establishing no influence would likewise require more than the absence of a disclosed concession.

This is a bounded update to Solomon's relationship mechanism. Personal standing remained a resource, and Chinese leaders visibly cultivated it. The evidence recovered here does not trace the crucial passage from cultivation through a specific transmitted message to changed policy. Nor does the American interest in advice establish a uniquely Chinese way of using former officials. The episode gives us observable parts of the mechanism and a precisely located missing link.

## A summit agreement, then implementation

At the November 15 Woodside summit, the two public accounts finally converged on named military mechanisms: high-level military communication, Defense Policy Coordination Talks, Military Maritime Consultative Agreement meetings and telephone conversations between theater commanders. The Chinese account specified equality and respect. This was more definite than a favorable description of the atmosphere, but the readouts supplied no guaranteed response time or unconditional promise that every mechanism would remain available during the next confrontation.[^27]

The summit also produced commitments on counternarcotics and government AI discussions. One adjacent American concession can be documented particularly well. A Commerce rule removed the Ministry of Public Security's Institute of Forensic Science from the Entity List, effective November 16. The rule establishes the act and its date. Separately, a named State spokesman, Matthew Miller, explained that the listing had obstructed precursor-chemical cooperation and that China's proposed steps justified removal.[^28]

This is evidence of a particular tradeoff, not merely two events occurring close together. It still does not establish that the delisting bought the military-channel agreement. Different deliverables could involve different exchanges, and the evidence connecting the institute to counternarcotics should not be generalized to every subject at the summit. Nor did delisting establish that the underlying human-rights concerns had been resolved or that future drug-enforcement cooperation would work. Contemporary reporting included objections to the choice.

The paired summit readouts continued to register disagreement over Taiwan and technology restrictions. Their value lies partly in that coexistence: a commitment to specified communication was compatible with unresolved rivalry. The parties had identified things they were prepared to do, rather than reached a comprehensive common understanding of each other's conduct.

On November 30, China's defense spokesman said the departments were communicating and coordinating implementation. Both the Chinese and official English formulations placed the resumption of the mechanisms ahead of them. An agreement to restore a channel had reached a follow-up stage; the statement did not establish that all the mechanisms had already convened. This chapter ends at that boundary, rather than borrowing later contacts to make the November announcement appear complete when issued.[^29]

## What changes between the two crises

Both episodes produced useful action short of a shared account of the dispute. In 2001, the immediate crew arrangement left responsibility, reconnaissance and the aircraft to other decisions. In 2023, renewed diplomatic contact and commitments to restore military mechanisms coexisted with disagreement about Taiwan, technology and the balloon. The continuity is the possibility of a limited commitment whose scope matters more than a declaration that the relationship has improved.

The bargaining objects differed. In 2001, people and an aircraft could be separated and moved through identifiable release and transport decisions. In 2023, several of the objects were institutions for repeated contact. Their value depended on future willingness to use them, including at moments when politics made communication difficult. That creates a distinctive implementation question: an announced channel is useful in a crisis only if the relevant officials will authorize participation and can act on what they hear.

This is not a clean estimate of historical change in Chinese character. The incidents differed in location, loss, custody, political background and desired outcomes. The comparison instead identifies a change in the problem a counterpart must solve. Establishing contact and obtaining a usable commitment are separate tasks; with institutionalized channels, maintaining contact can itself become a continuing negotiation.

The sources also place different limits on explanation. The 2001 legal packet recovers part of contemporary American preparation; later participant and scholarly accounts suggest channels and institutional problems whose decisive records remain missing. The 2023 record gives unusually explicit public refusals and a later interview-based account of American preparations, but still lacks the Chinese authorization trail. More source material improves the reconstruction without making every inference symmetrical across governments.

Solomon's strongest continuing contribution is to direct attention toward purpose, authority, the presentation of prior undertakings and the political uses of access. These cases make those questions more specific. Which contact was refused? Which action was authorized? Which unresolved issue was reserved? Which message reached someone able to decide? The answers explain more than a general claim that Chinese negotiators value relationships or protect face. They also identify where such explanations remain plausible and what evidence would make them persuasive.
