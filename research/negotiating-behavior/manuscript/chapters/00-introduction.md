# Introduction: What an agreement buys

On Friday, November 12, 1999, an American diplomat in Beijing wrote to China's minister of foreign trade with a departure time. The delegation led by US Trade Representative Charlene Barshefsky would leave its hotel at eight the next morning. If Premier Zhu Rongji would meet her, the Americans could stay. They wanted an answer by seven.[^1]

William McCahill's letter did more than threaten to end the talks. It challenged the minister's understanding of the American position, invoked the two presidents' intentions and asked for a decision from the man whom China's president had made responsible for the negotiations. The Americans were willing to discuss a disputed ownership provision as part of a package that satisfactorily resolved their demands for protections against Chinese imports. Their departure was conditional too. They could delay until Saturday evening, or even early Sunday, but no further. Barshefsky wanted Zhu to help close a bargain his negotiators had not closed.

There was much to lose on either side. China wanted to join the World Trade Organization. The United States wanted terms on which American firms and farmers could sell to, invest in and compete with it. Earlier that year Zhu had publicly defended accession as a way to expose Chinese enterprises to competition and encourage reform. He rejected press descriptions of his concessions as a gift to the United States. Both governments had reasons to reach an agreement, and both could reject the immediate package.[^2]

For a company hoping to enter China, the package contained several different gates. A foreign investor might be admitted but denied a controlling share. It might obtain an ownership stake but face restrictions on where it could operate or whom it could serve. A concession could promise access after a specified interval rather than give it immediately. The negotiators had to decide how much each permission was worth and what to offer for it.

Life insurance shows why those distinctions mattered. The Chinese and American papers of November 12 both contemplated a fifty-percent foreign stake. An American paper attributed to the following day added a demand for managerial control. A limit on foreign ownership could remain while the distribution of power inside the venture changed. The Chinese proposal, meanwhile, made its offered concessions conditional on settling the package as a whole. A figure on a page was not yet an entitlement a firm could use.[^3]

Behind the Chinese delegation stood more than one source of permission. Financial negotiator Deng Hongguo later described foreign requests passing through the trade ministry to regulators who prepared alternatives and sought clearance from their leaders. The trade delegation then had to assemble a package from what the sectors could offer. His account identifies a problem the visitor could not solve through rapport alone: the official who understood a proposal might still lack permission to accept it.[^4]

The Americans' stated deadline passed. Zhu met the delegation on Saturday. On Sunday McCahill wrote again. The overnight talks had reached another impasse, he told the minister. The Americans accused the Chinese side of reopening issues and introducing new ones. They had intended to leave that morning; they now urgently requested another meeting with the premier.[^5]

The second letter is a useful corrective to the image of a final encounter in which a leader resolves everything. Political intervention had been followed by more bargaining and another dispute over what had been settled. Nor were the Americans simply enduring a Chinese timetable. They were changing their own offers, invoking their own deadline and using their access to seek another decision.

On Monday, November 15, Barshefsky and minister Shi Guangsheng signed the bilateral market-access agreement. Its body directed the governments to submit four annexes for incorporation into the multilateral accession negotiations. The bilateral bargain was a major achievement. China was still not a WTO member; other governments had demands to settle, and the American administration still had work to do in Congress.[^6]

What had the weekend accomplished? It had turned enough disputed terms into an acceptable package to let the larger process advance. The letters cannot tell us exactly how much the departure threat contributed, or which private argument changed an instruction. They do show the work hidden by an announcement of success: distinguishing ownership from control, trading an opening in one field against protection in another, and finding someone able to authorize the exchange.

For anyone negotiating with China, the problem is practical. A defended limit can coexist with a valuable concession. The task is to discover which dimensions can move, who can move them and what the Chinese side wants sufficiently to pay for an accommodation. Insisting that Beijing first abandon its stated principle may obscure a bargain available within it. Accepting reassuring language without identifying the permission it conveys can produce an agreement worth less than it appears.

This book follows that problem from the opening to China into the negotiating environment of 2026. Its argument begins with a feature of the Chinese state that repeatedly matters at the table: politically protected choices coexist with an extensive ability to make specific adjustments. Chinese officials can defend Party leadership, sovereignty or national control while bargaining over the ownership, timing, procedure or conduct through which those positions take effect. Foreign negotiators need to understand both the defended boundary and the room inside it. Since Richard H. Solomon studied the normalization era, the objects and instruments of that bargaining have multiplied. China's participation in international institutions and the demands placed on its domestic administration have changed what counterparts seek and what Chinese officials can offer. Relationships still matter, but their usefulness has to be traced to those choices.

## The inheritance

Solomon knew the difficulty of doing that work. He had served on the National Security Council during the opening to China, read official American records and interviewed more than thirty senior negotiators. His principal study covered 1967–1984. The volume often encountered today is a 2005 printing of the 1999 edition, which added an essay by Charles W. Freeman Jr. about later developments. Its publication date can conceal how far back the main investigation reaches.[^7]

The book's enduring quality is its attention to the predicament of the person across the table. A visitor wants access to someone who can decide. A relationship offers it, but may also create obligations. An earlier expression of goodwill can return as an alleged commitment. A Chinese host may ask an influential American to explain Beijing's position in Washington; the American may believe that preserving the relationship will improve the chances of a useful agreement. Solomon followed these encounters beyond ceremonial language to the pressure they placed on the participant's choices.

His advice was demanding. Know the record well enough to challenge a tendentious account of it. Decide what terms your own government can accept. Keep the strategic purpose in view when the conversation becomes personal. He gave interests and official discipline more explanatory weight than interpersonal tactics. He also warned that compressing their conduct into a characteristic style could make it seem more consistent and far-sighted than it had been.[^8]

An update must retain that discipline. The ability to cultivate a counterpart has not vanished. In July 2023, at the age of one hundred, Henry Kissinger met Xi Jinping and defense minister Li Shangfu in Beijing. Li's ministry had declined a proposed formal meeting with US defense secretary Lloyd Austin earlier that year. The retired American received conspicuous honors and requests to help the relationship. The visit showed that the old-friend channel could still deliver unusual access.[^9]

Access left a further question. Secretary of State Antony Blinken had already met Xi in June, and Kissinger was visiting in a private capacity. Blinken later said he had consulted him. The available record does not show a message from the July visit producing a particular Chinese instruction or the agreements announced in November. For an official deciding whether to use such an intermediary, the distinction matters. A visitor may carry information, explain an offer or recruit support. A reception alone cannot tell the official which of those functions occurred or what changed as a result.

Freeman had already made room for learning and institutional change. He connected officials' room to improvise with communications, budgets and access to instructions; he described specialization and adaptation to particular counterparts. Solomon had expressly bounded his own study to an era when Beijing wanted a constructive relationship with Washington. Neither author requires us to believe that later China would negotiate every issue in the same way. The useful inheritance is a set of questions about relationships, authority and pressure, together with historical claims that must be tested at their original strength.[^10]

## What changed at the table

China's entry into the WTO altered the settings in which a rejected request could be pursued. During the normalization negotiations, American officials sought a bilateral political accommodation. Accession later allowed Chinese officials to use WTO rules to challenge American decisions. In the dispute known as DS379, China contested duties on steel pipes, laminated sacks and tyres, winning important rulings on the treatment of public bodies and the double counting of certain subsidies. In May 2012, Chinese ambassador Yi Xiaozhun and American ambassador Michael Punke signed detailed procedures governing the next steps in their dispute.[^11]

The change was consequential. Chinese representatives were acquiring experience not only in accommodating foreign demands for access, but in contesting the foreign government's exercise of authority. They could use an external procedure to press a claim that Washington had rejected. Legal expertise supplied arguments and remedies; it did not eliminate bargaining over the sequence in which those remedies could be used. The institution created particular opportunities and constraints, not a general escape from politics.

Climate negotiations opened another way to accommodate a defended national choice. From Copenhagen in 2009 through Paris in 2015, the governments argued over what countries should do at home and how those actions should be examined abroad. China accepted an increasingly specified arrangement for reporting, consultation and review while retaining national determination of its mitigation contribution. Paris required parties to prepare, communicate and maintain contributions and pursue domestic mitigation measures. It did not impose an internationally binding obligation to achieve each announced result.[^12]

This was substantial cooperation, but its value depended on understanding the bargain. A government buying a promise of a particular emissions outcome had not obtained the same thing as a government buying participation in an enduring system of contributions and scrutiny. The distinction concerned discretion over national policy—the very object over which the parties were negotiating. It was not simply a defect to be discovered after signature.

The later American trade negotiations brought foreign demands further into the administration of China's economy. Intellectual-property enforcement, financial permissions, agricultural approvals and purchases each required different acts from different authorities and enterprises. The Phase One agreement signed in January 2020 specified obligations and a procedure for disputes while preserving domestic choice over some means of implementation. It also addressed a difficulty familiar to firms dependent on Chinese regulatory decisions: reporting a failure might put the complainant at risk. In congressional testimony, Robert Lighthizer anticipated that companies would need to complain anonymously. The final agreement expressly allowed an appeal to omit a company's identifying information and protected the confidentiality of the material.[^13]

That provision makes an otherwise abstract problem tangible. The enterprise whose experience shows whether market access works may still need the goodwill or permission of the authority it would report. A negotiator must therefore obtain not only a promise and a remedy but a feasible way to learn whether the promise is being kept. Confidentiality helps. It cannot ensure that a firm will be willing to complain or that its circumstances will not identify it.

The results of Phase One were uneven in ways that a general verdict on Chinese compliance would miss. China's securities regulator attributed an eight-month acceleration of an ownership opening to the agreement; an initially approved JPMorgan venture later obtained full foreign ownership. Agricultural approvals still involved protracted administrative work. Purchases fell far short of the examined benchmarks. A change in an ownership rule, a decision on an application and an aggregate purchase commitment were different undertakings, dependent on different actors. Negotiators who valued them as interchangeable promises would misunderstand both the concession and the means of securing it.[^14]

The political relationship changed as well. In 2023 the United States sought military communication while continuing policies China opposed. After the balloon confrontation, China's defense ministry refused an American request for a call and blamed American conduct for failing to provide the conditions for dialogue. In June, Blinken left Beijing without the agreement on military communication he had sought. The November presidential meeting produced commitments concerning named military mechanisms, followed by work to arrange their resumption. Access to a risk-management channel had itself become an object of negotiation.[^15]

The 2025 negotiations exposed another source of Chinese bargaining power: permission to supply factories abroad. China’s export controls covered specified rare-earth items, including some magnet materials. After talks in London, Beijing described an arrangement under which it would approve eligible export applications while Washington removed particular restrictions. Software companies subsequently reported relief from American controls, and magnet shipments recovered. China’s licensing authority remained. For a foreign government, obtaining urgently needed supplies could mean accepting continued dependence on future permissions. The bargain concerned access in both directions, and the value of relief depended on which transactions could proceed.[^18]

These episodes require an update in more than vocabulary. The counterpart seeking an investment concession, a legal remedy or a reliable military contact confronts different Chinese authorities, different political limits and different means of applying pressure. The normalization-era account cannot simply be extended by replacing Mao's name with Xi's. Nor does every familiar maneuver prove an unchanged cause. The history has to explain what an official was trying to obtain, what could be conceded and which alternative made a refusal bearable.

## The terms on which China will agree

American experience supplies the center of this book. It does not set the limits of Chinese behavior. European officials negotiating accession had their own commercial priorities and authorization from member governments to manage. India sought local military arrangements while defending its position that the wider relationship depended on the border. Russia and China settled territorial questions through agreements that distinguished sovereignty from residents' continued use, and developed gas cooperation in which an operating pipeline coexisted with unresolved terms for further projects. Friendship, rivalry and partnership created different settings; none of those labels settled the price or content of a concession.[^16]

Foreign choices belong inside the explanation. Barshefsky's delegation could invoke a departure time and then stay. European negotiators could seek authoritative confirmation of a Chinese constraint before asking their own governments to reconsider what they would accept. American leaders could impose tariffs and later accept a partial settlement with some of them retained. A Chinese negotiating position takes shape against those choices. Treating the foreign side as a passive audience would miss part of the process Solomon wanted his readers to master.

The chapters therefore ask what a negotiator should notice before interpreting an encounter as a breakthrough or a rebuff. Has a senior leader settled the policy question, or instructed the delegates to keep trying? Does the proposed concession change an ownership rule, an operating permission or only the date at which a request can be considered? Where can a refusal still enter the process? What does the other side gain by accepting a narrower bargain now? The answers can justify patience, a revised offer or a decision to leave. Familiarity with Chinese political language helps the negotiator ask them. It cannot answer them in advance.

The account rests on documents, published testimony, scholarship and reporting, including material from both governments where it is available. It does not reproduce Solomon's access to private deliberations and interviews. Where the evidence cannot identify who blocked a concession or why a mandate changed, the narrative leaves that uncertainty intact. But uncertainty about motive does not erase a documented offer, an operative condition or a failure to perform. The task is to make the strongest explanation the record permits, and then show what follows for the person who must decide without a complete record of their own.[^17]

The practical challenge is to reach a worthwhile bargain with a government that can hold a political line while changing useful terms beneath it. Sometimes that means obtaining a larger concession. Sometimes it means accepting a limited accommodation that leaves the deepest dispute unresolved. Both require knowing what China wants, what one's own side is prepared to exchange, and what the words on the page will allow anyone to do the next morning.
