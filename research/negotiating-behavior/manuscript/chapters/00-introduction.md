# Introduction: What an agreement buys

On Friday, November 12, 1999, an American diplomat in Beijing wrote to China's minister of foreign trade with a departure time. The delegation led by US Trade Representative Charlene Barshefsky would leave its hotel at eight the next morning. If Premier Zhu Rongji would meet her, the Americans could stay. They wanted an answer by seven.[^1]

William McCahill's letter did more than threaten to end the talks. It challenged the minister's understanding of the American position, invoked the two presidents' intentions and asked for a decision from the man whom China's president had made responsible for the negotiations. The Americans were willing to discuss a disputed ownership provision as part of a package that satisfactorily resolved their demands for protections against Chinese imports. Their departure was conditional too. They could delay until Saturday evening, or even early Sunday, but no further. Barshefsky wanted Zhu to help close a bargain his negotiators had not closed.

There was much to lose on either side. China wanted to join the World Trade Organization. The United States wanted terms on which American firms and farmers could sell to, invest in and compete with it. Earlier that year Zhu had publicly defended accession as a way to expose Chinese enterprises to competition and encourage reform. He rejected press descriptions of his concessions as a gift to the United States. Both governments had reasons to reach an agreement, and both could reject the immediate package.[^2]

For a company hoping to enter China, the package contained several different gates. A foreign investor might be admitted but denied a controlling share. It might obtain an ownership stake but face restrictions on where it could operate or whom it could serve. A concession could promise access after a specified interval rather than give it immediately. The negotiators had to decide how much each permission was worth and what to offer for it.

Life insurance shows why those distinctions mattered. The Chinese and American papers of November 12 both contemplated a fifty-percent foreign stake. An American paper attributed to the following day added a demand for managerial control. A limit on foreign ownership could remain while the distribution of power inside the venture changed. The Chinese proposal, meanwhile, made its offered concessions conditional on settling the package as a whole. A figure on a page was not yet an entitlement a firm could use.[^3]

Behind the Chinese delegation stood more than one source of permission. Financial negotiator Deng Hongguo later described foreign requests passing through the trade ministry to regulators who prepared alternatives and sought clearance from their leaders. The trade delegation then had to assemble a package from what the sectors could offer. His account identifies a problem the visitor could not solve through rapport alone: the official who understood a proposal might still lack permission to accept it.[^4]

Barshefsky was asking for more than another meeting. She needed a decision about which limits China would defend, which terms could change and what Washington would have to accept in return. McCahill's letter put those choices on a clock. Chapter 2 follows what happened when the clock ran out.

The predicament recurs throughout this book: an official can defend one limit while making a valuable concession on another. The visitor must decide whether to seek compensation within that limit, challenge it, or reject a package that leaves the needed benefit out of reach. The choice requires an estimate of what the other government wants and a clear account of what one's own side can accept. Neither comes from interpreting political language alone.

Since Richard H. Solomon studied the opening to China, WTO membership has given Chinese officials a forum in which to challenge a powerful counterpart's decisions, and China has developed the capacity to use it. Later agreements have changed the procedures for scrutiny and disputed performance. Under Xi Jinping, Beijing has reorganized central supervision while retaining distinct responsibilities for carrying out decisions. China's position in supplying foreign industries has also given its licensing decisions greater consequences abroad. These developments change what a counterpart can obtain, where it can contest a refusal and what it needs to secure before announcing success.

## The inheritance

Solomon knew the difficulty of doing that work. He had served on the National Security Council during the opening to China, read official American records and interviewed more than thirty senior negotiators. His principal study covered 1967–1984. The volume often encountered today is a 2005 printing of the 1999 edition, which added an essay by Charles W. Freeman Jr. about later developments. Its publication date can conceal how far back the main investigation reaches.[^7]

The book's enduring quality is its attention to the predicament of the person across the table. A visitor wants access to someone who can decide. A relationship offers it, but may also create obligations. An earlier expression of goodwill can return as an alleged commitment. A Chinese host may ask an influential American to explain Beijing's position in Washington; the American may believe that preserving the relationship will improve the chances of a useful agreement. Solomon followed these encounters beyond ceremonial language to the pressure they placed on the participant's choices.

His advice was demanding. Know the record well enough to challenge a tendentious account of it. Decide what terms your own government can accept. Keep the strategic purpose in view when the conversation becomes personal. He gave interests and official discipline more explanatory weight than interpersonal tactics. He also warned that compressing their conduct into a characteristic style could make it seem more consistent and far-sighted than it had been.[^8]

Cultivation still deserves Solomon's close attention. In July 2023, Henry Kissinger received exceptional access and requests for help in Beijing, although he held no government office. The visit revived a familiar form of diplomacy. Whether it helped produce a particular decision is a separate question, examined in the crisis chapter. The distinction between access and an instruction matters to anyone considering an intermediary.[^9]

Freeman had already made room for learning and institutional change. He connected officials' room to improvise with communications, budgets and access to instructions; he described specialization and adaptation to particular counterparts. Solomon had expressly bounded his own study to an era when Beijing wanted a constructive relationship with Washington. Neither author requires us to believe that later China would negotiate every issue in the same way.[^10]

The later cases sustain Solomon's central insight: political purposes organize the bargain. A defended principle can leave room for a specific accommodation; a relationship can help obtain an answer without conferring authority to make the commitment. His stronger forecasts warrant more restraint. A hard line does not establish the internal division behind it, and a senior encounter does not identify which terms have been accepted. Use access to test an exchange and obtain a definite answer. Protect against the decisions that remain outstanding.[^verdict]

## What changed at the table

WTO membership changed where a refused demand could lead. China could contest American trade measures through an external procedure and obtain findings Washington had opposed. It also had to learn to assemble the legal arguments, economic evidence and organizational support that made such claims possible. The dispute known as DS379 shows the reach and limits of that capacity: China won important rulings, then faced further administrative proceedings over their application. By 2026, even the availability of appeal depended on arrangements that differed by counterpart.[^11]

American demands about China's domestic administration had already become detailed in the 1990s. The 1995 intellectual-property bargain prescribed enforcement bodies, a deadline for accepting petitions or explaining refusal, information and consultations, with Washington retaining the prospect of renewed trade action. Phase One therefore belongs to a longer argument about making promises perform. Its distinctive contribution in this comparison was the bilateral procedure the governments accepted for complaints, escalation and disputed remedies.[^ip-history]

The 2025 export-control negotiations made another dependency visible. Foreign factories needed Chinese supplies; after London, Beijing described approvals for eligible applications in exchange for relief from particular American restrictions. Transactions resumed while Chinese control authority survived. A government could obtain immediate relief yet leave its industries dependent on future permissions. The concession's duration and coverage mattered alongside its announced size.[^18]

The organization behind the counterpart changed too. The 2018 reforms consolidated specific coordinating functions in a central foreign-affairs commission and its office. Published responsibilities became clearer, but a visitor still could not read a delegation's authority from its place in the hierarchy. Fieldwork in the early Xi years describes tighter supervision of informal diplomatic contact alongside continuing room for initiative. These changes affect how a proposal can be explored and carried upward; they do not establish that fewer concessions became possible.[^institution-history]

The cases demonstrate changes in institutions and instruments without measuring a national increase in patience, rigidity or deception, or establishing that cultivating old friends became more or less effective. Familiar conduct can serve a changed purpose. The explanation has to reach the decision an official was trying to obtain and the alternative that made refusal tolerable.

## The terms on which China will agree

American experience supplies the center of this book. It does not set the limits of Chinese behavior. European officials negotiating accession had their own commercial priorities and authorization from member governments to manage. India sought local military arrangements while defending its position that the wider relationship depended on the border. Russia and China settled territorial questions through agreements that distinguished sovereignty from residents' continued use, and developed gas cooperation in which an operating pipeline coexisted with unresolved terms for further projects. Friendship, rivalry and partnership created different settings; none of those labels settled the price or content of a concession.[^16]

Foreign choices belong inside the explanation. Barshefsky's delegation could invoke a departure time and then stay. European negotiators could seek authoritative confirmation of a Chinese constraint while consulting their own governments about what they would accept. American leaders could impose tariffs and later accept a partial settlement with some of them retained. A Chinese negotiating position takes shape against those choices. Treating the foreign side as a passive audience would miss part of the process Solomon wanted his readers to master.

The chapters therefore ask what a negotiator should notice before interpreting an encounter as a breakthrough or a rebuff. Has a senior leader settled the policy question, or instructed the delegates to keep trying? Does the proposed concession change an ownership rule, an operating permission or only the date at which a request can be considered? Where can a refusal still enter the process? What does the other side gain by accepting a narrower bargain now? The answers can justify patience, a revised offer or a decision to leave. Familiarity with Chinese political language helps the negotiator ask them. It cannot answer them in advance.

The account rests on documents, published testimony, scholarship and reporting, including material from both governments where it is available. It does not reproduce Solomon's access to private deliberations and interviews. Where the evidence cannot identify who blocked a concession or why a mandate changed, the narrative leaves that uncertainty intact. But uncertainty about motive does not erase a documented offer, an operative condition or a failure to perform. The task is to make the strongest explanation the record permits, and then show what follows for the person who must decide without a complete record of their own.[^17]

A worthwhile bargain may change a defended rule, secure a useful exception or accomplish a limited task while the larger dispute remains. It may also be unavailable at an acceptable price. The chapters that follow examine how negotiators confronted those choices, what they managed to obtain, and what the agreement allowed anyone to do the next morning.
