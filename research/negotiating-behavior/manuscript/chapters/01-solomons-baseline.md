# 1. The price of a new relationship

Leonard Woodcock arrived a few minutes late for his nine o’clock evening meeting with Deng Xiaoping on December 14, 1978. Changes to his instructions had arrived at the last minute. The chief of the American liaison office in Beijing was trying to finish an agreement that would transform relations between the two countries. The words that might close it still depended on decisions in Washington.[^12]

The most difficult words concerned Taiwan. Beijing wanted Washington to end diplomatic relations with Taipei, remove its military presence and terminate the defense treaty. The Americans were prepared to take those steps while maintaining unofficial relations with Taiwan. They also intended to continue selling it arms. China opposed those sales. The question was whether the governments could establish diplomatic relations while each continued to defend a position the other rejected.

Deng had already shown Woodcock that the answer might be yes. The previous day, he had accepted important parts of the American proposal and agreed to visit Washington. But he had also asked for changes in what the Americans would do and what they would say. His intervention made a bargain possible to pursue; it did not make the remaining disagreement disappear.

## What could be left unresolved

The prospect of meeting Deng had itself been encouraging. At the end of their December 4 session, Vice Foreign Minister Han Nien-lung had told Woodcock that Deng wanted to see him. Han had presented a draft communiqué in both languages and a firm account of China’s requirements. He refused a pledge to settle the Taiwan question peacefully, describing it as a matter of Chinese sovereignty. Yet he offered a way to accommodate Washington: China could refrain from objecting to an American expression of hope for a peaceful solution, while issuing its own statement that reunification was an internal affair. A common announcement could coexist with separate positions.[^7]

Washington was working to make the larger relationship attractive. On December 11, national security adviser Zbigniew Brzezinski urged Chinese liaison-office chief Chai Zemin to meet the January 1 target. A visit by a senior Chinese leader could give the new relationship political momentum in the United States. Brzezinski also mentioned the possibility of a Soviet summit in Washington, while denying that the invitations were linked. Timing and personal access were resources the Americans could offer too.[^8]

Deng opened his December 13 meeting by acknowledging Brzezinski’s exchange with Chai. Woodcock then handed him the American draft. As the interpreter read it, Deng asked her to repeat particular phrases. His questions concerned what Washington’s promises would mean in practice. If diplomatic relations with Taiwan were ending, why would withdrawing the military presence take a year? Woodcock explained that it would not: troops and installations would be removed within four months. The one-year period applied to other arrangements and, separately, to the notice required to terminate the defense treaty.[^9][^10]

Deng accepted the withdrawal timetable after checking that it was separate from the American position on arms sales. He was less satisfied with the treaty’s remaining life. Public reference to its termination article could make the agreement look as though Washington had ended the treaty in name while retaining it in fact. Could the Americans avoid citing the article and handle the legal process internally?

Woodcock explained that following the treaty’s own terms mattered to the president’s authority to terminate it. Other approaches could involve other branches of government. Deng asked him to consult the lawyers. He then proposed an accommodation requiring a concrete American commitment: no new arms sales to Taiwan during the year in which the treaty remained legally in force. His acceptance of Washington’s termination language depended on that restraint and on avoiding an explicit reference to the article in the statement.[^10]

These were choices for Washington. In the margins of Woodcock’s report, Carter indicated that the administration should try to accommodate the one-year pause. He accepted the possibility of omitting the article from the communiqué and perhaps the exchanged statements, but insisted that public explanations could not conceal the legal position. On Deng’s request for language opposing hegemony, Carter was wary of wording that would unnecessarily antagonize the Soviet Union. Woodcock urged a formulation based on the already accepted Shanghai Communiqué. The American envoy was negotiating with instructions from a president who was making several judgments of his own.[^11]

Woodcock also warned against treating Deng’s willingness to proceed as consent to future sales. The one-year pause did not settle what would happen afterward. His assessment was that Deng wanted movement and had political reasons to limit the immediate controversy. That was an envoy’s interpretation of a counterpart whose internal deliberations he could not fully see. It was reason to keep negotiating, not permission to tell Washington that Beijing had accepted the American arms policy.

By the evening of December 14, much of the bargain was in place. Woodcock delivered the revised American presentation and read the proposed texts. Deng accepted the communiqué with a few changes. One came from Vice Foreign Minister Zhang Wenjin: the governments should reaffirm the principles they had *agreed on* in the Shanghai Communiqué, rather than everything *expressed* there. The earlier document contained separate positions as well as common ones. Woodcock checked its language and accepted the distinction.[^12]

A small change protected a large difference. Neither government wanted agreement on a common document to make every statement in it its own. The distinction would matter whenever either invoked the past agreement to demand more from the other.

Arms sales still tested it. Deng asked Woodcock to omit the reference to 1979 from what he understood would be a public answer about the pause: naming one year implied what might happen in the next. Woodcock explained that the assurance about 1979 was an answer to Deng’s request, not the proposed response to a press question. Deliveries already committed or under way could continue; the pause concerned new sales. Deng accepted the explanation. Woodcock reported that the American position was clearly on the record.

On December 15, the final difficulty lay as much in announcing the bargain as in writing it. Woodcock expected Chinese objections to future sales and thought their impact would depend on the wider relationship. He warned that an immediate presidential statement could embarrass Deng. Washington replied that silence was impossible. Congress would ask, reporters would ask, and the administration needed to reassure Taiwan. It wanted Chinese restraint when it explained its intentions, not explicit Chinese approval.[^13]

Woodcock met Deng again that afternoon. When he confirmed the intention to sell arms after 1979, Deng emphatically rejected it. Continued supplies, he argued, would preserve the substance of the defense relationship and obstruct peaceful reunification. Woodcock answered that American political conditions made denying arms to Taiwan untenable. He also promised that Washington would not misrepresent China’s position or imply its consent.

The discussion turned to whether the announcement should wait. Deng urged Carter to avoid direct answers that would force a Chinese response and diminish the achievement. Woodcock said the purpose of raising the issue was to prevent surprises. Deng agreed to proceed, while maintaining that China would answer an American public assertion of future sales. The envoy recommended going ahead. An agreement was available; agreement on arms sales was not.[^1]

Brzezinski’s final instruction preserved that difference. The United States would reaffirm its position when questioned and acknowledge that China did not endorse it. Woodcock conveyed the proposed response through Foreign Ministry channels. The joint communiqué established diplomatic relations from January 1, 1979, with embassies and ambassadors scheduled for March 1. The two governments had arranged recognition and the transformation of Washington’s official relationship with Taiwan without settling the dispute over future arms supplies.[^1][^14]

For Woodcock, the task had been to carry an available agreement through a disagreement serious enough to endanger it. For Deng, proceeding meant accepting that Washington would retain and publicly defend a policy he opposed. The value placed on the new relationship made that limited result worth considering. Nothing in the arrangement required either government to stop arguing its case afterward.

## The relationship Solomon studied

Richard Solomon made this world intelligible to people who would have to negotiate in it. His principal study covered 1967–1984, the years in which a previously hostile relationship was being opened and developed. He had served on the National Security Council staff, examined official American records and interviewed more than thirty senior American negotiators. He supplemented those interviews with other officials and businessmen. He could connect an exchange at the table with participants’ accounts of what they were trying to accomplish.[^2][^3]

His central subject was the political use of a relationship. Chinese officials assessed their counterparts over repeated encounters, sought common principles, cultivated selected interlocutors and kept close track of what had been said. A visitor’s earlier expression of sympathy could return as a claim on future conduct. Recognition as a trusted friend could create an obligation to explain China’s concerns inside the visitor’s own government or to help rescue a threatened negotiation.[^5]

The attraction and the pressure belonged together. Access might help an American understand a proposal, persuade a Chinese leader or get an answer that ordinary channels could not produce. Protecting that access could also make him more receptive to a Chinese request. Solomon’s practical warning was to keep the government’s purpose in view when the relationship became personal.

He did not imagine that Beijing had created all the American differences it could exploit. Agencies and advisers already disagreed. Selecting one channel could give influence to a sympathetic official without changing anyone’s convictions. Nor had personal diplomacy abolished Chinese institutions. Solomon described official discipline, vulnerability to political criticism and the increasing involvement of ministries and technical specialists. The person who understood a request might lack authority to grant it.[^6]

The normalization sequence gives these observations concrete force. Woodcock found room to move because Deng could make decisions, yet the American still had to obtain instructions. The parties altered words and sequences while reserving fundamental positions. Solomon expressly described partial agreements as a way for China to develop a relationship from which it could continue bargaining over unresolved issues. That possibility is part of his explanation, not a discovery that a later update can claim for itself.

His scope matters just as much. He asked whether insights developed while Beijing wanted a constructive relationship with Washington would hold if confrontation returned. He left the answer open. The attraction of a relationship was a condition of the historical experience, not a permanent Chinese disposition to agree. An official dealing with a counterpart who values avoiding a concession more than improving relations faces a different calculation.[^4]

Even during the opening, however, high-level engagement did not always produce the settlement the Americans expected.

## The money Zhou could offer

In November 1973, Henry Kissinger and Premier Zhou Enlai discussed a sum small enough for both men to disparage and large enough to obstruct an agreement. American private claimants sought compensation for property taken in China. Washington had blocked Chinese assets that might provide it. But some Chinese deposits in third-country banks had already been returned to Beijing. A settlement that excluded those funds would leave much less for the Americans.

Nixon had authorized an approach the previous year: seek a Chinese lump-sum payment, possibly in installments, in return for unblocking assets; if that proved unacceptable, propose retaining blocked funds to compensate the claimants. In February 1973, Zhou and Kissinger had favored treating the problem politically. The later American review described an agreement in principle to assign claims to each other. The details had proved harder.[^15]

On November 13, Zhou challenged the terminology inherited from the period of hostility. Accepting Washington’s designation of Chinese nationals, he argued, would imply accepting the American position that had justified blocking the funds. Kissinger treated it as something that should be settled politically, but admitted that he had learned of this particular problem only two days earlier. He would have to consult his lawyers. Zhou’s objection was not simply a dislike of precision. The precise category carried a judgment about the legality of earlier American conduct.[^16]

Old Chinese bonds posed another limit. Kissinger could offer that the American government would not support claims on debts incurred by previous governments. Diplomat Arthur Hummel explained that the executive could not prevent private citizens from approaching the courts. Zhou wanted to know how the People’s Republic could be made responsible for money lent to governments it had displaced. Personal agreement between the two senior men could not supply every protection Beijing wanted.

Zhou then offered a way around the missing money. China could return $17 million, either to the Americans or through the banks. He preferred the first route. The banks had already given China its money back; he did not want to reverse their action. Kissinger welcomed the possibility of putting the money into the compensation pool without making China repay it through those institutions. In his estimate, it could help bring compensation to roughly forty cents on the dollar, a level he believed Congress would accept; twenty cents would not suffice.[^17]

The proposed payment therefore served different purposes. Kissinger needed a settlement defensible to American claimants and legislators. Zhou wanted a route that did not undo China’s position on the blocked accounts. Both men could see an accommodation, but Kissinger still promised legal consultation and a further proposal. His report to Nixon six days later described continued work toward resolution. Zhou had offered a consequential sum. They had not completed the settlement.

Solomon was already trying to explain why progress at that level was not producing secure ties below it. In a December 31 memorandum to Kissinger, he warned that Chinese political and bureaucratic resistance might prevent the relationship from surviving Mao Zedong and Zhou Enlai. The claims talks were one of several examples. He criticized the manner in which officials had handled them, while acknowledging that the Chinese had some grounds for suspicion about the settlement’s mechanics.[^19]

His diagnosis led to proposals. Kissinger should send Zhou a personal message about the condition of the relationship. A forthcoming Chinese trade delegation could be led by an important political official. A later American visit might include provincial cities and contact with a wider group of Chinese leaders. Solomon wanted to broaden the group of people invested in the opening, rather than rely entirely on the leaders who had begun it. Kissinger approved preparing the message and exploring a senior trade visitor. He held back on the broader itinerary and required his clearance before a State Department démarche.

That memorandum shows the future author at work as an adviser: interpreting obstruction and recommending a response. Its reporting was partly redacted and its attachments were not printed in the later public record. In his later book, Solomon attributed the aborted arrangement to bureaucratic resistance and political pressure associated with the rise of the Gang of Four. The memorandum and book cannot independently corroborate each other. They do show that his account was more than a description of frustrating manners. He was asking how a relationship dependent on a few powerful sponsors could acquire wider political support.

Control of the American channel presented difficulties too. In February 1974, Hummel privately described delayed answers, withheld instructions and the difficulty of getting Kissinger’s attention while other crises competed for it. An initial authorization to discuss the liaison office’s space needs had been retracted pending his return from the Middle East. These were not records of what caused the claims failure. They show why a negotiator could encounter delay on either side of a tightly controlled relationship.[^20]

China withdrew Zhou’s monetary offer in 1974, according to the later Vance and Brzezinski memoranda. It had not been reinstated by the 1977 talks. In October 1975, Deng had made the continuing legal objection explicit to Kissinger: no settlement could require Chinese observance of American law. He professed little concern about how long the issue remained unsettled, while leaving open the possibility of a rapid agreement if the terms could be solved.[^18]

For Washington, waiting also became a choice. In May 1977, Secretary of State Cyrus Vance explained that definitions and the missing third-country funds still determined how much American claimants could receive. Without movement, he expected congressional trouble. Yet making diplomatic normalization depend on solving the claims would allow a secondary issue to obstruct the larger relationship. He recommended deferring further approaches until the administration had decided its normalization strategy. Carter approved.[^21]

The failure was real, and its object matters. An offer to contribute to a proposed settlement had been withdrawn; an agreement in principle had not become a completed instrument. Those events could damage confidence without being a refusal to perform a finished agreement. A negotiator needed to understand both the loss of the offer and why the remaining terms still mattered. The title and rank of the person who made it did not remove those questions.

## What a leader’s arrival tells the negotiator

Solomon included the aborted claims arrangement in his book. On the following page, discussing an endgame characterized by concrete drafting, he called senior intervention “the most reliable sign” that agreement was at hand. A political leader, he reasoned, would not risk his face in a negotiation about to collapse. His description was stronger than a claim that senior contact sometimes helped.[^22]

Normalization shows the attraction of that forecast. Han’s announcement of Deng’s interest preceded substantive decisions, detailed drafting and agreement. The claims talks show the difficulty in using it. Zhou could offer money while payment mechanics, definitions and the American ability to deliver remained unsettled. Solomon’s own evidence creates a tension with his confident account of the leader’s risk. It does not, from two cases, establish how reliably the signal predicts agreement compared with other signals.

The practical response is to ask what the intervention can accomplish. Does the visitor need a new instruction, acceptance of a particular formula or confirmation that a limit will hold? Which unresolved questions remain outside that leader’s power to settle alone? Woodcock still needed Washington’s answers. Kissinger still needed legal advice and a package he believed Congress would accept. Chinese seniority could not remove American conditions.

Solomon’s related judgment that Chinese rigidity usually indicates leadership conflict poses a similar problem. The cases here cannot establish that frequency. A firm refusal may arise from a divided leadership; it may also express a settled preference or a legal objection the other side has not answered. The responses differ. Seeking a more powerful sponsor may help in one situation, while changing the substance or form of the offer may matter more in another.[^23]

The same care applies to principles. Deng bargained over how Washington would describe treaty termination and future arms sales. Zhou objected to legal categories and routes of payment. The useful question was what accepting a formulation would concede. Neither a general preference for flexibility nor an assumption that principles are immovable can answer it in advance.

These are ways of using Solomon’s observations, not grounds for quietly weakening his original predictions until every outcome fits. He distinguished characteristic Chinese practices from uniquely Chinese ones. A later history must preserve that distinction while explaining how the people, institutions and purposes in a particular negotiation gave a familiar practice its force.

## Freeman’s next question

Freeman’s essay in the 1999 edition had already begun that work. Negotiating at home might reflect budgets, communications and proximity to the officials who could authorize a concession. Inviting the foreign side to speak first could give a Chinese official a proposal to report and a basis for seeking new instructions. The visible conduct might continue while the reason for it changed.[^24]

He described increased foreign contacts, research capacity and experience in international institutions, alongside the growing involvement of specialized ministries. He also put American domestic politics inside the account: the executive negotiating abroad might simultaneously be negotiating with Congress. A bilateral table could sit inside a larger process of assembling authority and support.[^25]

Specialists, Freeman suggested, could develop different approaches to different counterparts. He nevertheless regarded the basic approach as persistent and endorsed Solomon’s confidence that its recurring patterns could help foreigners anticipate Chinese conduct. Adaptation and continuity belonged in the same account. That made the next question more demanding than whether China had become more modern or more rigid. Which officials had learned what, and what could they do with it in the negotiation at hand? Which apparent change reflected a new instrument or new expertise, and which reflected a different interest in the result?[^26]

The next chapters follow those questions into a relationship with more institutions, more commercial interests and more means of pressure. They begin with an inheritance that is already complicated. The Chinese government could defend a political position and accept an agreement that left it unresolved. A foreign official could benefit from access while confronting limits in both governments. The relationship mattered because it made some bargains worth the concessions and remaining risks. Knowing China did not relieve the negotiator of deciding what those bargains were worth.
