# 1. What Solomon actually explained

On December 15, 1978, Leonard Woodcock reported that Deng Xiaoping remained opposed to American arms sales to Taiwan. Deng nevertheless agreed to proceed with the schedule for establishing diplomatic relations. Washington then instructed Woodcock to make clear that the United States would reaffirm its own position when asked, while acknowledging that China did not endorse it. The two governments were preparing to announce an agreement whose most consequential unfinished business was known to both.[^1]

Partial settlements, issue separation and agreement amid disagreement were already features of the period Richard Solomon studied in *Chinese Negotiating Behavior*. They were also part of his account. Authority mattered on both sides: Deng could authorize movement, but Woodcock still needed instructions from Washington. The historical question is how the forms and uses of agreement changed, and how far Solomon’s explanations can travel beyond the relationship that supplied his evidence.

## The book’s two historical baselines

The date on the supplied printing is 2005; the edition is copyrighted 1999. Neither date describes the principal empirical period. Solomon’s study was commissioned in 1983 and completed in the mid-1980s. An unclassified RAND briefing followed in 1985, and declassified portions appeared in 1995. The core study chiefly examines the American–Chinese negotiating experience of 1967–1984. The later edition adds framing and an essay by Chas. W. Freeman Jr. assessing developments since that period.[^2]

Solomon and Freeman supply related but distinct baselines: an intensive account of the establishment of a relationship, followed by a participant’s interpretation of its subsequent institutional development. Practices Freeman described in the late 1990s are part of that inheritance, not discoveries of a 2026 update. The edition date does not establish that Solomon’s central propositions had been tested against the intervening negotiations.

Solomon’s evidence went well beyond public outcomes. He used official memoranda of conversation and reporting cables, interviewed more than thirty senior American negotiators, and supplemented those interviews with officials outside the State Department and businessmen. His own service on the National Security Council staff and subsequent access helped him interpret the record. The acknowledgments show a deliberate effort to assemble documentation from successive administrations. Personal experience and institutional memory were research assets.[^3]

They also shaped what the study could see. Its perspective was principally American. A memorandum recorded an encounter, mediated by interpretation and note-taking; it did not necessarily reveal the Chinese official’s instructions, the debates behind them or why a concession became possible. Public Chinese statements do not supply equivalent access to those internal decisions.

The study’s scope is narrower than a theory of how China always negotiates. Solomon expressly asks whether insights developed while Beijing wanted a constructive American relationship would apply in a future return to confrontation. He leaves the answer open. He also contrasts the experience of earlier American interlocutors with that of the normalization years, sometimes involving the same Chinese officials. Strategic purpose already helps explain variation inside his account.[^4]

Confrontation therefore poses a new test, rather than an automatic refutation: which mechanisms still operate when the relationship itself is less attractive and avoiding agreement is a more credible choice?

## What a relationship could accomplish

Solomon organized his account around the construction and use of a political relationship. Chinese officials sought shared principles, assessed the counterpart over extended exchanges, cultivated selected interlocutors, preserved earlier statements and continued bargaining during implementation. Personal access could create obligations. An official who valued recognition as a trusted friend might be pressed to demonstrate that friendship through policy, explain China’s concerns inside his own government or rescue a relationship threatened by an impasse.[^5]

That account contains several mechanisms which should not be collapsed into one. A familiar interlocutor may convey information credibly. He may possess authority to obtain a decision. He may serve as an advocate because he agrees with the policy. Or he may become vulnerable to pressure because losing access would damage his status or ambitions. The same meeting can be consistent with all four explanations. To establish interpersonal leverage, a history needs the demand, the interlocutor’s response and some evidence of what changed inside his government. Familiarity alone is not that evidence.

Solomon recognized an important part of this problem. Beijing did not create every rivalry it could exploit among American officials. Differences between agencies and senior advisers existed before Chinese officials selected particular channels. A successful appeal to one official might reveal an existing alignment rather than transform the balance of policy. Equally, a personal channel might overcome an information problem that formal communication had failed to resolve. Labeling every such contact manipulation would erase the reciprocal work required to establish a previously hostile relationship.[^6]

The book’s treatment of discipline and authority also complicates a purely interpersonal account. An official could be constrained by instructions, vulnerable to political criticism or unable to reconcile the interests of different organizations. The senior leader’s role partly reflected that structure. Access mattered because someone could make a decision, not simply because someone could confer warmth. Solomon already describes the expansion of ministries and technical processes after the initial opening. The baseline is therefore not a China in which personal diplomacy had eliminated institutions.

The December 1978 records let us see these distinctions in a successful sequence. The earlier claims-and-assets talks show why they matter when senior intervention fails to finish a bargain.

## Normalization with a disagreement preserved

Woodcock’s December 4 report describes Han Nien-lung presenting Chinese conditions and a bilingual draft. Han opposed continued arms sales and refused a Chinese pledge to resolve the Taiwan question peacefully. He allowed the United States to express its own hope for a peaceful resolution. The parties were distinguishing their common text from the positions each might announce separately. At the close, Han informed Woodcock that Deng wished to meet him.[^7]

The announcement of senior involvement fits Solomon’s account of an approaching endgame. Its meaning becomes clearer when placed beside what the Americans were doing. In Washington on December 11, Zbigniew Brzezinski encouraged Ambassador Chai Zemin to meet the January 1 target and discussed the political value of a senior Chinese visit. A possible Soviet summit entered the conversation too. Sequence, access and the wider strategic relationship were instruments used by the Americans as well as concerns of the Chinese.[^8]

Deng opened the December 13 meeting by acknowledging the December 11 exchange. He knew of the appeal; whether it prompted the meeting or his readiness to compromise is less clear. Michel Oksenberg’s contemporary covering note offered an inference about timing, while his 1980 retrospective made a stronger claim about the message’s effect.[^9]

Deng’s discussion with Woodcock separated several clocks and commitments. He accepted the American communiqué draft as a basis, while proposing anti-hegemony language either in the common text or in separate statements. The period for withdrawing American forces was distinct from the one-year notice associated with the defense treaty. Acceptance of the treaty-termination wording was tied to a pause in new arms sales during that year and to how the termination would be described. Detailed work would pass to vice ministers. Woodcock would obtain further instructions.[^10]

This was substantive senior participation. It did not eliminate technical bargaining or American limits. Carter’s recorded annotations favored accommodating the one-year pause, resisted a broader anti-hegemony formulation and recognized that public explanations could not hide every legal detail. In his own assessment, Woodcock warned against treating Chinese willingness to proceed as permission for arms sales after 1979. He was recommending a way forward while identifying the boundary of what had been accepted.[^11]

On December 14 the wording of the communiqué remained consequential. The reported changes distinguished principles agreed by both sides in Shanghai from all the positions expressed in the Shanghai Communiqué. A common declaration containing unilateral positions could not simply be transformed into a list of mutually accepted obligations. This was not a dispute over ceremonial language alone. The distinction limited what one party could subsequently attribute to the other.[^12]

The last exchanges made the arms-sales disagreement explicit. Woodcock expected later Chinese objections and believed their effect would depend on the broader relationship. Washington thought public silence impracticable because Congress, the press and Taiwan required explanations. Deng then agreed to proceed with normalization while maintaining his objection. Brzezinski’s final instruction required the Americans to acknowledge China’s non-endorsement when asserting their own position.[^13]

The resulting communiqué established diplomatic relations effective January 1, 1979, with embassies and ambassadors separately scheduled for March 1. It stated common principles and the agreed description of future unofficial American relations with Taiwan. It did not convert the documented dispute over arms sales into shared consent.[^14]

Recognition, diplomatic representation, withdrawal, treaty termination and public explanations could be arranged while an important dispute survived. This was the value of a partial settlement. The arms-sales question had been reserved; its persistence did not itself represent departure from the shared commitment. Solomon’s attention to reserved positions remains essential to understanding what normalization accomplished.

## When Zhou’s intervention was insufficient

The private-claims/blocked-assets dispute is particularly valuable because Solomon included it himself. American property claims against China and Chinese assets blocked by Washington created a potential exchange, but the assets were not a single uncontested pool. Some deposits in third-country banks had already been returned to China. Definitions affected which funds could be transferred, and claims against earlier Chinese governments raised separate questions. The amount mattered to American claimants; the legal form mattered to both governments.

The American position also required authorization. Nixon’s August 1972 instruction preferred a Chinese lump-sum payment, possibly in installments, in return for unblocking assets. If that proved non-negotiable, Washington should propose retaining blocked assets to compensate American claimants. State and Treasury were to prepare a negotiating scenario for presidential approval. By February 1973, Zhou and Kissinger were advocating a broad political treatment; the later American review describes an agreement in principle on mutual assignment of claims.[^15]

In November the senior discussion exposed what that understanding had left unresolved. Kissinger told Zhou that excluding the third-country money would reduce the prospective compensation below what he believed Congress would accept. Zhou regarded the banks’ repayment of the money to China as lawful. He also objected to definitions inherited from the period of hostility. Kissinger acknowledged that he could not settle one of those wording questions without consulting legal advisers. Hummel explained that the executive could decline to support old bond claims but could not simply prevent private parties from approaching the courts.[^16]

Zhou then proposed restoring $17 million, favoring payment directly to the American government. Routing it back through the banks risked implying recognition of the blocking measures whose legality China disputed. Kissinger was receptive and emphasized the value of the sum for the prospective compensation. But he promised further legal consultation and another proposal. His report to Nixon six days later described continuing exchanges toward final resolution, not a concluded settlement.[^17]

Zhou’s $17 million proposal left the rest of the package open. Definitions, payment route, presentation and domestic acceptance still determined whether the proposed funds could become usable compensation.

The later Vance and Brzezinski memoranda report a reversal: Zhou’s monetary offer was withdrawn in 1974 and had not been reinstated by the 1977 discussions. An October 1975 meeting records the continuing disagreement, including Deng’s objection to any settlement saying that Chinese were required to observe American law.[^18]

The distinction does not erase the reversal. It identifies its object. Retraction of a monetary offer, abandonment of an agreement in principle and failure to perform a completed instrument are not interchangeable events. The first two may damage confidence seriously without being the third. An account concerned with implementation must establish which commitment existed before measuring departure from it.

Solomon’s explanation linked the failure to bureaucratic resistance and internal political pressure. A contemporary memorandum helps recover the origin of that interpretation. On December 31, 1973, he warned Kissinger that political and bureaucratic forces threatened the durability of the relationship. The claims talks were among his examples. He drew on reporting that is partly redacted in the published record and on attachments not printed there. He also acknowledged grounds for Chinese suspicion of the settlement’s mechanics.[^19]

The memorandum shows Solomon developing his explanation while participating in policy. It and the later book belong to one source chain, not two independent confirmations of Chinese internal resistance. The Chinese decision that withdrew the offer remains missing.

American officials faced constraints too. In February 1974, Hummel described Kissinger’s tight control of the relationship, competing demands on his attention, delayed answers and instructions held back or withdrawn. A Chinese counterpart waiting for an answer could also be observing constrained access to a senior decision maker.[^20]

By May 1977, Vance recommended deferring further approaches until Washington had decided its normalization strategy. He explained why the technical differences materially affected the funds available for claimants and why he expected congressional difficulty. He also warned against making normalization itself hostage to the settlement. Carter approved deferral. The record thus contains both a political interpretation of why movement might become possible and a specific account of why details still mattered.[^21]

## Preserve the observation; test the prediction

The comparison exposes a tension inside Solomon’s book. At page 148 he records the aborted claims arrangement. On the following page, in a discussion that also emphasizes concrete drafting, he calls senior intervention “the most reliable sign” that agreement is at hand, explaining that a political leader would not risk his face in a negotiation about to collapse. His description of the endgame is confident, even though his own evidence includes failure.[^22]

These two cases cannot rank senior intervention against other signals or estimate its predictive reliability. They show why an observer must identify what the leader does: grant authority, confirm a limit, resolve a term or endorse prior work. A leader can move one issue without making the whole package acceptable to the other government. A practical forecast also depends on information available before the outcome; much of this normalization sequence was released later. The proposed face mechanism remains a separate question: did concern for personal standing change a decision, or is that effect merely being inferred from the leader’s presence?

Using a signal cautiously is different from disproving Solomon’s prediction. Replacing his strong wording with the modest claim that senior involvement sometimes matters would change the question. His diagnosis that Chinese rigidity usually indicates leadership conflict raises a parallel problem. The evidence here does not establish that frequency. A firm position may reflect a settled preference, a legal objection, a bargaining tactic or lack of instructions; each would call for a different response.[^23]

His treatment of principles benefits from a similar separation. Principles can organize a relationship and constrain later claims. They can also be negotiated in their wording and placement, as the normalization sequence demonstrates. In the claims case, the payment route carried a question of legal recognition. An abstract preference for flexible political language is not enough to explain either outcome. The historian must ask which interpretation a formulation admits, which it forecloses and whether both sides accepted it.

Solomon himself distinguished distinctiveness from uniqueness. The question is whether the frequency, combination or institutional function of a practice differed in a way that explains an offer or a result. Showing that both sides invoked principle does not answer it.

## Freeman had already begun the update

Freeman’s later essay retained the importance of relationships but changed parts of the causal account. A preference for negotiating at home could reflect budgets, communications and proximity to the people empowered to issue instructions. Letting a foreign counterpart speak first could give an official a safer basis for reporting a proposal and seeking revised authority. These mechanisms could change even if the visible practice continued.[^24]

His account of learning was equally consequential. Research institutes, foreign contacts and international exposure had increased. Participation in multilateral institutions could move from procedural study toward more active engagement. Ministries with specialized portfolios had become more involved. An American executive negotiating with China might itself be mediating demands from Congress, making the formal bilateral table only one part of the bargaining process.[^25]

Freeman also proposed that specialists developed different approaches for different counterparts. That observation challenges a single national style without eliminating continuity. A negotiator’s accumulated experience, the organization represented and the audience addressed could affect how a shared repertoire was used. It suggests a better comparison than placing every encounter in a single before-and-after sequence: examine related issues across counterparts, trace the people and authority involved, and ask whether an approach travels with the specialist or changes with the situation.[^26]

Freeman’s account gives the later chapters a specific task: establish how specialization and institutional participation affected particular decisions, and distinguish those effects from changes in power and strategic purpose.

The baseline that emerges is demanding. Personal access could help, but authority remained consequential. Political agreement could coexist with disagreement, but the disputed terms had to be identified. Senior intervention could move a proposal, but completion and performance were separate achievements. The next chapter follows these problems into accession negotiations, where the relationship had to be expressed through schedules, exceptions and procedures that many officials and governments would subsequently use.
