# 6. After the signature

Nearly six years after the signing of Phase One, Josh Gackle faced an awkward choice. The North Dakota farmer, representing the American Soybean Association at a December 2025 hearing, acknowledged China's failures to fulfill the agreement. But he feared what another American investigation might do to the business his members still hoped to conduct. No single alternative market could replace China, he told the hearing. New purchase promises were already uncertain. He wanted the administration to pursue compliance through the negotiations under way.[^34]

The next witness wanted a stronger response. Geoff Cooper, president of the Renewable Fuels Association, told the officials that American ethanol producers had invested in expectation of Chinese access and then lost it. His association supported the investigation and action to protect producers. Both men spoke for intended beneficiaries of the same agreement. One worried about losing prospective sales; the other described a market already closed to his members.

Their disagreement exposed a difficulty that no enforcement clause could settle in advance. A government must decide whether the expected benefit of enforcing an old promise is worth the risk to the business it can still obtain. Its bargaining position depends partly on whether the people it represents will sustain that choice. China had obligations, but Washington still had decisions to make.

Those decisions required a more discriminating account of performance than either government's verdict on the agreement as a whole. Within the same political relationship, an American securities firm had obtained full ownership and begun earning substantial revenue; seed companies had secured particular approvals while continuing to contest the review process; aggregate purchases had fallen far short. Each result came through a different route. To understand what the bargain had bought, it is necessary to follow those routes past the signing ceremony.

## Getting into business

JPMorgan's new securities company opened in China on March 20, 2020. With the coronavirus epidemic disrupting ordinary gatherings, it marked the occasion virtually. Three days later, China chief executive Mark Leung explained to a *Yicai* reporter what he wanted to do with the opening. The firm could bring research and financial services to Chinese clients, but its Chinese operations remained divided among separate legal entities. He wanted regulators to permit a more integrated operation. Entry had made the next commercial ambition more immediate.[^1]

The government negotiation and the firm's preparations had proceeded alongside each other. In November 2017, after President Trump's visit, Vice Finance Minister Zhu Guangyao announced that foreigners would be allowed to own 51 percent of securities, fund-management and futures companies, with the ceiling to disappear three years after implementation. Chinese officials placed this opening within the summit's economic results while also advancing requests about American investment treatment and export restrictions. Investors from other countries would benefit too.[^2]

The securities regulator issued implementing rules in April 2018. JPMorgan then pursued a majority-owned company. The China Securities Regulatory Commission approved its establishment in March 2019, with RMB 800 million in registered capital and five Chinese shareholders alongside the American firm. Approval allowed the organizers to proceed; it did not yet allow the company to conduct securities business. They still had to register the enterprise, supply capital and obtain an operating permit. Registration came in August, the permit in December.[^3]

Inside the company, permission had to be made usable. Its 2019 disclosure describes hiring, training, systems procurement, internal procedures and regulatory inspection. The brokerage department expected formal operations around the middle of 2020, after connections and testing with exchanges, clearing institutions and custody banks. The following year's report recorded two completed investment-banking projects. An official could alter an ownership limit; the firm still had to construct a business within it.[^33]

Phase One made a particular contribution to this process. In October 2019, the regulator had scheduled the removal of the securities ownership ceiling for December 1, 2020. The agreement moved the deadline to April 1. When the regulator confirmed that date in March, it expressly attributed the change to implementation of Phase One. The bargain had advanced the published timetable by eight months.[^4]

That was a concession worth identifying accurately. JPMorgan possessed its operating permit before the agreement was signed. American negotiators could claim acceleration of the ownership opening without claiming to have created every permission that preceded it. Martin Chorzempa made this distinction in his contemporary assessment: the value added by the bargain had to be separated from liberalization already announced. Nicholas Lardy and Tianlei Huang placed the opening to foreign institutions within a broader, uneven process of financial integration. These accounts leave room for both Chinese reform priorities and foreign pressure; the regulator's revised date supplies the concrete gain.[^5]

Eligibility for full ownership still left a transaction to complete. In October 2020, one Chinese partner offered its 20 percent holding for sale. Reports described the larger stake JPMorgan could acquire. Yet the company's year-end shareholder table continued to show its original 51 percent. In August 2021 the regulator accepted the filing for it to acquire the five domestic partners' holdings. Shareholder registration was completed on August 25. The firm was now wholly foreign owned.[^6]

It was also doing business. Net brokerage fees rose from about RMB 0.93 million in 2020 to RMB 138.44 million in 2021 and RMB 555.95 million in 2022. Investment-banking fees rose sharply in 2021 before declining the next year. These accounts demonstrate commercial activity in the authorized businesses, beyond a license framed for display. They cannot isolate the effect of ownership from the firm's staffing, capital, clients or market conditions.[^7]

Leung's ambition for an integrated operation helps explain why successful implementation does not end discussion with regulators. A group admitted to one business may want to combine it with another or offer a new product. JPMorgan's 2022 disclosure recorded a newly registered Beijing branch whose operating permit was still being processed. A separate 2023 approval concerning its fund-management business imposed continuing requirements, including rules on data and personal information. Such decisions could matter greatly to the firm without constituting a reversal of the securities ownership concession.[^8]

The negotiator's task is to distinguish a new request from an old benefit withheld. If an agreement removes an ownership ceiling, the need for an additional branch license does not by itself show evasion. If the promised benefit depends on a license that remains unavailable, the ownership concession may be worth much less than expected. Before exchanging concessions, officials need the intended beneficiary's account of the remaining approvals, responsible authorities and commercial timetable. Otherwise they may obtain a permission the firm cannot use—or fail to recognize the value of one it can.

## Permission to sell a seed

For a seed developer, the decisive Chinese permission may govern a crop grown thousands of miles from China. A trait approved for cultivation in the United States can still face a commercial obstacle if China has not authorized imports. Grain moves through shared handling systems; developers and farmers must consider the risk that an unapproved trait will reach a customer who cannot accept it. The permissions examined here covered imported material for processing, not the cultivation of genetically modified crops inside China.[^9]

The companies tried to make that problem an American negotiating priority. In May 2017, lobbyist Hunt Shipman sought a meeting between Dow AgroSciences chief executive Tim Hassinger and the new agriculture secretary, Sonny Perdue. USDA adviser Rebeckah Adcock directed him to the scheduler and later offered to press the request. On May 9, she needed talking points before the following morning. Shipman supplied regulatory summaries for Dow's corn and soybean products and a paper on Chinese approvals.[^11]

Days later, the two governments announced an undertaking to move eight pending American applications through China's National Biosafety Committee. The committee would evaluate them by the end of May, explain any further information required and issue certificates for products that passed. It was a promise to undertake specified reviews, not to approve every application. Timely, science-based approvals had already appeared in the 2016 bilateral dialogue; the new arrangement attached work and dates to selected pending cases.[^10]

The announcement changed the immediate argument in Washington. On May 16, another lobbyist, Joe Bischoff, sent Adcock proposed questions for a congressional hearing. They challenged foreign regulatory barriers that prevented American farmers from using new seed technology. Adcock asked whether that was the right formulation after the China announcement. Bischoff replied that Dow still wanted the question sent and that he had already softened it. The question Representative Rodney Davis delivered the following day acknowledged the new arrangement and asked how China would be held accountable. The correspondence does not identify who revised it, but it shows the company pressing a claim while an official defended the progress just announced.[^35]

In June, Hassinger supplied a result that the administration could welcome. China had granted import approval for Enlist corn, he wrote, and Dow could now launch it for American farmers to plant the next year. He credited Perdue's support through the bilateral arrangement and sought continued help with the remaining traits. Adcock said she had passed the message to the secretary. The exchange follows a corporate request into official preparation and back to a reported benefit. It leaves the Chinese decision itself out of view; Hassinger's thanks cannot establish what persuaded the approving authority.[^11]

Beijing was also changing its own procedure. In February 2017, before the bilateral announcement, the agriculture ministry had ordered a change in responsibility for specified safety tests. For an initial application for an import safety certificate, foreign developers would no longer have to arrange those tests before the application could be accepted. The ministry would commission them. Testing remained part of the process; arranging it had become the approving authority's job.[^ag-review-reform]

The ministry's service guide, issued that May, separated acceptance, testing, preliminary laboratory review, National Biosafety Committee evaluation and the final ministerial decision. Its stated processing period was 270 days, but expert review and inspection/testing did not count toward that clock. A company waiting for permission needed to know more than whether its application was under review. The next action could belong to a laboratory, a committee or the officials routing the approval. The published procedure identifies those stages without locating the particular soybean or canola application within them.[^ag-review-guide]

Securing one approval did not by itself establish a dependable process for the next application. Phase One sought to change the process as well as obtain decisions. Its biotechnology annex required a completeness check within five working days, written safety-related explanations for additional information and sufficient committee meetings. For products that passed safety evaluation, China would issue an approval decision and certificate within twenty working days of the committee meeting's conclusion. Authorizations would last at least five years. The promised average interval from formal submission to a final decision was no more than twenty-four months.[^12]

An average was a consequential choice. It did not give each company a twenty-four-month deadline, and a final decision could be a refusal. To know whether this obligation was being met, American officials would need dates for a body of applications, including when they entered the process and what decisions followed. Acceptance records, testing dates, requests for further information and committee dispositions would help explain the intervening time. A celebrated approval would establish less than proponents might claim; a single exceptionally long review would not settle the average. Negotiating the measure and obtaining the information to apply it were parts of the same task.

Enlist E3 soybeans show how several permissions could intervene between a diplomatic announcement and a launch. China's approval became public in January 2019, during renewed trade talks. But the original certificate ran from December 20, 2018. The decision's legal period had begun before that week's diplomacy, and contemporary reporting distinguished the public announcement from the company's receipt of its paperwork.[^13]

The Chinese permission still left another importing country's decision outstanding. Corteva and MS Technologies announced the American launch in February, after Philippine import approval. China later renewed Enlist E3 for a five-year period ending in December 2026, compared with the original three-year authorization. The longer term was a usable benefit consistent with Phase One's requirement. It did not answer how quickly new applications were being processed.[^14]

Optimum GLY canola required a much longer wait. According to contemporary reporting, the application for event DP73496 had been filed in China in July 2012. The certificate eventually issued was valid from January 5, 2023. Roughly ten and a half years lay between the reported filing and permission.[^15]

That interval is striking, but assigning its whole commercial cost to China would misread the product's other difficulties. The European Union had received an application in May 2012 and did not authorize the product until March 2022. A favorable food-safety opinion came only in June 2021; both the standing committee and appeal committee subsequently failed to deliver an opinion. The Commission's eventual authorization excluded cultivation. China's permission came about nine months after the EU's. In neither jurisdiction does the filing date alone reveal every request for information or establish when a complete application was ready for decision.[^16]

By late January 2023, a named Corteva representative described China as the final outstanding global approval. Stewardship and communications work remained. The following month, the company announced a commercial launch in the United States and Canada for 2023, with a later full launch in Australia. At this point the Chinese permission can be connected to a particular business decision: the company could proceed with the launch it announced. Actual acreage and sales would be further measures of its value.[^17]

Meanwhile, the process remained disputed. At the December 2025 hearing, the seed industry's representative reported an average review period of sixty-nine months and two approvals that had taken at least nine years. The 2026 US National Trade Estimate likewise alleged failure to meet the agreed average and criticized the absence of published results from committee meetings. Neither supplied the complete application histories needed here to reproduce the calculation. Their complaints remain serious: securing selected products had not satisfied those who wanted a dependable route for the next product.[^18]

The contrast with finance is instructive. A published date for removing an ownership ceiling could be checked against a regulator's announcement. A promise about average review time required continuing information from applicants and authorities. The second obligation was harder to monitor even though the text looked precise. A negotiator who asks only whether China has accepted a number may overlook the harder question of how anyone will know whether it has been met.

## Buying a number

The purchase commitments posed a different test: China had undertaken to buy specified increments of American goods and services over two years.[^19]

By the end of the period, purchases were far short. Chad Bown's Peterson Institute calculations put covered goods at 60 percent of the commitment using American export data and 62 percent using Chinese import data. Adding covered services on the American basis produced $290.8 billion against a $502.4 billion benchmark, or 58 percent. His conclusion that China had bought none of the additional $200 billion concerned the increment above the baseline. China had continued buying large amounts; it had failed to deliver the promised increase.[^20]

Several forces intervened. The pandemic damaged services trade, aircraft supply faced problems and the trade war's restrictions persisted. Bown's analysis also had to work around unpublished allocations and incomplete coverage of some services. The arithmetic established a major shortfall without assigning it to a single cause. When USTR initiated a new investigation in October 2025, it used a different benchmark—more than $535 billion—and a shortfall exceeding $217 billion. Its notice did not reconcile that figure with Bown's. The precise estimates should remain separate; neither yields a plausible claim of full delivery.[^21][^22]

Both governments had to decide what to do with that failure. China's commerce ministry stressed the pandemic and disrupted supply chains while describing continued communication. Katherine Tai, appearing before the Senate Finance Committee in March 2022, said China had failed to meet its purchases and placed the administration's response within a wider policy involving domestic investment and allies. The parties continued talking, but the public accounts do not establish that they agreed to extend the expired 2020–2021 numerical target. The agreement's expectation of a continuing trajectory through 2025 was a different provision.[^23]

China's April 2025 white paper defended its implementation and invoked unforeseen events. Phase One supplied a route for that argument: Article 7.6.2 required consultation when an unforeseeable event outside a party's control delayed timely compliance. It did not automatically release either government from its obligation. The white paper also said neither side had formally activated the agreement's bilateral dispute procedure, despite repeated review contacts. These were China's representations of the process, not jointly published minutes.[^24]

The argument had consequently shifted from what China had promised to what each government was prepared to demand, concede or risk next. This is one reason Solomon's attention to the continuing relationship remains valuable. But treating the dispute simply as a difference in national attitudes toward promises would discard the things that made it negotiable: an expired purchase period, disputed responsibility, a consultation clause and several possible enforcement routes.

## Permissions in exchange for permissions

A separate dispute in the spring and summer of 2025 put a different kind of performance at issue. The immediate bargain concerned permission for future transactions; it did not settle the unfulfilled Phase One purchases. On July 2, Synopsys announced that it had received a letter from the US Commerce Department rescinding restrictions imposed on its China business in May. The chip-design software company was working to restore customers' access. The following day, Siemens reported that its own May restrictions had been lifted and that it had restored access, sales and support for the specified software and technology. These short company statements recorded part of what China had obtained through another negotiation with Washington.[^36]

China had something the Americans wanted in return. On April 4, its commerce ministry and customs administration had placed specified rare-earth items under export controls. The State Council had approved the measure. It took effect immediately. Exporters needed licenses, and customs could withhold shipments while questioning whether goods had been classified correctly. The notice covered items associated with seven elements, including some magnet materials; it neither prohibited every rare-earth export nor confined its controls to American destinations.[^37]

The industrial position behind that authority was formidable. The International Energy Agency estimates that China accounted for about 91 percent of global separation and refining of the four magnet rare earths it measures in 2024. A foreign manufacturer could have willing Chinese suppliers and still need the government's permission before they could ship. Restrictions at this stage could affect production far beyond the value of the materials themselves.[^38]

The Geneva meeting in May had appeared to offer relief. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer met Vice Premier He Lifeng, and the governments agreed to suspend much of the tariff escalation. The joint statement required action by May 14, including China’s suspension or removal of non-tariff countermeasures taken against the United States since April 2. It did not name the April 4 notice, publish a list of licenses or specify how quickly individual applications would move.[^39]

That omission left a commercially important distinction unsettled in public. Repealing a control and allowing transactions under it could both relieve a manufacturer, but they left different authority in Chinese hands. Reuters subsequently reported, on the account of one person briefed on Geneva, that China had privately accepted the rare-earth controls as covered countermeasures. The released statement did not disclose how that understanding was to be implemented. The same reporting described burdensome applications and efforts to add processing staff. A delayed license could reflect administrative work as well as pressure; the reporting could not establish the reason for each delay.[^40]

The application system explains part of the distance between an undertaking and a shipment. Ordinarily, an application concerned a contract and a specified end user and use. Changing the destination, end user or use of an approved export required a new application. The ordinary forty-five-working-day review period began at acceptance; returned applications awaited complete, compliant material. Time for technical identification, expert consultation and on-site checks fell outside the clock. A factory's wait could therefore begin before the period by which officials measured their own work. These published rules cannot explain a particular delay without the application's history.[^licensing-procedure]

The governments soon accused each other of undermining the accommodation. On June 2, MOFCOM defended China's implementation and protested new American restrictions, including those on chip-design software. Bessent later explained the American action differently: critical materials had not moved as agreed, so Washington had imposed countermeasures. Each side described its own restrictions as a response to the other's conduct. Each now had a specific administrative act it wanted reversed or accelerated.[^41]

Xi and Trump spoke on June 5. The Chinese account said they agreed to another meeting of their teams; it supplied no licensing timetable. Talks in London on June 9–10 produced agreement in principle on an implementation framework. He Lifeng faced Bessent and Greer, now joined by Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick. Further communication followed. On June 27, MOFCOM said that, after approval, the parties had confirmed the details. China would approve eligible applications under its law; the United States would correspondingly remove a series of restrictions. The authority to control exports remained, while its administration supplied the terms of relief.[^42]

The distinction could help the parties make a limited deal. Washington could seek supplies needed by manufacturers without first obtaining the dismantling of China's control system. Beijing could seek the removal of particular American restrictions without first securing an end to US technology controls. This left further disputes outside the arrangement. Reuters reported, citing two people briefed on London, that China had not committed to clear some specialized materials needed by military suppliers. Its report also described an apparent attempt to connect that access with advanced AI-chip restrictions. Bessent publicly rejected such an exchange. An attempted linkage was not a concession obtained.[^43]

The software companies' July statements show one side of the accommodation taking effect. China's commerce ministry likewise reported that it was processing eligible applications and that Washington had notified it of removals. Yet relief varied among customers. In late June, Volkswagen told Reuters its supply was stable, while Dexter Magnetic Technologies' chief executive reported only five approvals from 180 applications, all for non-defence uses. Europe's suppliers were pursuing their own relief: MOFCOM said in July that it had established an expedited channel for European enterprises. The Chinese licensing system served several diplomatic relationships at once.[^44]

Chinese producers also distinguished obtaining approvals from ending the need to apply. In its June 13 investor record, magnet maker JL Mag reported permits for destinations including America, Europe and Southeast Asia. Controlled products would continue to require applications and approval before export. The company repeated the position on June 26.[^licensing-exporter]

For a manufacturer unable to secure supplies, another source was not necessarily an immediate alternative. Bajaj Auto's Rakesh Sharma explained in May that changing the materials used in its vehicles required development, investment, testing and integration. His immediate concern was getting the application process moving. In August he reported that magnet shortages had begun compromising production in June, with a fifty-percent July shortfall for Chetak electric scooters. The company was pursuing official relief and alternatives at the same time. India's manufacturers had their own applications and commercial deadlines; the American accommodation could not be assumed to settle them.[^licensing-buyer]

Aggregate magnet shipments recovered in July. Reuters' report of Chinese customs data put July rare-earth magnet exports at 5,577 metric tons, including 619 to the United States, slightly above the American destination's July 2024 volume. Physical goods were moving again. Those totals could not establish that every controlled item or end user had received clearance, any more than the software releases could establish that all American technology restrictions had ended.[^45]

The next presidential bargain tested the distinction again. After Trump and Xi met in Busan in October, the White House announced that China would suspend the expanded controls announced on October 9 and issue general licenses benefiting American end users and their suppliers. Washington described the expected result as the effective elimination of controls. China's implementing notice of November 7 did something more precisely bounded: it suspended six specified announcements through November 10, 2026. The April 4 measure was not among them. Suspension of the new measures and permission under the older system were different parts of the accommodation.[^permissions-busan]

By the May 2026 summit, supply remained a subject for the presidents. The White House said China would address American concerns over shortages of yttrium, scandium, neodymium and indium, as well as restrictions on equipment and technology. China's commerce ministry described discussion of the concerns and continued review of compliant civilian applications under Chinese law. Neither account announced repeal. In the same explanation, the ministry said the teams would work toward extending the suspensions due to expire in November. An intention to negotiate an extension did not yet give a buyer a longer period of protection.[^permissions-may]

Another obstacle lay beyond the processing of an application. On August 5, China barred organizations and individuals within its territory from specified dealings and cooperation with six American entities, including the Responsible Business Alliance, a supply-chain standards organization. The ministry accused them of assisting American sanctions connected with Xinjiang. Its order named those entities; it did not announce a general prohibition on supplying American manufacturers with rare earths.[^permissions-rba]

Yet the measure could affect suppliers' willingness to transact. Reuters reported on September 4 that some had declined American shipments, fearing consequences from the sanctions or diversion to prohibited users. Its unnamed sources also described American companies receiving licenses after long waits. The reporters could not establish the extent of the refusals. Relief and hesitation could coexist.[^permissions-suppliers]

Officials seeking to restore trade consequently needed to distinguish a pending government decision from a supplier's unwillingness to proceed. A license, even if obtained, could not compel a company to accept an order it considered dangerous. Clarifying how separate restrictions applied could matter alongside securing an approval. The reporting did not establish a government instruction to hold those shipments.

The episode does not reveal a Chinese discovery that exports could be restricted for advantage. Earlier duties, quotas and trading restrictions on rare earths had already generated a WTO dispute; in May 2015 China reported that it had removed the condemned measures, while Washington disputed full compliance. What the 2025 sequence adds is a close view of how contemporary industrial dependence and administrative authority became negotiable together. The authority could survive the accommodation that restored trade.[^46]

Research by Viking Bohman, Audrye Wong and Victor Ferguson identifies a related difficulty: a government can explain the offense that prompted a restriction without specifying what would secure its removal. Formal rules can coexist with discretion over relief. In the London sequence, the negotiators did identify reciprocal administrative actions, and some of those actions reached firms. Obtaining that path mattered even though the controls and wider political disagreements survived.[^47]

For an official negotiating relief, that creates a choice with costs on either side. Insisting on repeal may seek a more durable opening but postpone the shipments firms urgently need. Accepting licenses may restore production while leaving later transactions exposed to review. The 2026 sequel supplied another reason to ask whether the supplier could use the permission, alongside questions about its products, end users and duration. A general assurance that trade will resume leaves too much of that choice to the next round.

## The cost of enforcing an agreement

In the separate Phase One dispute, the investigation that brought Gackle and Cooper to the December hearing began on October 24, 2025. USTR asked whether China had denied Americans the benefits of Phase One, what commercial burdens resulted and what remedies would be appropriate. It invited evidence and proposals for duties, service restrictions or import restrictions. It also requested consultations under the American Trade Act. This was a domestic Section 301 investigation, not a documented appeal under Chapter 7 of Phase One. Washington was choosing an instrument as well as deciding how much pressure to apply.[^25]

The agricultural witnesses exposed the constituency problem behind that choice. Gackle's farmers still depended on sales to China. Cooper described ethanol exports that had ceased and investments made on expectations of access. The practical difference was the business each industry believed remained at risk. Their testimony does not prove that those interests determined American policy. It shows why an official could not infer support for a remedy from agreement that China had fallen short.[^26]

The timing sharpened the choice. A new presidential accommodation in autumn 2025 had produced another American announcement of Chinese soybean purchases. Gackle questioned its certainty and timetable even as he feared disturbing the negotiations. The White House also described a suspension of the separate maritime and shipbuilding Section 301 action. That suspension did not establish that the Phase One investigation had been suspended too. In February 2026, China's commerce ministry said the old agreement had been discussed during the five rounds of talks in 2025, defended its implementation and warned against further measures.[^27]

An enforcement threat thus entered a relationship already carrying prospective benefits and other disputes. A government may gain negotiating room by keeping several matters open together. It may also find that obtaining the next concession becomes an argument for postponing a remedy on the last one. The records examined here establish that tension, an investigation and a contested response; they do not supply a final settlement of the enforcement dispute.[^28]

The implication for an agreement's design is practical. Before relying on a remedy, negotiators need to know which constituencies would bear its immediate costs, which would expect to benefit and what alternatives the government would retain if support weakened. A commitment to consult can keep a problem alive. A right to retaliate can improve the price of settlement. Neither compels a future administration to incur costs it has come to judge excessive.

## Negotiating the next mechanism

By the summer of 2026, both governments were considering how to organize further exchanges. The White House had announced Boards of Trade and Investment and new prospective agricultural commitments after the May summit. Chinese statements described preliminary results and further work.[^29] USTR's June consultation contemplated equal-value trade in nonsensitive products, with tariff changes subject to agreement and law.[^30] In July, Chinese officials described discussions of reciprocal tariff reductions covering $30 billion on each side. Products and implementation remained to be agreed; the public accounts did not provide an agreed agricultural purchase schedule.[^31][^32]

The proposed mechanisms did not settle the choice Gackle and Cooper had put before Washington. Prospective business could make restraint attractive to one industry while a lost market made action attractive to another. Both could agree that China had failed to deliver. The administration still had to decide whether to impose further costs, which Americans would bear them and what it would accept in return. Another agreement could change those choices; it could not make them on the government's behalf.
