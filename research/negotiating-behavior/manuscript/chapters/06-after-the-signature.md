# 6. After the signature

On March 20, 2020, JPMorgan announced that its new securities company in China had opened for business. The coronavirus epidemic had made an ordinary launch difficult; the firm marked the occasion virtually. Two months after the signing of the Phase One trade agreement, an American financial institution was beginning to use access that had occupied negotiators for years. The announcement could readily become an illustration of the agreement's success. Its chronology makes a more interesting argument. The company already possessed its operating permit before the agreement was signed. It opened with JPMorgan holding 51 percent, became wholly owned the following year, and subsequently reported substantial brokerage revenue. These were different achievements, obtained through different decisions.[^1]

The distinction matters for judging both negotiation and implementation. Chinese officials could deliver a meaningful opening without having originated it as a concession in the last negotiation. A signed commitment could accelerate an existing reform without securing every license needed to use it. Commercial operation could demonstrate that access was real without demonstrating that foreign ownership caused the business to prosper. Each proposition requires a different piece of evidence.

The cases produce a mixed but concrete answer. Phase One accelerated a published securities-opening timetable by eight months; company records trace access into ownership and revenue. Selected crop permissions enabled identifiable launch plans while procedural timing remained contested. Aggregate purchases fell far short. By 2026 the parties were still arguing about the old agreement while designing another mechanism for managing trade. Explaining those different results requires following each commitment to the decision that would make it useful.

## An opening that became a business

Financial reform did not begin in January 2020. In November 2017, after Donald Trump's visit to China, Vice Finance Minister Zhu Guangyao announced a prospective increase to 51 percent in foreign ownership of securities, fund-management and futures companies, followed by removal of the ceiling three years after implementation. Xinhua's account located the announcement among summit outcomes, while also recording Chinese requests concerning American investment treatment and export restrictions. The opening would benefit investors from countries other than the United States. This was evidence of reform within a bilateral political setting, not a basis for assigning its entire cause either to domestic policy or American pressure.[^2]

The securities regulator's April 2018 rules made foreign control possible. JPMorgan pursued a majority-owned firm. The establishment decision, signed by the China Securities Regulatory Commission on March 13, 2019 and received by the company on March 29, authorized a business with RMB 800 million in registered capital and five Chinese shareholders alongside JPMorgan. It identified permitted lines of business but required further steps: organizing and registering the company, providing capital and obtaining an operating permit. The company could not conduct the securities business merely because its establishment had been approved. Registration followed on August 22; the securities operating permit followed on December 18.[^3]

That sequence gives the agreement a narrower but firmer place in the story. In October 2019 the regulator had announced that the securities ownership ceiling would disappear on December 1, 2020. Phase One required its removal no later than April 1. In March, the regulator explicitly invoked implementation of the agreement when confirming the April date. Eight months of acceleration are therefore observable against the previously published timetable. The agreement did not invent the opening, and the opening was not merely a repetition of what China had already scheduled for the same date.[^4]

This is close to the distinction Martin Chorzempa drew in his contemporary assessment of the financial-services chapter: prior liberalization must be separated from marginal improvements obtained in the bargain. Nicholas Lardy and Tianlei Huang subsequently situated institutional opening within China's uneven financial integration, distinguishing the entry of foreign firms from the liberalization of capital flows. These analyses provide useful alternatives to a single-pressure explanation. The published Chinese timetable and the regulator's express link to Phase One establish the change more directly than an inference from the agreement's political celebration.[^5]

The company then had to turn eligibility into ownership. In October 2020 a Chinese partner's 20 percent holding was put up for sale. Contemporary reporting described what JPMorgan's stake would become if it acquired that holding; the company's year-end disclosure still recorded the old 51 percent structure. An announced sale was not a completed transfer. In August 2021 the regulator accepted the filing for JPMorgan to acquire the five domestic partners' holdings. The firm completed the shareholder registration on August 25 and became wholly foreign owned. Its capital also increased substantially. Those records establish a completed ownership change, beyond an application or favorable announcement.[^6]

The subsequent accounts establish something more valuable than permission alone. Net brokerage fees rose from approximately RMB 0.93 million in 2020 to RMB 138.44 million in 2021 and RMB 555.95 million in 2022. Investment-banking fees rose from RMB 3.80 million to RMB 41.29 million before falling to RMB 37.60 million. Revenue was being earned in the authorized activities. The different movements also warn against attributing every commercial result to the same regulatory change. Staffing, capital, clients, the firm's preparation and market conditions intervened between permission and income. Full ownership arrived during 2021, so the annual comparison cannot isolate its effect.[^7]

Nor did the achievement eliminate subsequent administrative work. The company's 2022 disclosure recorded registration of a Beijing branch while its operating permit remained in process. A separate January 2023 decision authorizing a JPMorgan fund-management ownership change imposed continuing compliance requirements, including obligations concerning data and personal information. Neither item proves that regulators deliberately withheld the benefit of the securities opening. They show why a negotiator cannot equate removal of one ownership restriction with the disappearance of regulation across a financial group.[^8]

The positive result changes how Solomon's concern with bargaining after agreement should be applied. A government can preserve continuing regulatory authority while implementing an important concession. Further applications may concern genuinely additional activity, or they may become a means of narrowing promised access. Distinguishing these possibilities requires the particular obligation, a complete application, a decision and its reasons. The interval since a summit supplies none of those by itself. Here, actual operation and income make a general claim of illusory access untenable; the surviving approvals make a general claim of completed liberalization equally inadequate.

## A certificate, a planting season and another country's decision

Biotechnology exposes a different problem. A seed developer may have permission to cultivate a crop in its production market but hesitate to launch it broadly while a major destination has not authorized imports. Commodities move through shared handling systems, and an unapproved trait can create risks beyond the individual seed sale. The relevant Chinese permissions in the two cases examined here were for imported material used in processing, not for planting genetically modified crops in China. Treating them as cultivation approvals would mistake the commercial bottleneck.[^9]

The effort to address that bottleneck also preceded Phase One. The 2016 Strategic and Economic Dialogue included commitments concerning timely, science-based approvals. The May 2017 joint early-harvest announcement required the National Biosafety Committee to evaluate eight pending American applications by the end of that month. It addressed written explanations for additional information, further committee meetings after resubmission, and certificates for products that passed evaluation. The undertaking was to evaluate pending applications through specified procedures. It did not promise that all eight would be approved.[^10]

American administrative records reveal some of the work behind these demands. Emails released by the Department of Agriculture show industry representatives seeking a meeting for Dow AgroSciences chief executive Tim Hassinger, supplying briefing material and discussing possible congressional questions with department official Rebeckah Adcock in May 2017. Adcock pushed back on proposed questioning after the China announcement. In June, Hassinger credited the bilateral effort for approval of Enlist corn, and Adcock forwarded that message within the department. The record reveals access, information supply, official response and the company's interpretation of a result. It does not establish that China approved the corn because of the particular meeting, or identify every soybean trait mentioned in missing attachments. The corresponding public congressional hearing shows how a broad demand for accountability became a question that also acknowledged the announced progress.[^11]

Phase One made the procedure more definite. Annex 16 required an average of no more than 24 months between formal submission and a final decision to approve or disapprove specified agricultural biotechnology products. It required an initial completeness check within five working days, written explanations connecting requests for information to safety, sufficient committee meetings, and a certificate within 20 working days after a product passed the committee's evaluation. Authorizations were to last at least five years. This was neither a guarantee of approval nor an individual 24-month maximum. The negotiated object was partly the administration of decisions: how an applicant would learn what was missing, how a stalled review would resume and how long a successful permission would remain useful.[^12]

Enlist E3 soybeans illustrate both a real permission and the danger of dating it by the news cycle. The original Chinese list, published on January 8, 2019, recorded a certificate effective from December 20, 2018 to December 20, 2021. Publication coincided with renewed trade talks, but the certificate's stated period had begun earlier. The January talks therefore cannot simply be credited with producing a decision made that week. Contemporary trade reporting also distinguished public approval from the company's receipt of the paperwork.[^13]

China's action still did not complete the product's international launch conditions. Corteva and MS Technologies announced the American launch on February 21, 2019 after Philippine import approval. The sequence identifies two separate gates: Chinese access was important, but the company still awaited another destination's decision. The Chinese renewal later listed Enlist E3 for December 17, 2021 through December 16, 2026, a five-year period compared with the original three. That is a concrete improvement consistent with the agreement's authorization requirement. It is not evidence that a new application had been processed within the promised average, or that the agreement alone caused the longer renewal.[^14]

Optimum GLY canola presents a harder outcome. Reuters reported that the relevant Chinese application for event DP73496 had been filed in July 2012. It remained pending in the agency's January 2019 survey. The Chinese certificate eventually listed a validity period beginning January 5, 2023; publication followed on January 13. Approximately ten and a half years separated the reported application from permission. That interval captures a company's prolonged uncertainty, although the public record does not reveal all requests for additional material or the dates on which a complete dossier was available.[^15]

A comparison of the same product prevents that interval from becoming an automatic explanation of a decade of lost sales. The European Union had received its application on May 15, 2012. A favorable European Food Safety Authority opinion came on June 17, 2021, and Commission authorization on March 31, 2022, excluding cultivation. The Commission decision records that both the standing committee and the appeal committee had failed to deliver an opinion. China was thus not the only major import market in which authorization took years. Its final permission came roughly nine months after the EU's. This does not excuse Chinese delay or prove that the EU was the sole earlier obstacle; it limits the claim that China's decision alone postponed a globally ready product throughout the entire interval.[^16]

In late January 2023, a named Corteva representative told the *Western Producer* that China had supplied the final global approval, while stewardship and industry communications still had to be completed. In February the company announced commercial launch in the United States and Canada for 2023 and a later full launch in Australia. Here the Chinese decision can be connected to an identified commercial sequence more closely than a general announcement of agricultural cooperation. The announcement remains evidence of a launch plan, not a measurement of acreage actually planted or revenue earned.[^17]

The procedural dispute nevertheless endured. At the December 2025 American hearing on Phase One, the seed industry's representative reported an average approval time of 69 months and two approvals that had taken at least nine years. The 2026 National Trade Estimate Report asserted that China had not met its average-time commitment and criticized unpublished results from biosafety committee meetings in 2025. These are consequential industry and American government assessments, not an independently reconstructed dataset of all Chinese applications. The certificates establish that permissions existed; they do not establish that the approval system satisfied its timing obligations. Conversely, slow approvals do not show that each delay was a deliberately traded bargaining asset.[^18]

The comparison with finance is therefore substantive. For JPMorgan, company accounts follow formal opening into sustained operation. For these crops, the record follows selected permissions into renewals and launch announcements while leaving the broader procedural promise disputed.

## The purchase target and the business it could not command

Phase One's purchase commitments made another kind of promise. They concerned aggregate flows of goods and services over 2020–2021, built on a 2017 baseline. A regulator could remove an ownership ceiling by a specified date; meeting an aggregate dollar target depended on many transactions, prices, demand, supply and the relationship's other restrictions. The agreement itself recognized commercial considerations and market conditions. Those provisions did not erase its numerical undertaking, but they matter when explaining why performance fell short.[^19]

Chad Bown's Peterson Institute tracker separates two official statistical perspectives for covered goods. Using American export data, purchases were $210.1 billion against an estimated $352.2 billion commitment, or 60 percent. Using Chinese import data, the corresponding figures were $235.3 billion against $380.5 billion, or 62 percent. These are estimates for the goods covered by the agreement, not every good traded between the countries. Including covered services on the American-data basis, Bown calculated $290.8 billion against $502.4 billion, or 58 percent.[^20]

His memorable conclusion that China bought none of the additional $200 billion described performance relative to the aggregate baseline. It did not mean that China bought nothing from the United States. Nor does the shortfall identify one cause. Bown's analysis discusses the pandemic's effect on services, aircraft supply problems and the continuing effects of the trade war. The agreement's unpublished allocations below its major categories and differences in statistical coverage also limit replication. In particular, the analysis excludes some services supplied through commercial presence that the agreement encompasses. Its monthly paths are illustrative allocations of annual commitments, not an additional set of contractual monthly deadlines.[^21]

The American government's later enforcement benchmark must be kept separate. The October 2025 initiation notice described a commitment exceeding $535 billion and an aggregate shortfall exceeding $217 billion, citing official American export data. That is not Bown's $502.4 billion benchmark with a different label. The notice does not provide the reconciliation needed to merge the two calculations. Both point to a large shortfall; the disagreement over denominators and coverage prevents a single exact number from being passed between them without explanation.[^22]

The governments also disagreed about what the shortfall meant for their next steps. In January 2022, China's commerce ministry emphasized the pandemic, recession and disrupted supply chains while describing normal communication over implementation. In March, Katherine Tai told the Senate Finance Committee that China had not met its purchase commitments and connected her administration's response to a broader approach involving domestic investment and cooperation with allies. Continued discussion was real, but the available public accounts do not establish an agreed extension of the numerical 2020–2021 target. The agreement's expectation of a continuing trajectory in 2022–2025 was a different provision.[^23]

China's April 2025 white paper developed its defense further. It described implementation measures and an extensive sequence of review calls, and asserted that neither side had formally activated the bilateral dispute procedure. It invoked unforeseen events in explaining difficulties. That argument must be compared with the text: Article 7.6.2 provides for consultation when an unforeseeable event outside the parties' control delays timely compliance. It does not automatically waive the obligation. The white paper's reference to Article 7.6.1 is inconsistent with the numbered clause in the agreement. Its reported meeting totals are evidence of China's public account, not independently verified minutes of each exchange.[^24]

Here Solomon's emphasis on the relationship after agreement remains useful, but insufficient. China could invoke the surrounding relationship and American restrictions to contest responsibility. The United States could retain pressure while deciding how much enforcement it was willing to risk. Yet the dispute now concerned measurable purchases, competing definitions of the benchmark, a negotiated unforeseeable-event clause and distinct enforcement routes. The analytical work is to connect those instruments to the parties' behavior, rather than to explain away the arithmetic as a difference in national attitudes toward promises.

## Enforcement creates another negotiation

The United States initiated its new Section 301 investigation on October 24, 2025. It asked whether China had denied the benefits of Phase One, what burden nonimplementation imposed on American commerce and what response might be appropriate. The notice requested concrete examples and possible duties, service restrictions or import restrictions. It also recorded a request for consultations under Section 303(a) of the American Trade Act. That request cannot be silently relabeled an appeal under the agreement's Chapter 7. A domestic investigation, a bilateral appeal and ordinary implementation reviews are different procedures even when they address some of the same conduct.[^25]

The December hearing exposed disagreement among intended beneficiaries of enforcement. American Soybean Association representative Josh Gackle criticized implementation but worried that the investigation could jeopardize current progress with China. Renewable Fuels Association president Geoff Cooper supported the investigation and measures to secure access. The contrast was not between one industry interested in trade and another indifferent to it. They faced different markets and judged the risks of renewed pressure differently. A government seeking to impose costs on China also had to consider costs to constituencies expecting Chinese purchases.[^26]

That concern had a concrete setting. The White House's November 2025 account of a new deal described soybean purchases and reciprocal policy measures, including suspension of the maritime and shipbuilding Section 301 action. The existence of that suspension does not establish suspension of the separate Phase One investigation. In February 2026, China's commerce ministry defended its implementation, blamed American restrictions for obstacles, and said Phase One had been discussed during the five negotiating rounds in 2025. It warned against additional measures. Old obligations and new accommodations were being discussed together, without a public instrument showing that the old dispute had been extinguished.[^27]

The published records examined here establish an investigation and a contested response. They do not supply a documented final enforcement settlement.[^28]

## The 2026 mechanism was itself an unfinished bargain

The May 2026 summit generated another ambitious American account of agricultural commitments and two proposed institutions: a Board of Trade and a Board of Investment. The White House described at least $17 billion in agricultural purchases annually in 2027 and 2028, with 2026 prorated, additional to the soybean commitments made in October 2025. China's May statements described preliminary results, further work on the mechanisms and agricultural targets whose achievement required suitable conditions. The different formulations need to remain visible. An American fact sheet was a public claim about the deal, not a jointly signed schedule of completed transactions.[^29]

By June, there was an observable American step beyond announcing a mechanism. USTR requested public comments on reciprocal managed trade in nonsensitive products. It asked for product classifications, recent trade values and assessments of effects on producers, workers and consumers. It also asked how frequently the Board should meet, when its product coverage should change and how the parties should exchange data. The contemplated arrangement involved equal-value trade and tariff modifications subject to agreement and applicable law. Comments were due July 10, with responses due July 27.[^30]

The Chinese side subsequently described its own consultation. At the July 23 State Council Information Office briefing, commerce official Meng Huating said the teams were discussing the Board's structure, functions and mode of operation, and exploring reciprocal tariff reductions covering $30 billion on each side. China, she said, was soliciting views from domestic businesses, associations, local governments and American-invested businesses and associations. Specific product arrangements still had to be agreed and implemented. The paired accounts identify channels through which preferences could enter the governments’ preparation, while leaving their influence and the Board’s final design unestablished.[^31]

Even the agricultural total remained a matter for careful attribution. On July 2, a Reuters question described the $17 billion figure as including soybeans and asked about purchase timing and tariffs. The commerce ministry's answer emphasized a guiding target and autonomous commercial decisions without affirming that characterization of the total. The White House's formulation cannot therefore be treated as a verified joint definition. Likewise, a May American statement that China would review additional biotechnology traits supplies neither a list of approved events nor a set of new certificates.[^32]

These developments extend the chapter's earlier distinctions. Summit announcements set public tasks for officials. Work on products, values and operating rules sought to turn a broad understanding into a usable arrangement. Actual tariff modifications, purchases and committee operation would be further events requiring their own evidence. Treating every stage as the same completed agreement would reproduce the very confusion the earlier cases reveal.

The revision to Solomon is therefore neither the abandonment of his attention to implementation nor its indiscriminate confirmation. The continuing relationship plainly matters. It can sustain review, supply reasons to resist enforcement and create opportunities to bargain again. But performance varies across obligations within that relationship. China accelerated a published securities timetable; a firm obtained permission, full ownership and revenue. Biotechnological permissions eventually enabled identifiable commercial plans while procedural timing remained contested. Purchase commitments fell far short under more than one benchmark. The parties then negotiated new mechanisms while disputing the old ones.

The most useful question after signature is consequently concrete: what decision would turn this obligation into a benefit, who controls that decision, and what happened when it was requested? That question makes genuine implementation visible. It also makes obstruction testable, without inferring its motive from every delay or mistaking another ceremony for delivery.
