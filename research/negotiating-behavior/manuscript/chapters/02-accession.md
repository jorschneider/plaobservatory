# 2. The bargain that came in pieces

At his joint press conference with Bill Clinton on April 8, 1999, Zhu Rongji objected to the idea that he had brought America a gift. China had made concessions in its attempt to join the World Trade Organization, he said, but those concessions served Chinese interests. Competition would put pressure on domestic enterprises and help improve the economy. He turned the complaint into a joke about American campaign contributions. The laughter recorded in the transcript should not obscure the argument: access to China’s market was being exchanged for something its premier believed China needed.[^1]

Zhu and Clinton nevertheless described the remaining distance differently. Clinton announced substantial progress without a completed deal. Zhu portrayed the commercial gap as small and the political atmosphere as the real difficulty. They could agree that the negotiation was valuable while disagreeing about why it had not finished. The public disagreement preceded the retrospective accounts in which American officials allocated responsibility for the missed opportunity.

An offer could be worth accepting while some terms remained unsettled. The April disagreement concerned both the price of a bargain and Washington’s readiness to make it. The surviving papers let us separate those questions: which commitments had been accepted, which remained conditional, and who could authorize the final combination?

The prize was participation in a trading system whose members negotiated the terms on which China would enter. The American bilateral agreement was a major part of that work, but other members’ demands and the multilateral legal package remained. Washington also faced a domestic decision over permanent normal trade relations: the executive could bargain with China while Congress retained authority over that legislation. Those separate decisions help explain why an offer of market access, a presidential assurance and a completed accession were different things.[^13][^14]

## The April record

Two days after the press conference, Long Yongtu and Peter Scher signed a memorandum of understanding. Its body survives in an official Chinese documentary collection. It records a wide range of agreement on market access, including goods schedules and most services, subject to checking and correction. It also names unfinished business: banking and consumer auto finance, securities, audiovisual services, textiles and apparel, and the duration of provisions governing dumping and product-specific safeguards. The parties would verify their schedules and continue work on the outstanding issues.[^2]

The memorandum gave the next negotiation a substantial starting point while preserving specific unresolved demands. Its two attachments are missing from the reproduced record, so the signed body establishes more securely what remained open than the complete terms already recorded in its schedules.

The Chinese editorial account disputes publication of the American statement issued on April 8. That statement cannot stand in for the signed memorandum or the joint statement of April 10. Even the latter still called for an agreement on strong commercial terms while supporting accession in 1999. The political commitment to seek membership left its price to be settled.[^3]

The disagreement within the American record is revealing. At an April 8 briefing, US Trade Representative Charlene Barshefsky defended the remaining commercial issues and rejected the suggestion that publication was intended to mobilize business pressure. Gene Sperling acknowledged differences among advisers and referred to a combined National Economic Council–National Security Council memorandum recording their views. In her later oral history, Barshefsky placed much more emphasis on Washington’s political unwillingness to close. Lawrence Summers also criticized the April episode, but questioned the attempt to obtain public credit for concessions without completing the bargain.[^4]

The dispute involved three decisions: whether to accept the package, whether more could be obtained, and whether publishing its contents would help. The contemporary briefing identifies a decision paper that could clarify the competing recommendations. Without it, the later accounts cannot settle how Washington chose among them.

The public exchange between Zhu and Clinton still establishes something important. Reform and competition were already part of Zhu’s stated rationale. His willingness to discuss their costs does not reveal every Chinese official’s preference, but it prevents interpreting all market opening as a favor extracted from an otherwise indifferent China. Clinton’s case likewise combined commercial gains with an expectation that integration would influence China’s development. Negotiators were seeking a commercial bargain that their leaders valued partly for different political reasons.

## Returning to a disputed baseline

The bombing of China’s embassy in Belgrade interrupted accession talks. Messages and technical contacts continued during the suspension, according to the Chinese published history and American records, before the announced resumption at Auckland.[^5]

When talks returned, the status of the earlier papers was itself at issue. The Chinese history describes disputes over the April American list, newer Chinese positions, permanent normal trade relations, safeguards and export controls. It reports Barshefsky treating high-technology export controls as outside her WTO mandate. The sides were bargaining over which earlier positions still counted and which subjects belonged in the negotiation.

The Americans also had to demonstrate that they could complete their part of the process. Summers later described an October mission intended to reassure Zhu about Washington’s readiness. His contemporary speech confirms the visit and meetings, without independently confirming the private assurance he remembered. The distinction leaves the central problem intact: a Chinese negotiator had reason to care not only about the generosity of an American offer but also about whether the people delivering it could sustain it in Washington.[^6]

Personal familiarity could make such an assurance more credible, but its value depended on what the official represented. Confidence in Summers, Barshefsky or Sperling could matter through information about presidential backing and domestic coordination. The relevant question is whether an assurance changed the counterpart’s assessment or instructions; familiarity alone cannot answer it.

## November’s written exchange

By November 12, the surviving papers permit a much closer view. China’s package was detailed and explicitly conditional on completing a final bilateral agreement in the current negotiation. It treated different telecommunications services differently, assigned phase-ins to ownership ceilings, identified banking restrictions and proposed a short duration for special trade-remedy provisions. Its technical specificity was itself part of the bargaining: percentage ownership, location, customer, date and regulatory permission could be adjusted separately.[^7]

The American papers changed over the following exchange, while retaining earlier schedules wherever new terms did not supersede them. An item omitted from the shorter paper attributed to November 13 was therefore not automatically abandoned: some issues were restated, while others continued under the earlier terms.

Four issue areas illustrate the structure of the package. The last column below is the American government’s contemporaneous description of the November agreement, not a substitute for the missing bilateral annexes or the later final WTO schedule.[^8]

| Issue | Chinese proposal, November 12 | American closing papers | Public American account after agreement |
|---|---|---|---|
| Telecommunications | Value-added and paging: 49% foreign equity within one year; separate mobile and basic-service schedules | November 13 distinguished equity from operational control and proposed 50% in value-added/paging within two years | November 16 summary described 50% for value-added in two years and paging in three; categories and dates therefore require separate tracking |
| Life insurance | Up to 50% foreign equity on accession | November 12 already specified 50%; November 13 explicitly sought management control and additional opening | Summary retained 50% and described freedom to choose venture partners and changes in branching; it did not reproduce a complete management-control clause |
| Banking and auto finance | Specific geographic acceleration, no national-treatment commitment in this proposal; foreign nonbank auto finance conditional on comparable Chinese nonbank permission | November 12 used accession-relative two/five-year customer periods; November 13 specified deadlines no later than January 1, 2002/2005 and accepted a subsequent Chinese auto-finance offer | Summary returned to accession-relative two/five-year periods, described national treatment within designated areas, and nonbank auto finance on accession |
| Product safeguard and antidumping method | Special provisions to expire no later than three years after accession | Product safeguard changed from a proposed fifteen years to twelve; November 13 proposed twenty years for the special antidumping provision | Summary announced twelve years for the product safeguard and fifteen for the antidumping provision; these remained different instruments |

A government could preserve an ownership ceiling while yielding on managerial rights, or shorten a transition in one sector while retaining protection in another. The papers expose the choices available to negotiators. They also show why a tally of concessions would miss the problem each delegation faced: finding a combination it could accept. The documents trace changes in the package without disclosing every trade between its parts.

The ownership histories are especially easy to confuse. Fifty percent life-insurance equity was already in both sides’ November 12 papers; the American closing papers inspected here do not demand 51%. Barshefsky’s later recollection of an ownership retreat concerns telecommunications. The American April public summary had described 51% after four years for value-added and paging services. Each sector had its own sequence of ownership, control and transition proposals.[^9]

The banking clocks also changed form. A deadline fixed to January 1, 2002 or 2005 differs from a period measured two or five years after accession. The practical difference grows if membership occurs later than negotiators anticipate. The November 13 offer and the post-agreement summary therefore cannot be harmonized simply because both mention two classes of customers. This is a documented difference in the terms’ public presentation; the bilateral annexes are needed to establish the complete final wording.

The trade-remedy gap was large. China proposed ending the special provisions no later than three years after accession; the American November 13 paper proposed twenty years for the antidumping provision. The post-agreement summary announced fifteen, alongside a separate twelve-year product safeguard. The periods moved, and they governed different instruments. The papers do not identify the private exchange that produced the final combination.

## Who moved the deadline?

The letters accompanying the offers make the American delegation an active participant in the management of time. On November 12, chargé d’affaires William C. McCahill Jr. described an impasse and requested direct talks between Barshefsky and Zhu. The letter set out the eight o’clock hotel departure and seven o’clock deadline for a reply. It offered an extension if a meeting could occur, but said the delegation could not remain beyond early Sunday morning. On November 14, a further letter again requested Zhu’s intervention and alleged that the Chinese side had reopened settled issues.[^10]

The Americans were trying to change both the clock and the level of decision. Their departure deadline could move if Zhu agreed to meet. Invoking the leaders’ intentions cast the remaining obstruction as inconsistent with a shared objective; accusing China of reopening issues asserted which concessions should already count as settled.

The Chinese published chronology places Zhu’s interventions on November 13 and 15. A financial negotiator’s later account describes changes in authority during the same period. American pressure may have helped bring a decision forward, but the sequence does not show how Chinese instructions changed in response to the departure threat. A deadline could press officials to decide without supplying the sector approvals needed for a bargain.

Solomon called senior intervention the most reliable sign of approaching agreement and connected it to leaders’ reluctance to risk face. This successful sequence cannot establish or overturn that comparative prediction. It permits a more concrete inquiry: what did the intervention enable or confirm? The letters show a frustrated counterpart requesting a higher decision on an elaborate draft. The financial negotiations reveal what some of those decisions involved.

## What sector authority adds

Financial negotiator Deng Hongguo’s account offers a specific institutional explanation. Foreign requests moved through the Geneva delegation and MOFTEC, the foreign-trade ministry, to the responsible regulator. Sector officials prepared alternatives and sought approval through their own hierarchy; MOFTEC coordinated the broader package. Deng describes new State Council authorization on November 13 and a separate request to central bank governor Dai Xianglong over financial leasing that evening. His account distinguishes ordinary negotiating discretion from concessions that required a higher decision.[^11]

On this retrospective account, technical understanding and negotiating permission were separate. A sector official could know exactly what a foreign request entailed while still needing approval to accept it; MOFTEC then had to fit the concession into the broader package.

Auto finance makes the proposed mechanism concrete. The November 12 Chinese offer made foreign nonbank access conditional on corresponding domestic permission. Deng recalls new authority the next day allowing foreign enterprise-group finance companies to offer auto credit on accession. The American November 13 paper then expressly accepts a Chinese auto-finance offer dated that day, and the November 16 summary announces nonbank access on accession. These sources converge on movement from a domestic-permission condition toward accession-linked access. They do not supply the full intervening Chinese offer or the instruction itself, and Deng’s narrower company category should not silently become the summary’s broader description. The change is better established than its complete authorization chain.[^15]

For a visitor facing repeated refusals, the distinction matters. An appeal to a premier may unlock a decision without establishing that a subordinate bargained in bad faith. A claim of limited authority can also be useful tactically. Deng’s account points to authority over particular choices—cities, customers, permissions—rather than the rank of the person in the most prominent chair. The offers document those choices; the internal instructions would establish who could make them.

Washington faced its own problem of credible authority. After April, a Chinese negotiator had reason to seek assurances about American readiness as well as American terms. The governments had different decision systems, but each needed confidence that the other could sustain an external commitment at home.

## The signature and the bargain beyond it

On November 15, Shi Guangsheng and Barshefsky signed the agreement on market access. The Chinese and English bodies reproduced in the collection say that commitments on industrial goods, agriculture and services were set out in Annexes 1–3; provisions applying rules to access to each other’s markets appeared in Annex 4. Both bodies direct the governments to notify the WTO director-general and send the agreement, including its annexes, to the secretariat for incorporation into the multilateral aspects of China’s accession negotiations.[^12]

The conditional package had become a bilateral agreement. Its reference to both markets establishes the reciprocal scope of the instrument, while the annexes contain the full allocation of obligations. Signature also opened the next stage: incorporating the bargain into the accession package.

The WTO’s announcement that day was explicit about the unfinished work. Director-General Mike Moore welcomed the bilateral deal while identifying remaining agreements with other governments and technical protocol negotiations. China would attend the forthcoming Seattle ministerial as an observer. His statement supplies an institutional perspective distinct from either delegation’s account of victory.[^13]

The American domestic process remained separate as well. Briefing reporters in Ankara, National Security Adviser Sandy Berger said the administration would seek permanent normal trade relations from Congress. He distinguished that legislation from approval of accession itself, and explained that China understood Congress to be an independent branch. The administration could promise an effort; it could not simply announce the congressional result as a completed part of the November bargain.[^14]

Berger’s case for accepting the bargain included improvements since April in trade remedies, motion pictures, Internet access, satellites, auto financing, securities and capital-market dialogue. He also expected competition to reinforce Chinese reform. Asked what China had gained, he left the Chinese side to answer. Zhu’s rejection of the gift metaphor remained pertinent: both governments valued a bargain whose domestic costs and hoped-for political consequences they assessed differently.

For Europe, the completed American package settled neither the price nor the distribution of access it wanted. The next negotiation began with a dispute over how much the first one had already accomplished.

## Europe’s unfinished business

At the January 24–26, 2000 talks, European chief negotiator Hans-Friedrich Beseler invoked most-favored-nation treatment and proposed building on the US bargain, according to the Chinese documentary history. He also explained why that bargain was insufficient. The European Union had fifteen member states with separate interests; satisfying their priorities required a balance of its own. Long Yongtu replied that the American package already included concessions of interest to Europe, and that Chinese leaders had compensated for protecting sensitive sectors by conceding more elsewhere.[^16]

For Beseler, the American agreement was a basis for comparable treatment and further gains. For Long, it explained why some demands approached decisions China would not readily reopen. Europe accepted a starting point, not a sufficient outcome. Its negotiators faced the same Chinese leadership, but with different priorities, an inherited package and fifteen member states whose support the Commission needed to sustain.

A European technical package dated February 23 made those priorities tangible. It proposed opening Zhuhai to renminbi banking on accession and dropping a request to advance Beijing’s opening. Its services page separated items for resolution by chief negotiators from unresolved telecommunications and insurance questions. This was a proposed package, including a division of work between negotiating levels.[^17]

The eventual WTO schedule required Zhuhai to open within one year after accession. Europe had requested immediate opening; China ultimately bound a one-year timetable. Deng Hongguo recalls a prior three-year benchmark, a Portuguese request to accelerate it and permission from central bank leaders to accept the change. The final one-year term is independently documented, and the Commission credited Europe with advancing it. The earlier benchmark and approval chain depend on Deng’s recollection.[^18]

## A premier’s meeting that did not close the deal

European Trade Commissioner Pascal Lamy’s March visit supplies the unsuccessful comparison missing from a story told only through November’s American success. Zhu met him, detailed packages were exchanged, and no agreement followed. According to the Chinese history, Lamy explained that his authorization did not permit acceptance of the Chinese ownership ceilings in life insurance and automobiles. He would need to consult at home. The European side had an approval problem too.[^19]

By May, the same account reports, Lamy had authority to accept the 50 percent life-insurance ceiling if compensated elsewhere in insurance. The automobile ceiling could also be accepted with improvements in tariff treatment. Mobile telecommunications remained unresolved. Its final ownership percentage, initial percentage and transition period offered separate ways of changing the bargain. A Chinese refusal to lift one ceiling did not mean every commercially important term was fixed.

The account of May 17–19 is particularly revealing. Lamy said he had understood Zhu’s March remarks to permit a 50 percent mobile ceiling, and had reported that understanding to European ministers. Accepting 49 percent exceeded his authority. On May 18 he sought a signal from a Chinese leader confirming that limit, which he could report to Brussels to obtain fresh authority. Meanwhile, disputes continued over initial mobile ownership, retail chains and insurance licenses. Shi Guangsheng agreed to consult other departments, while warning that an improved offer was unlikely. The following morning he reported that the coordination had not succeeded in improving it.[^20]

At his May 19 meeting with Lamy, Zhu described 49 percent as a collective Chinese government decision. Lamy reported consultations with member states and industry and said that, with Zhu’s confirmation, Europe would respect the limit. He sought approval of compensating terms in telecommunications, insurance and distribution. Zhu accepted their principle, leaving the particulars to the negotiators. Agreement followed that afternoon.

Lamy had requested authoritative confirmation of a limit, and Zhu supplied it. That could help Lamy justify accepting a package to the institutions controlling his mandate. The Chinese account does not establish when European authorization changed or whether the meeting caused the change. It does show that the requested intervention concerned confirmation of a Chinese constraint, alongside approval in principle of compensation elsewhere.

Shi’s unsuccessful coordination likewise identifies a result without exposing its internal cause: the offer did not improve. A firm collective decision or disagreement over compensation could look much like leadership division from outside. March and May leave the general frequency of such causes unresolved. They show why the content of authority matters on both sides: a premier could meet his counterpart while a bargain remained unapproved, then confirm a constraint around which the parties could settle.

## What the European bargain bought

The final mobile schedule retained 49 percent, reached within three years after accession, with 25 percent at accession and 35 percent within a year. Geographic restrictions followed a separate five-year timetable. Those are distinct commitments, not rival descriptions of one transition. The Commission characterized the equity phase-in as an improvement on the US agreement. The November American public summary confirms the ceiling without reproducing its complete equity timetable, so the missing US annex remains necessary for a fully authenticated bilateral comparison.[^21]

In distribution, the scope of the concession mattered as much as its announcement. The Commission’s account said the restriction on large foreign-owned chains had been lifted. The final schedule retained a restriction on foreign majority ownership for certain chains with more than thirty outlets selling specified products of different types and brands from multiple suppliers. The change could have substantial commercial value while stopping short of unrestricted foreign ownership of every large chain. The definition of the remaining exception was part of the bargain.[^22]

Some European benefits also sat outside the accession terms. The Commission recorded seven new insurance licenses—five life and two non-life—and two sub-branch licenses to be granted before accession. Its October 2000 memorandum was still seeking timing for five further new licenses and two sub-branches. The counts concern different stages and categories. They identify promised administrative acts whose completion must be traced separately from the signature and the WTO schedule.[^23]

That October memorandum also described continued multilateral bargaining over how China would give effect to its commitments. Existing members saw clarification as necessary; the Commission reported China accusing them of seeking obligations beyond those normally required of an acceding country. That disagreement concerned the appropriate burden of accession. The memorandum alone does not establish a dispute over the meaning of an already settled bilateral clause. The transition from bilateral agreement to multilateral rules was itself a negotiation.

Accession brought detailed written bargaining, sector expertise, personal assurance and senior authority together. Officials on both sides invoked prior agreements and domestic constraints. China defended important ownership limits while bargaining over timing, geography, control and administrative permission. A premier’s confirmation could form part of closing a package while preserving a contested ceiling; signature could settle a bilateral bargain while leaving other governments and multilateral terms to be addressed.

Solomon’s questions about authority and bargaining after agreement remain useful here. Their answers lie in particular decisions and commitments, not in a general negotiating atmosphere. Technical expertise was already present before accession. The next chapter follows its use in a different setting, where Chinese officials could contest the interpretation and application of WTO rules.
