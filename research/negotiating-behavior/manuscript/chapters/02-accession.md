# Chapter 2. The bargain that came in pieces

*First manuscript draft, 15 September 2026. Includes the US–EU comparison and selected final-schedule checks. Missing bilateral annexes and internal instructions still limit the reconstruction.*

At his joint press conference with Bill Clinton on April 8, 1999, Zhu Rongji objected to the idea that he had brought America a gift. China had made concessions in its attempt to join the World Trade Organization, he said, but those concessions served Chinese interests. Competition would put pressure on domestic enterprises and help improve the economy. He turned the complaint into a joke about American campaign contributions. The laughter recorded in the transcript should not obscure the argument: access to China’s market was being exchanged for something its premier believed China needed.[^1]

Zhu and Clinton nevertheless described the remaining distance differently. Clinton announced substantial progress without a completed deal. Zhu portrayed the commercial gap as small and the political atmosphere as the real difficulty. They could agree that the negotiation was valuable while disagreeing about why it had not finished. The public disagreement preceded the retrospective accounts in which American officials allocated responsibility for the missed opportunity.

That distinction is essential to the history. A proposition that China’s offer was good enough to accept differs from a proposition that all its terms had been settled. An American decision not to close could be politically unwise even if the document still contained reservations. Conversely, saying that issues remained open could defend a decision whose decisive cause lay elsewhere. The papers permit a more exact question than whether April produced “a deal”: which commitments had been accepted, which remained conditional, and who could authorize the final combination?

The prize was participation in a trading system whose members negotiated the terms on which China would enter. The American bilateral agreement was a major part of that work, but other members’ demands and the multilateral legal package remained. Washington also faced a domestic decision over permanent normal trade relations: the executive could bargain with China while Congress retained authority over that legislation. Those separate decisions help explain why an offer of market access, a presidential assurance and a completed accession were different things.[^13][^14]

## The April record

Two days after the press conference, Long Yongtu and Peter Scher signed a memorandum of understanding. Its body survives in an official Chinese documentary collection. It records a wide range of agreement on market access, including goods schedules and most services, subject to checking and correction. It also names unfinished business: banking and consumer auto finance, securities, audiovisual services, textiles and apparel, and the duration of provisions governing dumping and product-specific safeguards. The parties would verify their schedules and continue work on the outstanding issues.[^2]

The memorandum therefore preserved both progress and limits. It was not an empty promise to talk. Nor was it an unconditional completion of the whole accession bargain. Its references to attachments matter because the body cannot answer every question about the sectoral commitments it records. The two attachments are not reproduced with the signed page. Without them, the historian cannot turn a government’s public summary into the authenticated April schedule.

There is a second reason to distinguish texts. The American statement published on April 8 and the joint statement of April 10 are not interchangeable simply because each presented progress as joint. The Chinese editorial account disputes the publication of the earlier formulation; the later signed memorandum supplies a firmer basis for identifying what the negotiators formally recorded. The April 10 public statement still called for an agreement on strong commercial terms, even while expressing support for accession in 1999. A commitment to work toward membership did not remove the question of its price.[^3]

The disagreement within the American record is revealing. At an April 8 briefing, Barshefsky defended the remaining commercial issues and rejected the suggestion that publication was intended to mobilize business pressure. Gene Sperling acknowledged differences among advisers and referred to a combined National Economic Council–National Security Council memorandum recording their views. In her later oral history, Barshefsky placed much more emphasis on Washington’s political unwillingness to close. Lawrence Summers also criticized the April episode, but questioned the attempt to obtain public credit for concessions without completing the bargain.[^4]

These accounts identify competing decisions rather than resolve them: whether the package should have been accepted, whether more could be obtained, and whether publishing its contents helped or damaged the effort. The contemporary briefing gives the missing decision paper a name. The later interviews give reasons to seek it. Neither permits the author to narrate an unobserved vote inside the White House.

The public exchange between Zhu and Clinton still establishes something important. Reform and competition were already part of Zhu’s stated rationale. His willingness to discuss their costs does not reveal every Chinese official’s preference, but it prevents interpreting all market opening as a favor extracted from an otherwise indifferent China. Clinton’s case likewise combined commercial gains with an expectation that integration would influence China’s development. Negotiators were seeking a commercial bargain that their leaders valued partly for different political reasons.

## Returning to a disputed baseline

The interruption after the bombing of China’s embassy in Belgrade further complicates any simple April-to-November comparison. The Chinese published history records a suspension of accession talks and subsequent efforts to resume them. That suspension should not be enlarged into an assertion that the countries had no communication at all. The American record includes later contacts, and the Chinese account itself describes exchanged messages and technical contact before the announced resumption at Auckland.[^5]

When talks returned, the problem included the status of the earlier papers. The Chinese history describes disputes over the April American list, newer Chinese positions, permanent normal trade relations, safeguards and export controls. It reports Barshefsky treating high-technology export controls as outside her WTO mandate. This is a useful account of contested scope, though the underlying exchanges and authorization records are not all available. It shows why an apparent refusal to repeat an earlier position cannot be classified before establishing whether that position was agreed, provisional or attributed by one side.

The Americans also had to demonstrate that they could complete their part of the process. Summers later described an October mission intended to reassure Zhu about Washington’s readiness. His contemporary speech confirms the visit and meetings, without independently confirming the private assurance he remembered. The distinction leaves the central problem intact: a Chinese negotiator had reason to care not only about the generosity of an American offer but also about whether the people delivering it could sustain it in Washington.[^6]

This is where the personal and institutional explanations meet. An official known to a counterpart can transmit an assurance. Its value still depends on what the official represents. Familiarity with Barshefsky, confidence in Summers or Sperling’s participation could matter through information about presidential backing and domestic coordination. It is not enough to call those encounters friendship and infer a concession. The resulting instruction or response has to be found.

## November’s written exchange

By November 12, the surviving papers permit a much closer view. China’s package was detailed and explicitly conditional on completing a final bilateral agreement in the current negotiation. It treated different telecommunications services differently, assigned phase-ins to ownership ceilings, identified banking restrictions and proposed a short duration for special trade-remedy provisions. Its technical specificity was itself part of the bargaining: percentage ownership, location, customer, date and regulatory permission could be adjusted separately.[^7]

The American papers show changes over the following exchange. Both the November 12 paper and the one attributed by its Chinese heading to November 13 retained earlier schedules except where new terms superseded them. An omitted item in the later, shorter list was therefore not automatically abandoned. Some issues were restated; others continued under the earlier paper. A reconstruction that compared only the visible numbered lists would manufacture changes that their retention clauses expressly ruled out.

Four issue areas illustrate the structure of the package. The last column below is the American government’s contemporaneous description of the November agreement, not a substitute for the missing bilateral annexes or the later final WTO schedule.[^8]

| Issue | Chinese proposal, November 12 | American closing papers | Public American account after agreement |
|---|---|---|---|
| Telecommunications | Value-added and paging: 49% foreign equity within one year; separate mobile and basic-service schedules | November 13 distinguished equity from operational control and proposed 50% in value-added/paging within two years | November 16 summary described 50% for value-added in two years and paging in three; categories and dates therefore require separate tracking |
| Life insurance | Up to 50% foreign equity on accession | November 12 already specified 50%; November 13 explicitly sought management control and additional opening | Summary retained 50% and described freedom to choose venture partners and changes in branching; it did not reproduce a complete management-control clause |
| Banking and auto finance | Specific geographic acceleration, no national-treatment commitment in this proposal; foreign nonbank auto finance conditional on comparable Chinese nonbank permission | November 12 used accession-relative two/five-year customer periods; November 13 specified deadlines no later than January 1, 2002/2005 and accepted a subsequent Chinese auto-finance offer | Summary returned to accession-relative two/five-year periods, described national treatment within designated areas, and nonbank auto finance on accession |
| Product safeguard and antidumping method | Special provisions to expire no later than three years after accession | Product safeguard changed from a proposed fifteen years to twelve; November 13 proposed twenty years for the special antidumping provision | Summary announced twelve years for the product safeguard and fifteen for the antidumping provision; these remained different instruments |

The table changes the meaning of the endgame. It shows bargaining over combinations, not a single scale running from toughness to generosity. A government could preserve an ownership ceiling and yield on managerial rights. It could shorten a transition in one sector while retaining protection in another. It could accept a longer safeguard period in exchange for a different commercial package. Establishing the actual exchange requires more than observing that both changes eventually occurred, but the papers identify the separate margins on which a bargain could be made.

The documents establish separate ownership histories. Fifty percent life-insurance equity was already in both sides’ November 12 papers; the inspected American closing papers do not demand 51% life-insurance ownership. Barshefsky’s later recollection of an ownership retreat concerns telecommunications. The insurance chronology does not contradict that telecommunications recollection. The American April public summary did describe 51% after four years for value-added and paging services, a different sector and timing formula from the November papers. A sound comparison must follow each of those provisions through its own sequence, including control and transition dates, before evaluating a participant’s account.[^9]

The banking clocks also changed form. A deadline fixed to January 1, 2002 or 2005 differs from a period measured two or five years after accession. The practical difference grows if membership occurs later than negotiators anticipate. The November 13 offer and the post-agreement summary therefore cannot be harmonized simply because both mention two classes of customers. This is a documented difference in the terms’ public presentation; the bilateral annexes are needed to establish the complete final wording.

The distinction between proposed and announced trade-remedy periods is equally consequential. China’s November 12 proposal was much shorter than the American demands. The American November 13 paper proposed twenty years for the antidumping provision; the post-agreement summary announced fifteen. The twelve-year product safeguard was a different provision. Later disputes over the meaning and expiration of China’s accession commitments cannot be resolved by substituting one of these figures for another. Here the documents establish that the proposed terms moved and that a public account described a different final combination. They do not identify the private conversation that produced it.

## Who moved the deadline?

The letters accompanying the offers make the American delegation an active participant in the management of time. On November 12, chargé d’affaires William C. McCahill Jr. described an impasse and requested direct talks between Barshefsky and Zhu. The letter set out the eight o’clock hotel departure and seven o’clock deadline for a reply. It offered an extension if a meeting could occur, but said the delegation could not remain beyond early Sunday morning. On November 14, a further letter again requested Zhu’s intervention and alleged that the Chinese side had reopened settled issues.[^10]

Each element had a purpose in the argument the Americans were making. The announced departure presented a time limit. An offer to postpone demonstrated that the limit could change under a specified condition. Invoking the intentions of leaders framed the remaining obstruction as inconsistent with a shared objective. Accusing the other side of reopening issues sought to establish which concessions belonged in the existing baseline. The documents record these moves directly. Their causal success is a separate question.

The Chinese published chronology places Zhu’s interventions on November 13 and 15. A financial negotiator’s later account describes changes in authority during the same period. The timing is compatible with American pressure helping to force a decision. It is also compatible with a process of internal authorization and technical resolution that a departure threat alone could not replace. To decide between these explanations, the historian would need to see how the instructions or acceptable terms changed in response to the threatened departure. The fact that an agreement followed cannot supply that missing link.

This qualification does not make the letters less valuable. They overturn an account in which the Chinese side alone controlled the clock while the Americans merely endured it. They show the Americans trying to alter both the schedule and the level of decision. They also show why a visible summit intervention is an incomplete diagnostic: it can be sought by the frustrated counterpart, depend on revised instructions, and concern a specific unresolved part of an otherwise elaborate draft.

Solomon called senior intervention the most reliable sign of approaching agreement and connected it to leaders’ reluctance to risk face. A narrower authorization hypothesis is worth investigating here: what did the senior intervention enable or confirm? This is our qualification of his stronger prediction, which already sits beside an aborted claims-settlement example in his book. They do not establish that Chinese officials staged deadlock according to a predetermined script. Nor do they measure the reliability of the signal in negotiations that did not close. A successful episode can demonstrate a sequence without proving a forecasting rule.

## What sector authority adds

Deng Hongguo’s account of the financial negotiations offers a specific institutional explanation. Foreign requests moved through the Geneva delegation and MOFTEC to the responsible regulator. Sector officials prepared alternatives and sought approval through their own hierarchy; MOFTEC coordinated the broader package. Deng describes new State Council authorization on November 13 and a separate request to PBOC Governor Dai Xianglong over financial leasing that evening. His account distinguishes ordinary negotiating discretion from concessions that required a higher decision.[^11]

It is retrospective testimony, with apparent date and institutional inconsistencies elsewhere in the chapter. The instructions, if recovered, would be stronger evidence. Its value is the precise mechanism it proposes: technical officials could understand a request while lacking permission to accept it, and the delegation could need both sector approval and coordination across a package.

Auto finance makes the proposed mechanism concrete. The November 12 Chinese offer made foreign nonbank access conditional on corresponding domestic permission. Deng recalls new authority the next day allowing foreign enterprise-group finance companies to offer auto credit on accession. The American November 13 paper then expressly accepts a Chinese auto-finance offer dated that day, and the November 16 summary announces nonbank access on accession. These sources converge on movement from a domestic-permission condition toward accession-linked access. They do not supply the full intervening Chinese offer or the instruction itself, and Deng’s narrower company category should not silently become the summary’s broader description. The change is better established than its complete authorization chain.[^15]

The document sequence is consistent with that explanation without proving all of it. The Chinese proposal specified matters a financial regulator would care about: customer categories, cities, national treatment and the relationship between foreign and domestic permissions. Subsequent American language accepted some changes while pressing others. On Deng’s account, authority was attached to particular choices, not simply to the rank of whoever occupied the most prominent chair. The detailed offers fit that account but do not independently establish the delegation of authority.

This changes how a visitor should interpret rigidity. Repeating an instruction can be a tactic, but it can also be what a delegated official is empowered to do. An appeal to a premier may unlock a decision without demonstrating that a subordinate acted in bad faith. Conversely, a claim of limited authority can itself be useful in bargaining. The decisive evidence is the instruction and its revision, not the outsider’s impression that the official seemed either sincere or obstructive.

The same reasoning applies to the United States. The April episode and the later emphasis on Washington’s readiness show that an American negotiator’s authority could also be uncertain from the other side of the table. A comparison that assumes American offers were always stable would misidentify some Chinese caution as an unexplained national habit. The two governments had different systems of decision, but both faced the problem of making an external commitment credible within them.

## The signature and the bargain beyond it

On November 15, Shi Guangsheng and Barshefsky signed the agreement on market access. The Chinese and English bodies reproduced in the collection say that commitments on industrial goods, agriculture and services were set out in Annexes 1–3; provisions applying rules to access to each other’s markets appeared in Annex 4. Both bodies direct the governments to notify the WTO director-general and send the agreement, including its annexes, to the secretariat for incorporation into the multilateral aspects of China’s accession negotiations.[^12]

This is a different documentary status from the conditional November 12 package. It records that the governments had reached agreement. Yet it is still not a declaration that China had become a WTO member. The signed instrument itself points to the next process. Its reference to rules concerning both markets identifies the scope of the instrument, not the distribution of new concessions. The body establishes the reciprocal formulation; the full allocation of obligations lies in the annexes.

The WTO’s announcement that day was explicit about the unfinished work. Director-General Mike Moore welcomed the bilateral deal while identifying remaining agreements with other governments and technical protocol negotiations. China would attend the forthcoming Seattle ministerial as an observer. His statement supplies an institutional perspective distinct from either delegation’s account of victory.[^13]

The American domestic process remained separate as well. Briefing reporters in Ankara, National Security Adviser Sandy Berger said the administration would seek permanent normal trade relations from Congress. He distinguished that legislation from approval of accession itself, and explained that China understood Congress to be an independent branch. The administration could promise an effort; it could not simply announce the congressional result as a completed part of the November bargain.[^14]

Berger also identified what he believed had improved since April: trade remedies, motion pictures, Internet access, satellites, auto financing, securities and capital-market dialogue. Asked what China had gained, he declined to give a balancing account and left the Chinese side to speak for itself. His statement is useful evidence of the administration’s case for its decision, including its expectations that competition would reinforce internal reform. It is not an impartial measure of the value each government obtained.

The record thus returns us to Zhu’s rejection of the gift metaphor. Both governments could value a bargain that imposed visible costs on China. They could also disagree about the remaining price, the meaning of an earlier document, or their counterpart’s ability to follow through. Political expectations helped make an accession agreement attractive; they did not settle each clause or guarantee the long-run development each side hoped to encourage.

## Europe’s unfinished business

The American agreement gave Europe something to build on. In the Chinese documentary history’s account of the January 24–26, 2000 talks, European chief negotiator Hans-Friedrich Beseler invoked most-favored-nation treatment and proposed continuing on the basis of the US bargain. He also explained why that bargain was insufficient. The European Union had fifteen member states with separate interests; satisfying their priorities required a balance of its own. Long Yongtu replied that the American package already included concessions of interest to Europe, and that Chinese leaders had compensated for protecting sensitive sectors by conceding more elsewhere.[^16]

The same earlier agreement was doing different work in the two arguments. For Beseler, it established a basis for comparable treatment and further gains. For Long, it helped explain why some demands approached decisions China would not readily reopen. Neither position required denying that a previous bargain existed. A later Chinese participant’s shorthand that Europe would not use the American agreement as its basis misses this distinction between accepting a starting point and accepting it as sufficient.

The European comparison is therefore unusually close without being a controlled experiment. It involves the same Chinese leadership and accession campaign, but Europe bargained later, with different commercial priorities and an existing American package. Moreover, the Commission had to negotiate a position it could sustain among member states. A different result could reflect any of these differences. It cannot simply measure which foreign negotiators understood China better.

One surviving paper makes the remaining work tangible. A European technical package dated February 23 proposed opening Zhuhai to renminbi banking on accession and dropping a request to advance Beijing’s opening. Its services page separated items intended for resolution by chief negotiators from unresolved telecommunications and insurance questions. The handwriting and checked lines do not identify a Chinese acceptance. The paper is evidence of a proposed package, including the effort to settle which issues belonged at which level.[^17]

The eventual WTO schedule committed China to opening Zhuhai within one year after accession. This is a recoverable request-to-outcome comparison: immediate opening was requested; a one-year timetable was ultimately bound. Deng Hongguo’s later account adds that the earlier benchmark was three years, that Portugal wanted the change, and that he obtained permission from PBOC leaders. The final one-year term is independently documented; the starting three-year benchmark and the approval chain still depend on his recollection. The Commission also credited Europe with advancing Zhuhai’s date. These records support a specific geographic concession, while leaving its complete internal history to be established.[^18]

## A premier’s meeting that did not close the deal

Lamy’s March visit supplies the unsuccessful comparison missing from a story told only through November’s American success. Zhu met the European commissioner, detailed packages were exchanged, and no agreement followed. According to the Chinese history, Lamy explained that his authorization did not permit acceptance of the Chinese ownership ceilings in life insurance and automobiles. He would need to consult at home. The European side had an approval problem too.[^19]

By May, the same account reports, Lamy had authority to accept the 50 percent life-insurance ceiling if compensated elsewhere in insurance. The automobile ceiling could also be accepted with improvements in tariff treatment. Mobile telecommunications remained unresolved. Its final ownership percentage, initial percentage and transition period offered separate ways of changing the bargain. A Chinese refusal to lift one ceiling did not mean every commercially important term was fixed.

The account of May 17–19 is particularly revealing. Lamy said he had understood Zhu’s March remarks to permit a 50 percent mobile ceiling, and had reported that understanding to European ministers. Accepting 49 percent exceeded his authority. On May 18 he sought a signal from a Chinese leader confirming that limit, which he could report to Brussels to obtain fresh authority. Meanwhile, disputes continued over initial mobile ownership, retail chains and insurance licenses. Shi Guangsheng agreed to consult other departments, while warning that an improved offer was unlikely. The following morning he reported that the coordination had not succeeded in improving it.[^20]

At his May 19 meeting with Lamy, Zhu described 49 percent as a collective Chinese government decision. Lamy reported consultations with member states and industry and said that, with Zhu’s confirmation, Europe would respect the limit. He sought approval of compensating terms in telecommunications, insurance and distribution. Zhu accepted their principle, leaving the particulars to the negotiators. Agreement followed that afternoon.

Here senior intervention did more than display a willingness to settle. The counterpart had explicitly requested authoritative confirmation of a limit. Such confirmation could help a negotiator justify accepting a package to the institutions that controlled his mandate. That is a plausible mechanism in this sequence, not an independently recovered Brussels decision: the published Chinese account does not establish precisely when European authorization changed or whether Zhu’s meeting caused the change. It does establish why treating every senior meeting as the release of a new Chinese concession would misread what was being requested.

Nor does Shi’s unsuccessful coordination establish that Chinese leaders disagreed with one another. It records a failed attempt to improve an offer. A decision to preserve an agreed limit, a disagreement over compensation and a divided leadership could each produce outward rigidity. The observed refusal alone cannot choose among them. March and May instead direct attention to the content of authority on both sides: a leader could be present while a bargain remained unapproved, and confirmation of a constraint could contribute to agreement without removing it.

## What the European bargain bought

The final mobile schedule retained 49 percent, reached within three years after accession, with 25 percent at accession and 35 percent within a year. Geographic restrictions followed a separate five-year timetable. Those are distinct commitments, not rival descriptions of one transition. The Commission characterized the equity phase-in as an improvement on the US agreement. The November American public summary confirms the ceiling without reproducing its complete equity timetable, so the missing US annex remains necessary for a fully authenticated bilateral comparison.[^21]

Distribution illustrates another danger in describing a concession too broadly. The Commission’s account said the restriction on large foreign-owned chains had been lifted. The final schedule retained a restriction on foreign majority ownership for certain chains with more than thirty outlets selling specified products of different types and brands from multiple suppliers. The change could have substantial commercial value while stopping short of unrestricted foreign ownership of every large chain. The definition of the remaining exception was part of the bargain.[^22]

Some European benefits also sat outside the accession terms. The Commission recorded seven new insurance licenses—five life and two non-life—and two sub-branch licenses to be granted before accession. Its October 2000 memorandum was still seeking timing for five further new licenses and two sub-branches. The counts concern different stages and categories. They identify promised administrative acts whose completion must be traced separately from the signature and the WTO schedule.[^23]

That October memorandum also described continued multilateral bargaining over how China would give effect to its commitments. Existing members saw clarification as necessary; the Commission reported China accusing them of seeking obligations beyond those normally required of an acceding country. That disagreement concerned the appropriate burden of accession. The memorandum alone does not establish a dispute over the meaning of an already settled bilateral clause. The transition from bilateral agreement to multilateral rules was itself a negotiation.

The American and European records thus establish a demanding coexistence. Detailed written bargaining, sector expertise, personal assurance and senior authority were already present together at accession. Officials on both sides invoked prior agreements and domestic constraints. The Chinese delegation defended important ownership limits while bargaining over timing, geography, control and administrative permission. The result was neither a single concession nor a single moment when politics gave way to implementation.

For the later history, the question is how this combination developed. Solomon’s attention to authority and bargaining after agreement remains productive, provided those processes are reconstructed rather than inferred from a negotiating atmosphere. Institutional expertise and technical precision cannot simply be announced as discoveries of the post-accession period. What changed must be shown in the choices officials could make, the instruments they used, and the commitments their counterparts could actually obtain.
