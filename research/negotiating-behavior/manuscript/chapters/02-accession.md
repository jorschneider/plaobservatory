# 2. The bargain that came in pieces

On March 30, 1999, US Trade Representative Charlene Barshefsky and State Councillor Wu Yi disagreed about whose applause a trade agreement needed. Premier Zhu Rongji was about to visit the United States, and China wanted an agreement on the terms of its entry into the World Trade Organization. In the Chinese published account of their meeting in Beijing, Barshefsky explained that a strong package had to command support in a hostile American political atmosphere. Wu replied that American businesses and farmers would applaud the concessions already available; opponents of China and of Clinton’s China policy never would. Barshefsky agreed that the extremes were unreachable. The problem was the middle, whose support was eroding. Business leaders, she complained, said different things to Chinese officials, American officials and members of Congress.[^1]

Wu was inviting Barshefsky to stop chasing people who could never be satisfied. Barshefsky was asking for terms that would hold together a constituency large enough to matter. Their disagreement was about the political value of additional concessions. If the existing offer was already enough to win business support, further concessions would buy China little. If it was not, declaring the commercial bargain complete would leave the administration’s domestic problem unresolved.

The Chinese account records Barshefsky making the same argument to Zhu through the composition of the package. Market access alone would not do. The accession protocol, agricultural sanitary arrangements and textiles all belonged in the bargain. A smaller agreement could lose the very interests the administration needed. Zhu answered that the decision had become political and that China could survive without joining. The two sides were testing whether the other government wanted accession enough to pay its domestic price.

Barshefsky’s later recollection adds a warning to that exchange. She says she told Zhu before his visit that even an agreement with her might be refused by Clinton. Her purpose, she recalled, was to avoid leaving him feeling deceived and to preserve the negotiation if Washington declined to finish. That warning separated her ability to negotiate from the president’s readiness to accept.[^2]

## An offer without an agreement

At their April 8 press conference, Zhu and President Bill Clinton made their different assessments public. Clinton announced substantial progress, with work still to do. Zhu said the gap was small and the political atmosphere was the real obstacle. He also rejected the suggestion, circulating in the Hong Kong press, that he had brought America a gift. Opening China would expose its enterprises to competition, he acknowledged, but that pressure would serve Chinese reform. He joked that calling it a gift might get Clinton into trouble over campaign contributions.[^3]

The joke carried a serious defense of the price Zhu was prepared to pay. He was presenting exposure to foreign competition as a Chinese benefit, including when Chinese enterprises found it uncomfortable. That did not mean every sector official wanted the same opening, or that every concession was valuable to China in itself. It meant the premier could justify a package partly through consequences that an individual protected industry might resist. A foreign negotiator who saw only reluctance to yield would miss an important source of Zhu’s willingness to bargain.

Washington did not close. Nor did it simply put an agreed text away for signature at a more convenient time. The memorandum signed by Long Yongtu and Peter Scher on April 10 recorded extensive agreement on goods and services, subject to checking and correction, but identified unfinished banking and auto-finance terms, securities, audiovisual services, textiles, and the duration of special trade-remedy provisions. Washington faced both a dispute over its readiness to close and consequential terms still to settle.[^4]

Publicizing the offer created another difficulty. At the April 8 American briefing, Barshefsky defended the commercial issues still outstanding and denied that publication was intended to mobilize business pressure. Economic adviser Gene Sperling acknowledged differences among advisers. In his later interview, Lawrence Summers criticized the attempt to claim the concessions publicly without completing the agreement. What could appear in Washington as evidence of negotiating success could appear in Beijing as a price paid for a bargain withheld.[^5]

The surviving memorandum mattered because it preserved a starting point more definite than those competing presentations. Yet it also preserved unfinished business. When talks resumed, the sides would argue over which April positions still counted. A negotiator who publicizes a provisional package may gain supporters, but also creates an audience that will treat its contents as a minimum entitlement. The other side then has reason to insist that the offer depended on receiving the whole bargain. China’s November proposal would state that condition explicitly.

## Bringing Washington to Beijing

The bombing of China’s embassy in Belgrade suspended accession talks. Communication did not cease altogether: the Chinese published history records messages and technical contacts during the interruption, before the leaders announced a resumption at Auckland. The earlier offers returned as disputed baselines, alongside differences over permanent normal trade relations, safeguards and the scope of the negotiation. The Chinese account reports Barshefsky treating high-technology export controls as outside her WTO mandate.[^6]

Washington also tried to reassure Beijing. Summers later recalled going to China in October with a presidential mandate to make clear that, if Beijing was prepared to finish, Washington was too. He met Zhu in Lanzhou while the premier was touring rural China. Economic development and the Asian financial crisis were part of their conversation; the WTO assurance belonged to a relationship in which they discussed more than the unfinished offer. His contemporary speech confirms the visit and meetings, though the private message comes from his later account.[^7]

Barshefsky wanted the composition of her November delegation to carry a similar assurance. In her oral history, she says she asked for either White House chief of staff John Podesta or National Economic Council director Gene Sperling to accompany her. Both, she recalled, had opposed closing during Zhu’s April visit. Bringing one to Beijing would demonstrate White House commitment and prevent interference from that official at home. Sperling went.[^8]

Barshefsky wanted the same travelling companion to reassure Beijing and protect her position in Washington. In her calculation, his part in the decision mattered as much as his title.

Beijing brought fourteen departments into the final round, according to financial negotiator Deng Hongguo. He recalled six days and nights at the ministry, camp beds between sessions and a pager that could call him back. Its sound accelerated his heartbeat.[^9]

Deng described sector regulators seeking clearance for alternatives while the trade ministry assembled the package. Knowing the answer and having permission to give it were different accomplishments.

## The price of another day

By November 12, the choices were detailed enough to put in a closing package. China offered 49 percent foreign equity in value-added telecommunications and paging within one year, with separate terms for mobile and other basic services. It offered 50 percent in life insurance on accession. Its banking proposal adjusted geography but did not promise national treatment; foreign nonbank auto finance remained conditional on comparable permission for Chinese institutions. It proposed that special trade-remedy provisions end no later than three years after accession. The package depended on reaching the final bilateral agreement during the current negotiation.[^10]

The American response preserved earlier schedules except where new provisions displaced them. The negotiators had a considerable accumulated text before them. In his November 12 letter to Shi, chargé d’affaires William C. McCahill Jr. made one proposed exchange explicit: discussion of sensitive equity terms was available within a package that met American requirements on safeguards and antidumping. Washington wanted protection against some consequences of the market integration it was negotiating; Beijing wanted to preserve ownership limits. The Americans asked for Zhu to help decide the combination.

McCahill attached a clock to that request. The delegation would leave its hotel at eight the following morning; Shi had until seven to say whether Zhu would meet it. If the premier could see them on Saturday, they could remain until later that day or early Sunday, but no longer. The Americans were offering more time for a particular decision, with a stated limit on how much time they would give.[^11]

The size of the trade-remedy gap explains why those terms could not be left to ceremonial closure. China wanted a maximum of three years. The American November 12 paper sought fifteen years for the product-specific safeguard; the next paper reduced that demand to twelve, while proposing twenty years for the special antidumping provision. The administration’s public account after agreement announced twelve years for the former and fifteen for the latter. These were different protections with different clocks, extending far beyond the Chinese proposal.[^12]

The equity bargaining was also more intricate than a contest between 49 and 51 percent. Both sides’ November 12 papers already specified 50 percent life-insurance ownership. In its next paper, the United States explicitly sought management control as well. Half the shares with control was commercially different from half the shares without it. In value-added telecommunications and paging, the next American paper sought 50 percent within two years, while distinguishing equity from management and operational control. Other telecommunications categories followed other schedules. A concession on one percentage could be offset, enlarged or qualified by the rights attached to it.

Zhu met Barshefsky on November 13. Deng describes new State Council authority for that day’s talks and a further financial-leasing approval from central bank governor Dai Xianglong that evening. Senior intervention still left individual commitments to be cleared.[^13]

Auto finance shows the movement. The November 12 Chinese proposal tied foreign nonbank access to corresponding domestic permission. Deng recalls immediate access for foreign enterprise-group finance companies under the new authority. The American paper attributed to November 13 expressly accepts a Chinese auto-finance offer made that day. The post-agreement American summary announces nonbank auto finance on accession. Together, the records trace a move away from waiting for a domestic permission toward access tied to membership, even though they do not expose every step of the instruction or reproduce the intervening offer.[^14]

That change mattered because a condition controlled by future domestic policy was different from a commitment activated by accession. The same sensitivity to clocks applies to banking. The American November 13 paper proposed customer-access deadlines no later than January 1, 2002 and January 1, 2005; the administration’s post-agreement summary described periods of two and five years after accession. A fixed date and an accession-relative period assign the cost of a delayed accession differently. The annexes, rather than an assumption that the formulations were equivalent, had to settle the obligation.[^12]

The November 13 meeting did not finish the bargain. On Sunday, the morning beyond which McCahill had said the delegation could not stay, he wrote again. The Americans alleged that China had reopened settled matters and introduced new ones. They invoked Zhu’s willingness to meet again if the talks reached another impasse and asked for his intervention. The letter treated the leaders’ intention to conclude as a reason for a further decision, not as evidence that the negotiators had already made it.[^15]

Here the later accounts separate sharply. Barshefsky recalled using a threatened departure to accelerate Chinese internal decisions, with Clinton’s backing. Deng dismissed the Americans’ departure tactics as ineffectual. The letters establish the pressure and the request for higher authority; they cannot decide which participant correctly judged its effect.[^16]

The distinction should survive the story’s happy ending. China might have made some of the same decisions without a threatened departure. The threat might also have helped force a choice among options already prepared. What the Americans demonstrably did was attach more time to a specified opportunity: a meeting with Zhu about an unresolved package. Their willingness to extend kept that opportunity alive. Their assertion that they could not remain beyond early Sunday proved negotiable.

For a negotiator considering a deadline, the tradeoff is exacting. A firm departure can end a fruitless round, but may cut off a decision that is close to authorization. An extension can buy that decision, but tells the counterpart that the announced clock can move. The November 12 letter made an extension conditional on a particular senior meeting. It did not guarantee agreement at that meeting, and the delegation ultimately stayed longer still. The test for an extension is therefore what additional decision it can obtain, and who is in a position to make it. More hours alone cannot supply missing authority.

Deng recalled Shi mistaking a summons from Zhu for a telephone call, then discovering that the premier was already at the ministry. The person who could give an instruction had arrived.[^17]

Zhu’s November 15 intervention was followed by the bilateral agreement. Its signed Chinese and English bodies sent industrial goods, agriculture and services commitments to three annexes, with provisions applying rules to access to each other’s markets in a fourth. The governments were to notify the WTO and transmit the full agreement for incorporation into the multilateral accession work. They had concluded an important bargain. They had not granted China membership.[^18]

Washington had not completed its domestic task either. Sandy Berger told reporters that day that the administration would seek permanent normal trade relations from Congress, an independent branch whose decision could not be supplied by the executive. The WTO welcomed the bilateral settlement while identifying negotiations with other governments and technical protocol work still ahead.[^19]

## What Europe still wanted

For the European Union, the American agreement changed the starting point without settling the price. At the January 2000 talks, chief negotiator Hans-Friedrich Beseler invoked most-favored-nation treatment and proposed building on the US bargain. But Europe’s fifteen member states had priorities of their own. Long Yongtu answered that the American package already contained concessions valuable to Europe, and that China had compensated for protecting sensitive sectors by giving more elsewhere.[^20]

Each was describing the inheritance that favored his next move. Beseler wanted the American gains as a base from which to negotiate European priorities. Long wanted the balance of the American package recognized as a reason not to reopen its protected sectors. The same Chinese offer could be generous in a negotiation weighted toward American interests and insufficient for a coalition with a different distribution of interests. Neither delegation could close simply by announcing that the other should be satisfied.

The European demands extended well below the political symbolism of accession. In a February 23 technical package, Europe proposed opening Zhuhai to renminbi banking immediately on accession while dropping its request to advance Beijing’s opening. Deng recalls central-bank approval for a Portuguese request to open Zhuhai in one year rather than three. For a firm waiting to operate there, the date could matter as much as the ownership ceiling. The final WTO schedule bound a one-year opening: earlier than Deng’s recalled benchmark, later than the immediate access in the European proposal.[^21]

By March, the difficult ownership questions remained. Trade Commissioner Pascal Lamy met Zhu but left without an agreement. According to the Chinese history, he told Shi that his authorization did not permit acceptance of China’s 50 percent ceilings in life insurance and automobiles. The same account records a more personal explanation after the meeting: Lamy had only recently become commissioner, had five years ahead of him and considered accepting the present terms too risky. He needed to return and coordinate a position.[^22]

This was a consequential refusal by the visiting side. China’s premier was accessible, but the European commissioner could not accept what China would offer. For Shi, another explanation of the Chinese ceiling would not resolve Lamy’s problem. The package had to become defensible to the governments whose authority he carried.

By May, the terms of that defense had changed. The Chinese account reports Lamy saying he could accept 50 percent in life insurance if Europe received compensation elsewhere in insurance. He could also accept the automobile ceiling with better tariff treatment. Acceptance of an ownership limit had become conditional on an alternative commercial gain. The point was to find benefits that the disappointed constituencies could value, rather than to make every sector yield on the same dimension.

Mobile telecommunications remained a problem. On May 17, Lamy said he had understood Zhu’s March remarks to allow a 50 percent ceiling and had reported that understanding to European ministers. Accepting 49 percent exceeded his authority. The next day he sought confirmation from a Chinese leader that 49 was the limit, so that he could report to Brussels and seek new authority.[^23]

A one-point difference was now entangled with a commissioner’s account of what a premier had said. Insisting on 50 would defend that earlier account but threaten the wider agreement. Accepting 49 without explanation would leave Lamy to justify the retreat himself. Authoritative confirmation could help him explain why the package had to be built around a constraint that he had previously described differently.

Lamy kept negotiating the surrounding terms. On the evening of May 18, he offered alternatives for mobile services: a faster path to the retained 49 percent ceiling, with 35 percent initially, or different ownership treatment for public and private networks. He also pressed on retail chains and insurance licenses. Shi agreed to coordinate with the relevant departments, while warning that improvement was unlikely. The following morning he reported that the coordination had not produced a better offer. Lamy then said he could accept 25 percent mobile equity initially, alongside a package in distribution and insurance.[^23]

At their May 19 meeting, Zhu described 49 percent as a collective Chinese government decision. Lamy reported consultations over the preceding two days with member states and industry. With Zhu’s confirmation, he said, Europe would respect the limit. He asked Zhu to authorize compensation in telecommunications, insurance and distribution. Zhu accepted the principle, leaving the details to Shi and Lamy. Wu Yi emphasized that the scope of the remaining retail-chain exceptions still needed negotiation. The parties checked the text and signed that day.[^24]

The premier’s contribution was partly to make a refusal authoritative. That supplied confirmation Lamy had requested before accepting the limit, alongside approval in principle of compensation. It was a different use of senior intervention from asking a leader to overrule an obstructive subordinate. The exact moment at which Brussels changed Lamy’s authority is not disclosed; the account places consultations before the meeting. What it does disclose is the problem he brought to Zhu and the answer Zhu gave.

For someone facing a similar impasse, the European sequence suggests a demanding option. A ceiling may be retained while its commercial cost is reduced through timing, geography, permitted activities or additional licenses. That works only if the counterpart can accept compensation and the people authorizing it regard the substitute benefits as worth having. Faster entry into a joint venture cannot automatically compensate a firm whose essential requirement is control. Nor is confirmation of a limit valuable merely because it comes from a senior person. Here it addressed a specific difficulty in the European mandate and travelled with a substantive package.

## The limits inside the opening

The final terms show what that package could—and could not—buy. In mobile telecommunications, the WTO schedule retained the 49 percent ceiling within three years after accession, with 25 percent at accession and 35 percent within one year. Geographic restrictions followed a separate five-year timetable. Europe had obtained a path into the market while accepting the ownership limit. The Commission called the equity phase-in an improvement on the American agreement; the November US public summary does not reproduce enough of that timetable for a complete bilateral comparison.[^25]

In distribution, the boundary of an exception determined the value of the opening. The Commission announced that the restriction on large foreign-owned chains had been lifted. The final schedule preserved a prohibition on foreign majority ownership for certain chains with more than thirty outlets selling specified products of different types and brands from multiple suppliers. That was a narrower restriction than treating every large chain alike. It was also different from requiring Chinese managerial control: an equally owned venture could satisfy a ban on a foreign majority. The commercially useful question was which enterprises and products fell on each side of the boundary.[^26]

Insurance supplied another kind of benefit. The Commission recorded seven new licenses—five life and two non-life—and two sub-branch licenses to be granted before accession, outside the accession terms themselves. Its October 2000 memorandum was still seeking timing for five further new licenses and two sub-branches. The promise of an administrative permission required an act of administration. Its value could not be cashed simply by pointing to the signed agreement.[^27]

The remaining multilateral negotiation also had a price. Existing WTO members wanted to know how China would give effect to its commitments. The Commission reported China accusing them of seeking burdens beyond those normally required of an acceding country. The bilateral settlements had narrowed the disagreement; they had not removed the need to turn market-access bargains into an acceptable body of rules.

The Americans threatened to leave and stayed. The Europeans met the premier and went home without an agreement, then returned with ways to accept limits they had resisted. Chinese officials prepared intricate offers, sought authority and defended important ceilings. All three delegations tried to make the other side’s domestic constraints part of the price.

Solomon directed negotiators’ attention to authority, timing and the arrival of senior leaders. Accession makes those questions more useful by giving them specific objects. The November letter sought an exchange across equity and trade remedies. The auto-finance negotiation changed the condition that would activate access. Lamy sought confirmation of a ceiling and compensation he could defend to his own principals. Senior contact helped when there was a decision for it to accomplish. It could not substitute for deciding what the bargain was worth.

The negotiations also place technical expertise firmly before accession. Chinese officials were already preparing schedules, distinguishing ownership from control and separating permission from implementation. Membership would give them another setting in which to use those skills. The next chapter follows a dispute in which China could challenge how the United States applied multilateral rules that membership now allowed China to invoke.
