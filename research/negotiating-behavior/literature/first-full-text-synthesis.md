# Updating Solomon: a wider repertoire under continuing political control

**First substantive literature assessment · 8 September 2026**

The evidence reviewed so far supports updating *Chinese Negotiating Behavior* around **a wider repertoire of instruments, more specialized institutions, and changing relationships with counterparts**. It does not yet support replacing Solomon with a single new account of how “the Chinese” negotiate. His emphasis on interests, political authority, and the purpose of the relationship remains productive. Some familiar diagnostic shortcuts—reading delay as manipulation, rigidity as factional conflict, or nationalist language as a binding domestic constraint—need much stronger qualification.

The distinction matters because Solomon’s own baseline is more conditional than a handbook of timeless cultural traits. His central study concerns 1967–1984, principally a period of constructing cooperation with Washington. He left open whether the same findings would apply under renewed confrontation. Freeman’s 1999 commentary already recognized specialization, multilateral learning, and variation across counterpart relationships. An update should trace those developments and test their limits, rather than claim their discovery. The supplied 2005 printing is not the date of the underlying research.[^baseline]

The present assessment combines full-text scholarship with selected checks of the underlying official record and the existing 1999 WTO documentary pilot. It establishes several defensible revisions to the book’s questions and provisional conclusions. It is **not yet a researched account of every major negotiation through 2026**. In particular, the later US trade-war episodes still require the same reconstruction of offers and decisions already begun for accession.

## What survives, and what changes

| Element of Solomon’s account | Provisional assessment | Implication for the update |
|---|---|---|
| Interests and the intended relationship constrain what tactics can achieve | **Retain** | Explain the stakes and acceptable alternatives before interpreting style. Renewed confrontation changes the setting Solomon himself qualified. |
| Senior authority and negotiating mandates matter | **Retain; update the institutional account** | Identify which official or body could change an offer, and distinguish political approval from technical preparation and execution. |
| Broad principles and relationships help structure bargaining | **Retain with issue-specific limits** | Political formulas coexist with detailed schedules, legal argument and implementation rules. Their relative importance is an empirical question. |
| Institutional learning changes practice | **Expand Freeman’s existing argument** | Trace who learned, which instruments became available, and where professional advice remained subordinate to political objectives. |
| Inferences drawn from delay, ambiguity or rigidity (S-P07, S-P11, S-C19) | **Downgrade as a diagnostic** | Similar behavior can result from tactical choice, limited authority, conflicting goals, or an unattractive proposed deal. |
| Public sentiment constrains negotiators | **Disaggregate** | A claim about public anger, measured attitudes, mobilization, leaders’ beliefs, and an actual negotiating instruction are different evidence. |
| A recent succession of confrontations demonstrates a new national style | **Not established** | Separate increased capabilities and activity from changed procedures, objectives and willingness to settle. Date the change before explaining it. |

These are analytical judgments from the evidence below, not a numerical score of how often Solomon was right. The source-linked reading memos preserve disagreements and limitations that the table necessarily compresses.

## 1. Legal and institutional learning is the clearest expansion of the repertoire

The strongest trade literature in this pass connects changes in practice to people and organizations. Shaffer and Gao’s 2018 study follows trade officials, lawyers and internationally engaged businesses through interviews, observation and documents. Gao’s later account interprets changing Chinese attitudes toward the WTO. Read together, they suggest a substantial expansion in legal capacity alongside an increasingly contested assessment of what the institution can deliver.[^trade]

Our conclusion is narrower than a story of China becoming a “legalistic” negotiator. **Expertise, compliance, confidence in an institution, and willingness to compromise can move independently.** A lawyer can become better at contesting an American measure without gaining authority to settle the dispute. A ministry can use WTO obligations to oppose another agency’s proposal while accepting a political instruction that overrides its advice. Greater skill in drafting or litigation does not necessarily make bargaining easier for a counterpart.

Nor was technical bargaining absent before accession. The 1999 pilot includes a signed MOU identifying unresolved issues, sector-specific conditional offers, and American proposals separating ownership from management control. Those documents prevent us from treating 2001 as the birth of detailed Chinese economic negotiation. What requires explanation is the subsequent scale, routinization, personnel and deployment of expertise.[^pilot]

A primary-source check also complicates a simple sequence from early legal optimism to later geopolitical skepticism. Jiang Zemin’s published February 2002 speech discusses WTO-related training and administrative adaptation within a broader account of international competition and national interests. The political and professional agendas were present together. The text is an edited publication of a leadership speech, not direct access to all the deliberations behind it.[^jiang]

For the book, the appropriate revision is therefore to give institutional learning a substantive chapter. It should show how technical expertise changes the options available to political leaders and the terms a foreign counterpart encounters. This develops Freeman’s existing insight rather than repudiating it.

## 2. Central authority remains consequential, but its exercise must be reconstructed

Jakobson and Manuel’s early-Xi institutional account describes senior authority together with overlapping responsibilities and competition among information channels. Subsequent official documents establish formal changes: the 2018 reform plan converted the foreign-affairs leading group into a commission and reassigned maritime-rights coordination; the 2023 Foreign Relations Law specifies central leadership and departmental responsibilities.[^institutions]

This supports a concrete update to the machinery described in an older book. It does not establish that every move at the negotiating table is personally designed at the top. Technical drafting, operational initiative, political approval and implementation can be distributed differently. A system can centralize final authority while leaving considerable discretion over how an objective is pursued.

That distinction changes how to interpret apparent inconsistency. An agency that pursues a broad instruction too aggressively and is subsequently corrected is one possible sequence. An agency executing a centrally approved pressure campaign is another. A final outcome compatible with the leadership’s broad interests cannot distinguish them. Nor can a disorderly interval alone establish fragmentation. The relevant evidence concerns when a superior knew, what was authorized, and which intervention changed the subordinate’s conduct.

The original WTO pilot gives this question empirical traction. Chinese retrospective participant testimony describes sectoral approval chains; American letters request senior Chinese intervention while setting their own deadlines. Leader access appears as something both parties actively seek to influence. Our provisional conclusion is that authority should remain central to the updated book, with greater attention to **who prepares a choice, who approves it, and who can deliver it**.[^pilot]

This also weakens several behavioral diagnostics. A pause is observable; its motive is not. A rigid position may reflect unified resolve rather than leadership conflict. An appeal for instructions may be sincere and advantageous at the same time. Treating these as alternative mechanisms makes Solomon’s observations more useful and less vulnerable to cultural overinterpretation.

## 3. Implementation has a more developed formal apparatus—and remains part of bargaining

A particularly useful recent primary source is China’s March 2025 WTO-compliance opinion. The 2014 notice already required assessment during policy drafting. Its replacement makes the procedures more explicit: assessment as a necessary step before issuance, written findings accompanying submissions for approval, and necessary modifications where incompatibility risks are identified. It concerns WTO rules and China’s accession commitments. The published text is expressly abridged.[^compliance]

This is a demonstrable institutional development after much of the scholarship’s observation window. It gives a 2026 update a concrete question beyond whether China generally “keeps agreements”: how are WTO obligations translated into domestic policy decisions, which officials identify conflicts, and what happens when a policy objective conflicts with an obligation?

The document does not answer those questions by itself. It cannot establish that assessments are independent, that they alter outcomes, or that all agencies comply. The important evidence would be an assessment that changed a draft, a referral resolving an interagency conflict, or an implementation dispute that exposed the limits of the process.

Solomon’s treatment of continued bargaining after agreement remains a useful proposition to test against these procedures. The update should examine domestic implementation alongside legal remedies and subsequent exchanges with counterparts; the 2025 document alone does not establish how such interactions work. A signed commitment, a domestic implementing measure and a foreign counterpart’s judgment of compliance are three different stages. Their divergence is an object of research, not a sufficient diagnosis of a national attitude toward agreements.

## 4. Public language is evidence of communication; its bargaining effect is a separate question

Weiss and Dafoe’s survey experiments investigate how rhetoric affects domestic approval during crises. The complete author manuscript provides grounds for considering how framing can make restraint acceptable, but it also reports small effects and several statistically uncertain estimates. The comparison between explicit and vague threats spans different scenarios, limiting causal interpretation.[^audiences]

The resulting revision is to stop treating nationalism as a single external constraint handed to negotiators. A diplomat can invoke public anger, the state can influence its visibility, citizens can hold views that officials do not publicize, and leaders can anticipate costs that a survey does not capture. Those processes can coexist. None alone establishes the instructions governing an actual concession.

This means both easy conclusions are premature: that public opinion forces Chinese leaders into confrontation, or that propaganda allows them to neutralize any domestic cost. The useful unit of analysis is a particular proposed compromise, observed by a particular audience, at a particular point in the crisis. We need to connect public framing to that choice.

Mokry’s comparison of Chinese and official English statements adds another important discipline: preserve and compare language versions. Yet its interpretations also require checking. An omitted modifier or a reference to a charter’s spirit does not automatically establish a different substantive position. One of the documents driving its reported 2019 increase lacked a complete English translation, making document completeness consequential to the comparison.[^translation]

For a new edition, white papers and official readouts should serve as evidence of publicly authorized positions, justifications and audience choices. Their vocabulary must be tested against the proposals, conditions and implementation rules at issue. We should retain Solomon’s attention to wording while becoming more precise about what the wording can establish.

## 5. The timing of greater assertiveness does not map neatly onto a new negotiating style

Johnston’s assessment of the 2010 assertiveness debate and Chubb’s longer study of South China Sea actions make different measurements and should not be treated as interchangeable verdicts. Both nevertheless demonstrate the value of an explicit baseline. Chubb identifies important expansions of activity and capability before Xi, while Johnston audits whether prominent episodes were new and whether reporting provided independent evidence.[^security]

The conclusion for this project is not that China has become no more coercive. It is that **changes in means, frequency, intensity, objectives and bargaining procedure must be dated separately**. More capable ships can make a pressure tactic feasible without changing the authorization process. A greater number of confrontations can coexist with familiar diplomatic formulas. Conversely, unchanged language can conceal a shift in acceptable terms.

Neither study supplies a systematic account of 2016–2026. Nor does an action count establish the effectiveness of pressure. To judge negotiating change, an episode needs the counterpart’s response and the available settlement alternatives. A deployment followed by talks might have caused movement, accompanied a new political instruction, or responded to the same third development as the talks.

The bounded reading of Chadha’s India–China report illustrates the problem. Around the Pangong sequence, military leverage and senior diplomatic engagement change together. The available interviews are with Indian stakeholders, limiting access to Chinese internal decisions. The account is useful for reconstructing the interaction, but insufficient to allocate all causal weight to either coercion or political authorization. India’s own relationship framing also reminds us to compare both parties’ conduct before labeling a tactic distinctively Chinese.[^india]

## 6. The update needs two kinds of revision

Some revisions concern genuine developments after Solomon’s observation period: more extensive trade-law expertise, changed coordinating institutions, and a more elaborated compliance apparatus. Other revisions concern what can now be known about the earlier period. Later archival scholarship can alter the interpretation of an old negotiation without showing that Chinese behavior subsequently changed.

Wei and Summers’ newly accessible study of the Hong Kong negotiations supplies a concrete example. Their reconstruction questions an interpretation of Chinese resistance to continued British administration as ignorance of capitalist economics, examining prior economic research and contacts with business elites. It describes flexibility within a sovereignty-first position. It also reports British efforts to use American and Japanese business intermediaries in 1982—a useful counterpart comparison for supposedly distinctive indirect influence.[^hongkong]

These findings revise the historical baseline; they are not evidence that such flexibility emerged after 1984. Their evidentiary asymmetry also matters: British archival records are more accessible than China’s internal files, and Chinese memoirs can depend partly on previously published British accounts. Hong Kong’s particular sovereignty dispute cannot stand in for every negotiation between large countries. Its value is a documented comparison of moves and an opportunity to test explanations against both sides’ conduct.

That distinction should organize the book. Each substantive chapter should distinguish **a revised account of the baseline**, **a documented later development**, and **an unresolved comparison**. Otherwise a better historical source can be mistaken for evidence of evolution, and a recent publication date can be mistaken for recent empirical coverage.

The strongest working conclusion is already clear enough to guide further writing: **Chinese negotiating practice is better understood as a changing set of capabilities and procedures operating under political objectives and authority constraints than as one timeless style or one comprehensive Xi-era replacement.** The continuity lies partly in questions of interests, authorization and relationship purpose. The change lies partly in who participates, which instruments they command, and how commitments are translated into domestic action.

For the US comparison, the next substantive bridge is from accession to WTO disputes and then to the 2018–2026 trade negotiations. The key comparisons are successive demands, draft provisions, settlement authority, retaliation and implementation—not simply whether a round ended in agreement. Britain, India and Russia supply important tests of how far findings travel, but require their own bilateral evidence rather than transfer from Washington’s experience.
