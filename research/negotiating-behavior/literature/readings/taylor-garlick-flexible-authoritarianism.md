# Central direction and delegated action: Taylor and Garlick reconsidered

Complete scholarly reading, 15 September 2026. Monique Taylor and Jeremy Garlick, “‘Flexible’ versus ‘fragmented’ authoritarianism: evidence from Chinese foreign policy during the Xi Jinping era,” *Australian Journal of International Affairs* 79(2), 2025:189–208. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/10357718.2024.2437035); [lawful published-text deposit](https://zenodo.org/records/15016796). Published online 18 December 2024. The issue year does not describe the end of the empirical observations.

All twenty printed pages, 189–208, were read, including acknowledgments, disclosure, data-availability statement and complete references. The repository PDF has twenty-two pages because it adds two cover pages. Printed p. 189 was visually checked for bibliographic details and the explicit CC BY 4.0 license. The earlier complete reading of the authors' April 2025 public brief remains a separate format of the same argument, not a second independent scholarly study. ## The argument worth retaining

Taylor and Garlick give a useful warning against treating every variation in Chinese conduct as evidence of organizational conflict. Their alternative is delegation within central objectives: officials and enterprises can adjust implementation, pursue opportunities and sometimes fail while the Party leadership sets the broad direction and retains the capacity to intervene. The relevant distinction is between discretion permitted by a principal and conduct that escapes or defeats that principal's wishes. An outsider can observe similar local variation under either arrangement. [189–191, 195–196.]

This matters for negotiation. A technical counterpart need not be either autonomous or powerless. It may possess room to develop a proposal within a political instruction while lacking permission to accept the final price. Conversely, a later intervention from Beijing does not establish that every earlier action followed an approved plan. The article supplies an alternative explanation to investigate; it does not remove the need to establish who could authorize the particular act being sought.

The authors acknowledge that the original fragmented-authoritarianism literature described a strong center with dispersed bargaining below it. Their subsequent presentation sometimes turns that account into a stronger claim of incoherence than is necessary for the rival model. Central priorities, bargaining among organizations and uneven implementation can coexist. The manuscript should therefore avoid choosing a single label for the whole state when the evidence concerns different stages of a transaction. [192–195.]

## What the cases establish—and what the inference adds

The article uses existing literature and reporting rather than a new disclosed interview sample or an original dataset. Its data-availability statement expressly says the study generated no new data. Several case sources are the authors' earlier work. That does not disqualify their expertise, but it means their repeated interpretation is not independent confirmation. The four broad goals they identify—Party survival, national unity, security and growth—also accommodate many competing policies. To distinguish their explanation from alternatives requires evidence of the permitted boundary, not just a policy that can be related to one of those goals. [195–196, 204–208.]

**CEFC in the Czech Republic.** The account follows the company's political endorsement and investments, subsequent failures and the replacement of its position by the state-owned CITIC. It is plausible evidence that the center could intervene when a commercial actor caused political and financial trouble. The authors go further: the emergency intervention itself is said to reveal the original operation of their model. That inference is not compelled. Subsequent correction can follow deliberate delegation, insufficient oversight, conflicting interests or a changing assessment of an initially supported project. The article does not publish the original mandate or the decision ordering the rescue. Its discussion repeatedly relies on Garlick's earlier research. [196–197.]

There is also a concrete identification error. Page 197 calls Patrick Ho CEFC's chief financial officer. The American Justice Department's March 25, 2019 sentencing account identifies him as secretary-general of the **China Energy Fund Committee**, a nongovernmental organization funded by CEFC China Energy. Those entities should not be collapsed. This correction does not determine whether the takeover was centrally directed; it prevents an inaccurate title from becoming part of the manuscript's evidence about control. [TG-04, “Overview,” first paragraph.]

**South China Sea.** The authors infer coordination from the scale and sustained execution of island construction and the absence of public contestation. A large coordinated undertaking is relevant evidence of organizational capacity. Lack of public disagreement is weaker evidence about whether officials bargained internally, and the article supplies no directions settling that question. Similarly, alternation between pressure and accommodation can be deliberate, but identifying that possibility does not establish that every change was centrally intended. The account's categorical language is stronger than the available process evidence. [198–200.]

One supporting observation also needs a date boundary. On p. 199, citing Morris (2019), the authors assert that no forced boarding by the China Coast Guard had occurred since its formation in 2013. A Chinese official account of the June 17, 2024 Second Thomas Shoal confrontation expressly includes boarding inspections and forced expulsion among the measures taken. The article was published online in December 2024 without giving its no-boarding sentence a 2019 endpoint. The Chinese statement is enough to make an undated claim of continued abstention unsafe; it does not independently establish every Philippine allegation, the legality of either side's conduct or the instruction behind the operation. Nor does boarding itself disprove central direction. It removes one of the article's proposed indicators of restraint, rather than proving the competing institutional model. [TG-03, complete statement.]

**Energy companies.** Appointments, Party committees, state finance and the possibility of disciplinary action give the center means of influence. The account also allows company initiative. Its Brazil discussion cites Zhang and Chen's 2021 study for interviews concerning directives and limited participation in policy formulation. That is a promising major-country source lead, but the underlying study was not acquired or read in this pass. Neither its interviews nor the authors' description of them should be presented as fresh interviews conducted for Taylor and Garlick's article. The removal of an executive demonstrates an intervention; without prior instructions it does not establish how much operational behavior the leadership previously controlled. [201–202.]

The closing comparison between Chinese consistency and American discontinuity is not supported by a matched sample of decisions or negotiations in this article. It should not become a national ranking in the book. The case studies are too heterogeneous to estimate a change in willingness to concede under Xi. [203–204.]

## A constitutional citation corrected

The reference on p. 207 labeled “NPC. 2017. Constitution of the People's Republic of China” links to the **Constitution of the Communist Party of China**, revised at the Party's Nineteenth Congress on October 24, 2017. Its title page resolves the identification; PDF pp. 7–8 contain the foreign-policy and Belt and Road passages. Party constitutional change and state constitutional change are different acts. The text supports the article's narrower observation that the Belt and Road entered the Party's constitutional program, but the reference must not be carried over under its erroneous title or issuer. This pass read only pp. 1, 7 and 8 of the twenty-eight-page source. [TG-02.]

## What the manuscript should do with this reading

The historical update can establish changes in assigned responsibilities more securely than a general change in negotiating flexibility. The 2018 reform transferred maritime-rights coordination, intelligence assessment and emergency coordination to the Central Foreign Affairs Commission and its office. The 2023 Foreign Relations Law states different roles for central direction, military exchanges, diplomatic coordination and authorized local activity. The April 2024 local-affairs conference's published instructions combine central discipline with initiative. The [separate primary-source study](../../manuscript/development/authority-routing-research-2026-09-15.md) identifies the provisions and their limits.

Read together, the sources support a practical question that is more useful than deciding whether China is uniformly centralized: what can this counterpart develop, what must be referred upward, and which body must perform the resulting commitment? They also make the May 2019 uncertainty sharper. Even after the 2018 reforms, the public record does not reveal whether a trade draft had final approval. A clearer account of the supervisory structure is not a recovered mandate.

This reading therefore qualifies the stronger institutional explanations on both sides. It earns discussion of deliberately delegated room and the center's means of correction. It does not earn an assumption that apparent friction is staged, that every commercial failure was an authorized experiment, or that ministerial differences have vanished. Further work should trace bounded proposals through preparation, referral, decision and execution; the missing instructions cannot be replaced by an attractive general model.

## Unfinished source leads

- Zhang and Chen (2021), the Brazil energy study cited on pp. 201–202: acquire the actual article and examine who was interviewed, when, and about which decisions before using it as an additional major-country comparison.
- Morris (2019): the no-boarding proposition was checked against later primary evidence, but the original chapter's date boundaries and intended scope were not independently audited.
- CEFC/CITIC: the account's cited takeover reporting and earlier Garlick study remain separate acquisition targets if the Czech episode is to enter the manuscript. The present memo does not certify the complete corporate or decision history.
