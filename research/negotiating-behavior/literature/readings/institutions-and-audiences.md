# Authority, audiences, and the interpretation of official language

Reading memo, 8 September 2026. This memo distinguishes authors’ arguments, the evidence actually inspected, and our proposed tests. The companion [evidence register](institutions-and-audiences-evidence.json) records versions and coverage. These are tests of mechanisms in Solomon’s account, not a completed history of foreign-policy decision making through 2026.

## 1. Jakobson and Manuel: institutional context is a rival explanation for apparent tactics

**Linda Jakobson and Ryan Manuel (2016), “How are Foreign Policy Decisions Made in China?”, *Asia & the Pacific Policy Studies* 3(1):101–110.** [Publisher](https://doi.org/10.1002/app5.121); [institutional full text](https://openresearch-repository.anu.edu.au/bitstreams/c80aa17c-93cd-4059-93b8-5793127b0aa4/download). All ten pages, including references, read; p.109 visually checked.

The article synthesizes the authors’ earlier research, interviews, workshops, and institutional mapping. It describes overlapping responsibilities and competing information channels alongside ultimate senior authority. Its early-Xi discussion cautions against extrapolating comprehensive transformation from the first few years. The ambiguous maritime instructions discussed on p.109 derive from Jakobson’s 2014 report, p.29, which attributes them to an unnamed official involved in preparing leading-group meetings. That is a reported interview chain, not an instruction document independently reproduced in this article.

**Our assessment.** Solomon’s ambiguity proposition (S-P07) needs an actor and recipient attached to every observation. An ambiguous instruction from a leader to subordinate agencies is analytically different from an ambiguous offer to a foreign negotiator. The first might accommodate conflicting domestic objectives; the second might preserve room to bargain. Both can occur in one episode. Neither is identified merely by observing vague language. Likewise, “waiting for instructions” could reflect an approval boundary, competition for access, bureaucratic caution, or strategic delay. The observable test is the sequence of requests, internal decisions, and changed offers.

Two later primary documents update the article’s institutional snapshot. The **2018 Party and state reform plan**, part I(4), converts the foreign-affairs leading small group into a commission with coordination and implementation-oversight responsibilities; I(17) transfers the maritime-rights leading group’s functions to that commission and its office. These clauses were read in the [MOFCOM reproduction](https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/xxgcxjpxsdzgtsshzysx/zywj/art/2018/art_3fa8291d599c4e959c83947a9900c262.html), which identifies its source as *People’s Daily*. The **2023 Foreign Relations Law**, articles 5, 9, 14 and 16, formalizes Party leadership, central coordination, departmental responsibilities, and authorized local activity; articles 30–33 address treaty performance, protected interests, and countermeasures. [Official bilingual text](https://www.mfa.gov.cn/web/wjb_673085/zfxxgk_674865/zcfg/fl/202306/t20230628_11105189.shtml).

These records establish formal changes. They do not establish that instructions became unambiguous, that every agency complied, or that summit decisions were always implemented. A 2026 institutional chapter should examine the difference between authority prescribed and authority exercised. The existence of the 2023 law also makes it inappropriate to carry forward the article’s p.108 description of the legislative environment as an undated current fact.

**A competing interpretation, with limited access.** Taylor and Garlick’s scholarly article, “‘Flexible’ versus ‘fragmented’ authoritarianism,” remains unread in full. The authors’ complete 22 April 2025 [Australian Outlook brief](https://www.internationalaffairs.org.au/australianoutlook/beyond-fragmentation-how-xis-china-implements-foreign-policy/) was read. It argues that operational discretion can serve centrally defined objectives, drawing on cases including Chinese investment in the Czech Republic. This is an authored summary of the argument, not a substitute for auditing the article’s evidence.

Our proposed discriminator is timing. If a problematic organization is replaced, the replacement is compatible with effective correction after a delegation failure as well as with sustained central direction. Evidence of broad strategic consistency cannot by itself demonstrate that each preceding move was centrally intended. We need the original mandate, the deviation, the timing of central knowledge, and the instruction that produced correction. This avoids deciding the institutional debate by selecting either a coherent outcome or a disorderly moment.

## 2. Weiss and Dafoe: public opinion is a mechanism to investigate, not a fixed bargaining constraint

**Jessica Chen Weiss and Allan Dafoe (2019), “Authoritarian Audiences, Rhetoric, and Propaganda in International Crises: Evidence from China.”** [Final article DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/isq/sqz059). The version actually read is the complete **29-page author manuscript dated 28 January 2019**, including notes and references, [available from Weiss](https://www.jessicachenweiss.com/uploads/3/0/6/3/30636001/19-01-24-elite-statements-isq-ca.pdf). Figures on manuscript pp.17 and 19 were visually checked. The final typeset article and supplementary files were not read or replicated.

Two online surveys conducted in 2015–2016, with 2,992 and 5,445 respondents, examine reactions to rhetoric in hypothetical and historically based scenarios. Their findings suggest that framing and threat specificity can influence approval of government conduct, including restraint. However, pp.17–23 show small effects, several statistically uncertain estimates, and limitations arising from the designs. The explicit-threat and vague-threat conditions occur in different scenarios; p.20 discusses the additional difference in how final the outcome appears.

**Our assessment.** We should avoid converting suggestive experimental differences into a confident rule about actual crisis concessions. A hypothetical withdrawal and continued flights through an air-defense identification zone differ in more than rhetorical specificity. A cross-scenario contrast does not cleanly isolate the cost of a specific threat. Nor does a survey response identify what a leader believed, feared, or authorized during a negotiation.

For S-P15, distinguish five observations: a diplomat’s invocation of public sentiment; independently observed attitudes; state encouragement or suppression of mobilization; leaders’ assessment of the domestic consequences of compromise; and the actual limits placed on negotiators. These may point in different directions. A government can shape public interpretations and still encounter constraints. Nationalist language may justify waiting as well as confrontation. Conversely, weak average treatment effects do not demonstrate that a concentrated, politically salient audience has no influence.

A useful case reconstruction should pair Chinese public messaging with the exact proposal under discussion, the audience able to observe it, and evidence of private deliberation. Public approval is an intervening mechanism to test, not a proxy for the concession itself. This qualification complements the [security memo](security-change.md), which distinguishes measured actions, public signals, and later interpretations.

## 3. Mokry: compare language versions, then test the interpretation

**Sabine Mokry (2022), “What is Lost in Translation? Differences between Chinese Foreign Policy Statements and Their Official English Translations,” *Foreign Policy Analysis* 18(3), orac012.** [Full article](https://academic.oup.com/fpa/article/18/3/orac012/6575815). The complete publisher HTML article, notes, references, tables and all eight figures were read. Citations below use section/table locators because the typeset PDF was not used.

The study compares selected foreign-policy passages in paired Chinese and English documents. Its contribution is a systematic warning that the two versions are not interchangeable. The corpus includes speeches and policy documents, rather than negotiation transcripts. Its inference about the intentions signaled by particular differences depends on interpretive coding; its conclusion leaves the motivations behind differing translations for further research.

**Our assessment.** Table 3 provides reasons to adopt the comparison method while independently testing the substantive coding. For example, the presence of a temporal modifier in a Chinese description of global governance is interpreted as suggesting a challenge to existing rules. But identifying a system as contemporary does not logically commit its speaker to overturning it. A change from referring to a charter’s “spirit” to the charter itself also requires substantive context before it can establish a different negotiating position. These are hypotheses about meaning, not self-interpreting measurements of intent.

Footnote 4 is especially consequential for historical comparison: eleven of the twelve substantive differences identified for 2019 are concentrated in two documents, and the author says that a full English translation of one was unavailable. Comparing an abridged English account with a complete Chinese speech can generate omissions without demonstrating a new translation policy or a shift in national intentions. Document completeness must therefore be checked before treating the time series as evidence of behavioral change. The underlying corpus and coding were not replicated in this pass.

For S-P05, S-P07 and S-P17–19, the working rule is to preserve the Chinese original, official English, our translation of the relevant passage, issuing institution, date, audience, document type, and completeness status. Then ask whether a difference changes an obligation, condition, demand, or commitment. Wording can shape the presentation of a position while leaving the substantive proposal unchanged. Conversely, an apparently small change to a condition or implementation clause may matter greatly. This is why white papers need to be read alongside drafts and agreements.

## Implications for the research design

These readings make three claims more discriminating. First, senior authority remains worth investigating, but formal centralization does not establish the origin of every negotiating move. Second, public sentiment may affect bargaining through multiple channels that need separate evidence. Third, linguistic differences matter, but interpretation requires matched documents and the relevant proposal.

The best next institutional test is a dated US–China trade negotiation in which public statements, successive drafts, approval requests, and implementation records can be assembled together. The 1999 pilot already supplies some of those components. Later cases should be reconstructed to the same standard before making a claim about a distinctive Xi-era negotiating style.
