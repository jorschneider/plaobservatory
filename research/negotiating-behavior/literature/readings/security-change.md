# Security bargaining: measuring change before explaining it

**Reading date: 8 September 2026. Provisional research memo; not a conclusion about behavior through 2026.**

The strongest implication for updating Solomon is methodological: separate changes in objectives, instruments, negotiating procedure, and settlement outcomes. A government can acquire stronger means of coercion while retaining familiar approval procedures. Conversely, repeated diplomatic language can conceal a change in acceptable terms. Neither continuity of vocabulary nor a succession of confrontations establishes continuity or transformation of a national negotiating style.

This memo draws on two complete readings and a bounded contemporary case. Johnston’s final journal article was read sequentially, printed pp. 7–48, including notes 1–116. Chubb’s **accepted manuscript**, pp. 1–41, was read sequentially, including notes 1–142; these are manuscript page references, **not** the final article’s pp. 79–121. Chadha was read sequentially within printed pp. 1–7 and 13–20, with notes 1–34 and 64–139. The remaining report and its online meeting annexure have not been read here. Exact files, hashes, visual checks, and original-source scopes are in [the evidence register](security-change-evidence.json).

## Johnston: establish the baseline and audit the source chain

Alastair Iain Johnston, [“How New and Assertive Is China’s New Assertiveness?”](https://www.belfercenter.org/sites/default/files/pantheon_files/files/publication/IS3704_pp007-048.pdf), *International Security* 37(4), 2013, pp. 7–48, interrogates seven prominent episodes associated with the 2010 assertiveness narrative. His tests include prior behavior, cooperative counterexamples, chronology, media sourcing, and rhetoric. He recognizes meaningful South China Sea changes; the argument is not that nothing changed (pp. 19–20, 31–34, 45–46). His definition emphasizes threatened costs (pp. 9–10), which should not be silently equated with Chubb’s broader measure of strengthened positions.

The most useful design lesson is to specify the comparison before selecting vivid incidents. Our cases need a denominator: opportunities to negotiate, comparable provocations, and concessions as well as breakdowns. Johnston’s cooperative examples help expose selection bias but are themselves not an exhaustive population. A future dataset must make its inclusion rules reproducible rather than answer one selective list with another.

His discussion of claims that the South China Sea became a core interest demonstrates why repeated reporting is not independent corroboration (pp. 17–20). For our source graph, every newspaper account should point to its underlying interview, document, or earlier report. An anonymous official quoted by several outlets remains one evidentiary channel. The same rule applies to an academic article repeating an earlier diplomatic memoir.

Some of Johnston’s rebuttals require equally careful limits. In the rare-earth episode, inconsistent trade patterns are used to question a centrally imposed embargo (pp. 22–28). Our inference: this does not exclude partial, deniable, unevenly implemented coercion. Shipment timing, stock movements, customs discretion, and the distinction between instructions and compliance could all produce heterogeneous observations. The discriminating evidence would be contemporaneous customs instructions and firm-level shipping records across affected and comparison destinations. We should not code the embargo as disproved from this article alone.

Likewise, public military commentary and its timing cannot establish who authorized policy. Johnston’s treatment of possible bureaucratic outflanking is appropriately tentative in places (pp. 20–21). Correlated military and party rhetoric later in the article cannot, by itself, prove either institutional unity or autonomy (pp. 41–45). Missing evidence of elite conflict has less force when internal records are inaccessible.

## Chubb: a longer baseline changes both the timing and the explanation

Andrew Chubb’s [accepted manuscript](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/151535/1/Chubb_China_s_assertiveness_AUTHOR_ACCEPTED_COPY.pdf), published as “PRC Assertiveness in the South China Sea: Measuring Continuity and Change, 1970–2015,” *International Security* 45(3), 2021, pp. 79–121, defines assertiveness through actions strengthening a disputed position. It distinguishes declarative, demonstrative, coercive, and forceful actions and codes 132 instances of new or intensified activity. Its main temporal claim identifies surges in 1973, 1987, 1992, and 2007 (manuscript pp. 5–16). Chinese chronologies, yearbooks, and institutional accounts substantially widen the evidence beyond foreign press attention.

This approach is especially valuable for avoiding a Xi-first chronology. The manuscript reconstructs a sequence from maritime legal commitments to administrative investment, vessel delivery, and expanded operations that precedes Xi’s accession (pp. 32–35). It also distinguishes overall operational authorization from tactical initiative in its 1988 discussion (pp. 24–28). Neither a captain’s discretion nor a civilian agency’s initiative necessarily tells us who approved the wider policy.

The next research step should test how much these preconditions explain, rather than treat them as sufficient causes. Acquiring a fleet expands the feasible set; it does not identify why a leader selects a particular target, timing, or negotiating demand. To compare Hu and Xi, we need decisions made when similar instruments were already available, including occasions when officials held back. Chubb explicitly acknowledges the absence of the internal deliberations needed for full process tracing (p. 18).

Three measurement cautions matter for our project. First, a count of intensified actions is not a count of bargaining rounds, and different events need not have equal strategic significance. Second, the accumulated activity chart includes both known and inferred persistence or diminution (Figure 2, p. 15, visually checked). It must not be described as independently observed annual pressure. Third, the manuscript suggests that inclusion in an official retrospective strongly indicates intentional government action (p. 11). Our caution: retrospective institutional credit can ratify or appropriate an initiative after the event. Authorization requires a dated instruction, approval account, or other independently assessed evidence.

The discussion of concealed operations and later public attention creates a useful test of diversionary explanations (pp. 36–39): an operation unknown to the public cannot immediately distract that public through its publicity. This does not exclude anticipated public reactions, elite audiences, or nationalism affecting subsequent bargaining. Search interest is also not a direct measure of public preferences. The argument should therefore be split into onset, authorization, public amplification, and crisis effects. The 2026 Chubb article preserved in the register is an **abstract-only lead** on this last mechanism, not a third completed reading.

The replication appendix was located bibliographically but could not be retrieved in this pass. I have not independently checked the 132 event codings or reproduced the figures. Nor does the completed reading supply systematic evidence for 2016–2026.

## Original-source checks: two narrower findings that survive tracing

Johnston’s notes 18 and 20 lead to Chinese MFA statements on U.S. arms sales. The complete relevant bodies were read in Chinese. The [7 January 2010 Q&A](https://www.mfa.gov.cn/gjhdq_676201/gj_676203/bmz_679954/1206_680528/fyrygth_680536/200912/t20091231_9361384.shtml) concerns implementation of a Bush-era sale and objects to interference and damage to security interests. The [30 January statement](https://www.mfa.gov.cn/web/wjb_673085/zygy_673101/201001/t20100130_7459402.shtml), concerning a newly announced package, adds an intensifier to interference and describes serious danger to national security. This confirms a textual distinction within January; it does not establish drafting intent, consequences, or the entire earlier baseline. Speaker rank and triggering event also differ. A valid extension should compare equivalent speakers and genres. The first page’s URL contains a December 2009 date string; its displayed dateline is 7 January 2010.

Chubb’s account of the Law of the Sea negotiations can be checked against [UN record A/CONF.62/SR.25](https://legal.un.org/diplconf_records/1973_los/docs/english/vol_1/a_conf62_sr25.pdf). The complete Chinese intervention, paragraphs 11–22, printed pp. 80–81, was read and visually checked. Chai Shu-fan’s 2 July 1974 speech combines support for 200-nautical-mile coastal rights and developing-country solidarity with participation in international rulemaking. This is evidence of a public negotiating position. It is not a record of internal preference formation or proof that the speech caused later maritime policy. Geographical precision matters: its prior-notification/permission proposal for foreign warships concerns territorial straits; it must not be generalized into an exclusive-economic-zone navigation rule. The separate committee records supporting other parts of Chubb’s argument remain unread.

## A bounded India comparison: follow authority and leverage together

Saheb Singh Chadha’s [*Negotiating the India-China Standoff: 2020–2024*](https://assets.carnegieendowment.org/static/files/Negotiating%20the%20India-China%20Standoff%202020_2024.pdf), Carnegie India, December 2024, uses paired official statements and Indian stakeholder interviews. The read methodology describes strategic, diplomatic, and military channels and Indian pre-meeting mandates (pp. 4–7). Chinese officials were not interviewed. This supplies valuable evidence about Indian perceptions and processes while limiting claims about Chinese internal authority.

The selected sequence runs from the August 2020 Indian deployment around Pangong through subsequent senior meetings and the Pangong and Gogra disengagements (pp. 13–20). Military leverage and high-level political engagement change together. It would be premature to attribute movement exclusively to coercive pressure, a new mandate, or a restored relationship framework. Several accounts of the Moscow meetings derive from the same unnamed stakeholder, rather than independent witnesses (notes 77–85).

Joint statements document an agreed public formulation, not the complete private bargaining range. Separate statements may expose disagreement, but do not automatically prove deadlock. India’s insistence on linking broader relations to border conditions also warns against coding relationship framing as an exclusively Chinese tactic. The next step is to place each private offer, public formulation, deployment, and approval on the same timeline.

One source-quality correction is already warranted: printed p. 16 says the subsequent military talks occurred on 20 September 2020; p. 17 and the [official joint release](https://www.indembassysweden.gov.in/section/press-releases/joint-press-release-of-the-6th-round-of-senior-commanders-meeting-between-india-and-china/) give 21 September. The former date is in the rendered PDF, not an extraction error. Another meeting/release-date ambiguity at p. 18 remains flagged in the register, not silently corrected.

## Provisional implications for the book update

Using the [existing Solomon/Freeman claim ledger](../../book/context-claims.json), retain **S-C01** and **S-C18** as research disciplines: relationship purpose and substantive interests must enter the explanation. These readings do not validate every underlying historical claim. Recast **S-C19**, rigidity as a usual sign of leadership conflict, as an unproven diagnostic: unified resolve, local incentives, mandate limits, changed leverage, and deliberate pressure remain alternatives. For **S-C09–12** and **F09**, distinguish bureaucratic participation, operational initiative, reporting incentives, and final authorization. More actors and stronger central control can coexist.

For **S-C13, S-C15, F01**, test what broad principles actually do: unlock detailed terms, defer incompatibility, or support competing demands. For **S-C17**, distinguish invoking public sentiment from being constrained by it and from changing a foreign decision. Across all these propositions, the necessary comparison is between dated decision sequences under specified stakes and authority structures. None of the two complete works warrants a universal verdict about Chinese negotiating behavior in 2026.
