# Climate bargaining: what changed between Copenhagen and Paris?

Research date: September 15, 2026. Bounded new literature reading for Chapter 3; no chapter or shared reading-ledger edits. Source IDs and preserved originals: [register](climate-change-mechanisms-register.json).

The strongest contribution of these two readings is to explain how a different bargain became possible, rather than to supply another account of Chinese negotiators becoming more flexible. Domestic energy and industrial priorities could make some mitigation measures more attractive to Beijing, while the international agreement changed the obligations that accompanied a nationally chosen target. Neither development eliminated bargaining over responsibility, scrutiny or implementation. A second contribution concerns the other delegations: trust in the presidency and cooperation across negotiating groups helped determine whether a compromise worked beyond the American–Chinese relationship.

The current Chapter 3 already makes the crucial distinction between a national target and reciprocal acceptance of a joint announcement. It also preserves Copenhagen's movement on consultation and analysis. These readings support that account. They would strengthen it with a short passage on domestic feasibility before the November 2014 announcement, and one on procedural trust when the narrative reaches the French presidency. They do not justify replacing the existing participant accounts with a comprehensive causal explanation.

## The new readings and their actual evidence

**CCM-01: Maximilian Högl, “Kooperationsfaktoren in den Klimaverhandlungen: Eine vergleichende Analyse der Klimagipfel in Kopenhagen 2009 und in Paris 2015,” *Zeitschrift für Außen- und Sicherheitspolitik* 12 (2019), 357–378.** [Publisher article](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12399-019-00782-9). All 22 physical pages read, including seven numbered notes, references and three figures. The German article was first published online December 23, 2019. The subsequently corrected publication carries 2020 copyright: the [May 14, 2020 erratum](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12399-020-00794-w) concerns conversion to open access, not a correction of findings.

This is original process research, not just a review. Högl combines seven semistructured interviews conducted in November–December 2016 with coding of statements summarized in the *Earth Negotiations Bulletin* (ENB). Six interviewees belonged to official delegations: two EU negotiators, an African-country negotiator, an AOSIS representative, an LDC representative and a Western climate scientist accredited by Uganda. A climate-policy expert supplied the seventh interview. The six had attended at least one of the two conferences; the article does not say all attended both. No Chinese, Indian or Brazilian negotiator was interviewed. Interview questions moved from an open comparison to theory-guided questions, finally showing respondents the cooperation model under investigation. Transcripts were coded using MaxQDA; the published article does not provide them or an independent coding-reliability test. These features make the interviews useful evidence of participants' interpretations, with limited access to Chinese decision-making. Methods: pp.363–365 / PDF 7–9.

The network analysis adds a different observation: publicly reported support and opposition among delegations. It does not measure accepted concessions. Because informal Paris negotiations escaped this record, Högl adds the June, August and October 2015 Bonn meetings. Copenhagen is therefore compared with a longer Paris preparation-and-conference window. The major-actor threshold excludes the United States in Copenhagen; the Paris figure does not display China as a separate national node. A comparison of the diagrams cannot establish how Chinese behavior toward Washington changed. The graphs remain informative about the wider alignment of reported positions, provided that narrower object is retained. Methods and results: pp.364–365, 369, 373–375 / PDF 8–9, 13, 17–19.

**CCM-03: Lisa Williams, *China's Climate Change Policies: Actors and Drivers*, Lowy Institute Analysis, July 2014.** [Institutional repository PDF](https://www.files.ethz.ch/isn/182715/chinas-climate-change-policies.pdf). All 31 physical pages read: covers and publication notice, printed pp.1–27, all 104 notes, biography, ten figures and two tables. This is a complete policy-research report, kept separate from the scholarly-article count. Lowy identifies support from its Rio Tinto China Fellowship; the author disclaimer distinguishes the report from Australian government views.

Williams reports approximately 25 interviews in early 2014. The notes identify roles and dates for Chinese researchers, government advisers, a provincial official, NGO representatives, consultants, an energy state-enterprise member and a Beijing-based UN official. They are not 25 government negotiators. The report supplies neither a full sampling account nor transcripts or a coding method. Much institutional analysis also comes from earlier scholarship. Its special value is timing: the interviews and publication preceded both the November 2014 joint announcement and Paris. Expectations and disagreements recorded here were not reconstructed after those successes. The report concerns mitigation; its note 9 expressly excludes a separate examination of forestry policies. Figures include contemporary estimates and projections, which cannot be used as later realized outcomes.

## Domestic priorities changed the feasible package, not every obligation's cost

Williams gives reasons to treat domestic policy as more than background scenery. Government-advising researchers said the Twelfth Five-Year Plan's carbon-intensity target had been derived from energy-intensity targets. This is an attributed observation about how two targets related, not proof that either imposed no additional cost. It does show why a negotiating account organized only around foreign pressure can miss an important source of policy movement: an emissions measure may travel with an energy policy that Beijing already wants for other reasons. Printed p.14, note 63 / PDF 16 and 27.

Air pollution, industrial restructuring and energy security contributed different pressures. Williams's interview with an energy-enterprise member captures concern about citizens' demands for cleaner air; her treatment of restructuring links low-carbon sectors with other economic priorities. These connections do not make the policies interchangeable. The report itself notes that replacing coal use with coal gasification could improve local air quality while increasing greenhouse-gas emissions. A lower coal share, a carbon-intensity target and an absolute national carbon-dioxide peak describe different quantities and different commitments. Printed pp.16–18, notes 77–84 / PDF 18–20 and 27–28. The coal-gasification qualification rests on a cited secondary report; it is not a new plant-level finding by Williams. The parent researcher's separate examination of the original 2013 and 2014 plans should control any specific government target inserted in the chapter.

Nor did a national objective settle execution. Williams records provincial bargaining over target allocations and latitude in choosing implementation measures. Interviews also describe competing interests among different state-owned enterprises, rather than a single corporate bloc: coal producers, electricity generators and grid companies did not necessarily want the same policy. These accounts make domestic feasibility a political and administrative task. They do not identify who approved a particular international concession. Provincial allocation: p.10, notes 34–36; conflicting enterprise interests: pp.13, 15, notes 59–61 and 74.

This evidence can deepen the chapter's Xie passage. Expert estimates of a peak date supplied a range of possible commitments; existing domestic priorities could make parts of that range more attractive; the leadership still had to select a target and deal with distributional consequences. The practical question is not whether a negotiator can discover a hidden national preference with certainty. It is whether published plans, implementation responsibilities and the counterpart's explanations identify actions it already has reasons to undertake—and which additional obligation the proposed agreement would place upon them. Independent reporting, a timetable, a public association with Washington and an internationally chosen target need not have the same political price.

## Reputation: an observed concern, not a recovered Chinese decision rule

Högl's strongest China-specific reputation claim comes from an EU negotiator interviewed on December 2, 2016. The interviewee described Chinese concern about renewed blame and European leaders connecting climate with other bilateral business. This is evidence that a European participant perceived and sought to use reputational pressure. It supplies no identified Chinese instruction, lost transaction or exact concession purchased by that pressure. Treating the interview as proof that Beijing accepted a particular clause to protect its image would go beyond the record. Högl pp.372–373 / PDF 16–17.

Williams offers a complementary, earlier Chinese perspective. A researcher who was also a member of China's climate delegation described international image as a concern and negotiators as taking a harder position abroad than at home. Other interviewees emphasized respect, resistance to unfair pressure and the role international pressure had played in putting climate change on the agenda. A provincial official thought domestic action had made China less vulnerable to criticism. The collection supports coexistence of reputational concern and resistance to externally imposed burdens; it cannot rank them or show which determined the November announcement. Williams pp.18–19, notes 87–92 / PDF 20–21 and 28–29.

One disagreement is particularly valuable for avoiding hindsight. Williams reports a research-institute director's prediction that China might turn toward regional or bilateral alternatives if Paris failed. Note 93 immediately records the contrary expectation of a researcher on the Chinese negotiating delegation: the UN negotiations would remain the principal focus. The report has preserved uncertainty within its interview sample. A narrative claiming that a settled Chinese strategy led inevitably from Copenhagen to Paris would erase this evidence. Printed p.19 and note 93 on p.27 / PDF 21 and 29.

The chapter should therefore present reputation as a possible pressure channel whose operation needs an episode, an audience and a decision—not as an explanation inferred from the difference between conference outcomes. The already acquired Ramesh account gives a more concrete Copenhagen account of coalition members defending a compromise and avoiding responsibility for failure. Högl does not warrant saying that reputational pressure first became important after Copenhagen. His own comparison is too narrow, and the participant record already complicates that claim.

## Trust in the process could make confidential bargaining acceptable

The most distinctive addition from Högl is about the French presidency. An AOSIS adviser interviewed on November 17, 2016 recalled that an initially transparent, predictable process allowed the presidency to work less openly in the final days without losing participants' trust. An LDC senior adviser emphasized listening to smaller and vulnerable states. These are retrospective judgments from participants outside the American–Chinese pair. They identify a plausible mechanism: a delegation can accept temporary exclusion from a final drafting conversation if earlier conduct gives it reason to expect that its concerns will be represented. Högl pp.370–371 / PDF 14–15.

This is more useful than the general proposition that personal relationships help. It concerns the sequence of consultation and the legitimacy of the person managing the process. For someone organizing talks, the implied choice is when to move from wider consultation to a smaller negotiation, and what prior conduct could make the later result acceptable to those outside the room. The finding does not guarantee acceptance, establish that China shared these views or prove that French procedure caused China's substantive concessions. It nonetheless fits the chapter's existing recognition that a bilateral formula needed wider assent.

Högl also links greater cooperation to the hybrid design that left mitigation contributions nationally determined while creating international obligations of review. This argument draws substantially on Falkner and other existing scholarship rather than a newly recovered Chinese account. The chapter already has stronger primary sources for the legal distinction. Högl is useful for making its bargaining significance explicit: changing the obligation can change the distributive problem being negotiated. The exact treaty remains the authority on what was binding. Do not adopt the article's stronger suggestion that fairness disputes were thereby eliminated; the current Lima and Paris material demonstrates their persistence. Högl pp.371–372 / PDF 15–16.

Coalition evidence is similarly bounded. The ENB analysis records more cooperative connections across developed/developing groupings in the selected Paris window, but supporting another party's speech is not necessarily a costly concession. The article's account of Brazil joining the High Ambition Coalition draws on Brun's participant treatment, not an additional Högl interview. It is a lead to that source, not an independent confirmation of the underlying decision. Finally, Högl explicitly doubts that reciprocity ordinarily induces states to cross their red lines: it can reinforce partnerships within their limits. That qualification is directly relevant to the book's treatment of relationships. Högl pp.373–375 / PDF 17–19.

## What these sources cannot establish about central control

Williams describes central decision-making alongside ministry rivalry, expert advice and local discretion. Her account of foreign-affairs and development-agency competition at Copenhagen comes from Jakobson and Knox (2010), not a new interview about the summit. Her treatment of experts and officials jointly developing policy draws on Wübbeke (2013). A new interview does support the narrower point that expert agreement made advice more influential while government retained the decision. Printed pp.8–10, notes 21, 26–27 and 33.

Neither study isolates a change under Xi in the delegation of authority or the range of concessions a climate negotiator could make. Williams is a contemporary institutional snapshot; Högl has no Chinese interviews and does not observe the relevant instructions. They can explain why expertise, domestic support and procedural design might matter together. They cannot show that centralization created cooperation, that bureaucratic competition caused the earlier resistance or that expert rapport overcame a leadership prohibition. The new participant evidence and dated coordination instruments should continue to carry those narrower questions.

## Suggested chapter changes

The present chapter needs additions of argument more than additions of citations. Two short passages could do the work; both are proposed text only.

**After Xie's account of domestic target selection:**

> The domestic reasons for action were visible before the joint announcement. In interviews conducted in early 2014, researchers advising the Chinese government told Lisa Williams that the existing carbon-intensity target had been derived from energy-intensity targets. Cleaner air, energy policy and economic restructuring gave officials reasons to pursue measures that could also limit emissions. The interests did not always align: cleaner local air did not necessarily mean lower greenhouse-gas emissions, and provinces and enterprises still disputed how policy would be implemented. A counterpart could look for proposals that served those existing purposes. It still had to identify what additional commitment it was asking China to accept.

Suggested note: Williams, pp.10, 13–18, especially p.14 n.63, p.17 nn.79–81; limits and original-source checks above. This is a proposed synthesis, not Williams's own statement about the November negotiations.

**Following the paragraph on French facilitation:**

> The presidency's earlier conduct could matter when the room became smaller. An adviser to the small-island states later told researcher Maximilian Högl that the French had earned enough trust through a transparent, predictable process to work less openly in the final days. That recollection supplies a different reason to invest in relationships: countries outside a particular conversation needed confidence that their concerns would survive it. A compromise negotiated by a few would still need acceptance from the rest.

Suggested note: Högl, pp.370–371, interview November 17, 2016; AOSIS adviser's retrospective assessment, not evidence of Chinese instructions.

Neither passage is necessary to repeat if the parent integrates equivalent domestic-policy or presidency material from stronger originals. The contribution to the Solomon update is a more discriminating use of his propositions: understand the counterpart's purposes, authority and relationships, but also examine how the proposed agreement changes the obligations being accepted. These readings demonstrate interacting mechanisms and specific participant interpretations. They do not establish a national transformation from inflexibility to cooperation.
