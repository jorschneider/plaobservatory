## Mao's explanation and what supports it

Mao argues that stalled normalization explains the claims impasse: Washington wanted bilateral economic progress to demonstrate a closer relationship while postponing recognition and the Taiwan decision, and Beijing would not accept that substitute. His concluding distinction between apparent drafting disputes and an underlying political cause is stronger than a claim about background conditions.

The article's main base is American records—FRUS, released cables and Digital National Security Archive documents—with published participant materials. Its detailed chronology distinguishes agreement in principle from a completed settlement. Its central causal judgment relies heavily on American assessments. A Chinese-language historical argument is not necessarily an independent Chinese account of the decision.

Two exact source leads are valuable. Section III, paragraph **“1973年12月22日”**, cites Priscilla Roberts, ed., *Window on the Forbidden City: The Beijing Diaries of David Bruce, 1973–1974* (Hong Kong, 2001), **391**. The next paragraph, **“1974年6月14日”**, cites 范中汇主笔、刘海风副主笔, *将军、外交家、艺术家：黄镇传* (中央文献出版社, 2007), **631**. These cited pages were not acquired here. They remain leads, not replacements for the missing instruments.

## The original memorandum narrows the causal claim

The July 3 paper genuinely supports a normalization-linkage interpretation. At 680–681, its authors report that the Chinese had not moved on claims and cultural exchanges without progress on Taiwan. They ask whether a limited package could exchange claims settlement and other bilateral arrangements for troop withdrawal or a lower diplomatic presence on Taiwan. This is more discriminating than a binary assertion that no economic agreement was possible until full recognition: the contemplated exchange could fall short of normalization.

The paper also preserves alternatives that Mao's closing verdict compresses. At 677, the authors say the extent of Chinese flexibility can be learned only through direct negotiation. At 680, they expressly consider that Chinese leaders might not want normalization immediately, owing to their own domestic politics or regional concerns. At 681–682, they discuss possible pressures from China's left and military, while acknowledging no direct evidence of military opposition to dealing with Washington. Note 6 says they cannot draw firm conclusions about leadership changes' effect on bilateral relations. Later sections propose using Chinese leaders' earlier formulations and linking other issues to progress; these are American negotiating recommendations, not proof that the Chinese would accept them. The final recommendation asks Kissinger for a meeting and instructions. **This was an options paper, not an adopted bargain or a record of Beijing's internal choice.**

The November 1975 briefing, **CLA-I03**, printed 2, is an especially useful check. It treats normalization, domestic rivalries, trade's low priority, possible MFN linkage and other countries' claims as alternative speculative explanations. Its next page discounts the Chinese legal objection as apparently a pretext, but that too is State's assessment. Reading the two American documents together supports a consequential uncertainty: officials could identify what remained unacceptable in the draft without agreeing why Beijing would not settle. Mao advances one explanation for that uncertainty; he does not remove it.

This changes the chapter's emphasis, not its proposition verdicts. A failure of detailed bargaining need not be the product of subordinates defeating a leader's agreement. Nor does the record license treating the legal objections as camouflage. Washington's recognition policy, the value of a limited settlement to Beijing, actual language difficulties and internal politics can coexist. No new evidence here establishes their relative causal weight or turns Solomon's “most reliable sign” prediction into a disproved proposition.

## A material textual discrepancy

In section II, the paragraph beginning **“在3月21日的谈判中”** says the agreement's US claims **included** the earlier Chinese governments' unpaid bonds: its operative Chinese wording is **“包括旧中国政府发行的尚未支付的政府债券和国库票据等”**. This is an affirmative inclusion statement in the acquired HTML, not merely a possible ambiguity in English translation.

The reproduced contemporary cable **State 105075, May 31, 1973, paragraph 1 (CLA-I04)** quotes the March 21 statement as expressly saying that the draft exchange **“does not include”** such claims. Washington excluded them from that settlement while declining government espousal and leaving direct negotiations to bondholders and the foreign governments concerned. Mao's subsequent discussion of the October 24 instruction correctly separates the bonds from the settlement. An omitted negative or other transcription error is possible; without a print comparison, its origin is unknown. **Do not import the inclusion statement into the manuscript or infer from it that Washington demanded payment of those bonds within the proposed agreement.** The continuing exposure to claims, which Mao also discusses, is a distinct question.

## Roberts is a useful disagreement, not a surrogate Burr reading

Roberts's review stresses normalization, Chinese succession politics, US domestic limits and the availability of other commercial partners. She also questions whether the need to remain consistent with past anti-imperialist claims could by itself explain refusal, given the leadership's ability to make much larger policy reversals. At 4, she explicitly describes Burr's largely American documentary base and missing interdepartmental/Chinese records. At 6, she attributes a public-opinion explanation to Burr and challenges it. Those are **Roberts's characterizations and criticism of Burr**, pending reading the article itself.

The review's causal claims vary in strength: its characterization of Lin Ping's enjoyment of obstruction is an inference from reputation, not a disclosed decision; its later explanation of the 1979 settlement extends beyond Burr's 1975 endpoint. Neither should furnish unqualified narrative facts. The review's most valuable contribution here is to show that the rival explanation and its evidentiary limitations were already visible in the literature. Its complete reading is recorded separately from the research-article total.

## Proposed Chapter 1 insertion

**108 words; place before the October 1975 Habib return, after the November 1974 legal-formulation discussion.** The backward reference to the November briefing should remain explicitly dated. This paragraph does not require adding a scene or asserting a newly established Chinese motive.

American officials were also unsure what a settlement required politically. In July 1975, Habib, Gleysteen, Lord and Solomon argued that Beijing had withheld progress on claims without movement on Taiwan. They considered exchanging a settlement for further American withdrawals or a reduced diplomatic presence. Yet State's November briefing still listed several possible explanations: normalization, Chinese political rivalries, trade priorities, most-favored-nation treatment and other countries' claims. Mao Ruipeng later made stalled normalization the central explanation. That reading has contemporary support, but the American assessments do not establish Beijing's internal decision. They show why another improvement in the draft might be insufficient, even if the wording disputes could be resolved.[^claims-scholarship]
