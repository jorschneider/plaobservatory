# Critical reading: contract theory and the 2018–2019 trade negotiations

**Read 15 September 2026.** Angela Huyue Zhang, “The U.S.–China Trade Negotiation: A Contract Theory Perspective,” *Georgetown Journal of International Law* 51 (2020):809–866. The [complete official PDF](https://www.law.georgetown.edu/international-law-journal/wp-content/uploads/sites/21/2020/11/THE-U.S.-CHINA-TRADE-NEGOTIATION.-A-CONTRACT-THEORY-PERSPECTIVE.pdf) was read, including the abstract, body, acknowledgments and all 376 notes. Selected pages were also checked visually. [Preservation and reading register](../../sources/phase-one-contract-theory-register.json), source **PCT-01**. This is a full article reading, not a claim to have read every work in its bibliography.

Zhang offers a useful explanation of why a detailed promise to change a law can remain a poor guarantee of administrative behavior. Her strongest contribution is to distinguish what governments can specify, observe, verify and credibly punish. The article is less convincing when it moves from these constraints to categorical predictions that the agreement cannot be effectively enforced, or when it treats reported bargaining positions as established internal decisions. It should supply mechanisms and tests for Chapter 5, not an independent confirmation of the missing May 2019 draft.

## What the mechanism adds

The article organizes contractual incompleteness around unforeseen contingencies, enforcement costs and the costs of writing an agreement. Greater specificity can constrain discretion and improve monitoring, but it also makes agreement harder to conclude and adaptation harder after conditions change. The relevant comparison is therefore between different costs of commitment, rather than between a supposedly legalistic American culture and an inherently flexible Chinese one. Zhang applies existing contract theory to a historically narrated case; she does not estimate a new model or identify the effects of alternative drafts experimentally. (Pages 810–816.)

Her most productive distinction concerns legal changes and their implementation. National legislation is publicly observable. Informal pressure during licensing, administrative inaction and local enforcement effort can be much harder for a foreign government—or China's central authorities—to observe and verify. A promise to change legislation therefore does not eliminate discretion over implementation. The problem may arise from central policy, local incentives, organizational fragmentation or information barriers; a subsequent shortfall does not by itself identify which one. (Pages 829–840.)

This argument is not a denial of Chinese legal development. Zhang describes substantial improvements in intellectual-property institutions, judicial enforcement and private remedies. She also notes the limits of the anecdotal and survey evidence used to substantiate forced technology transfer. Her criticism concentrates on administrative behavior that is harder to scrutinize. The full reading therefore supports a more discriminating use than quoting the conclusion that legal concessions achieve little. (Pages 830–836, 864–865.)

The second mechanism shifts attention to the enforcing government. Threatening a sanction is different from finding it worthwhile to impose and maintain that sanction after a violation. Costs to US importers, consumers and firms, combined with Chinese retaliation, may undermine the credibility of punishment. Zhang connects this argument to domestic opposition to tariffs and the 1990s IP negotiations. These are reasons to investigate the credibility of particular threats; they do not establish that every costly threat lacks credibility. (Pages 845–851.)

The third mechanism concerns the political cost of the text itself. Public wording that appears to subordinate China can make a substantively acceptable bargain more difficult to announce. Zhang treats dignity and historical memory as costs of reaching agreement, drawing on behavioral theory, commentary and published reporting. This makes a useful distinction between resistance to the substance of a concession and resistance to how a concession is described. It does not measure the domestic audience's influence on a specific Chinese instruction. (Pages 851–860.)

The prior independent reading of TUS08 found six reported sources—three US government sources and three private-sector sources briefed on the talks—but no statement that Reuters possessed an authenticated draft. This reading traces Zhang's citations; it does **not** claim that the NYT, SCMP and WSJ articles were all newly obtained and independently reread. The missing draft, Chinese reservations and clearance sequence remain missing after reading Zhang.

There is nevertheless original interview material in the article. Page 824 n. 81 cites Zhang's April 2019 interview with an unnamed senior Chinese judge about burdens of proof in trade-secret disputes. That matters for her assessment of legal design. It is not an interview establishing Xi's instructions, a negotiator's authority or the cause of the May breakdown. Calling the article wholly devoid of original interviews would be inaccurate; using this interview as evidence of the negotiating room would be equally inaccurate.

## Qualifications that matter for the manuscript

**The article is not a clause-by-clause analysis of signed Phase One.** Its introduction acknowledges the January 15, 2020 agreement and predicts instability, but substantial passages retain the prospective framing of the 2019 negotiations. The conclusion expressly declines to predict whether a comprehensive agreement will be reached or its contents. Its statement on p. 860 that termination is the only US recourse must not be carried into a description of the final text. Article 7.4.4(b) provides a conditional route to suspension of obligations or proportionate remedial action, alongside withdrawal; Article 8.3 separately provides for termination. The absence of an independent adjudicator is real, but it is distinct from the absence of an agreed remedy. See **POA-EN/POA-CN** and the [independent clause review](../../manuscript/chapter-05-source-review-2026-09-15.md).

**The final text combines precision and discretion.** Articles 1.3–1.5 contain specific trade-secret commitments, including liability coverage and burden shifting; Article 1.34 permits each side to choose its implementation method. This is more informative than classifying the entire contract as rigid or flexible. It also qualifies the inference on pp. 825–826 that prior amendments would keep those subjects out of the accord: Article 1.5 still specifies burden-shifting duties, a subject Zhang discusses as already amended in 2019. This is overlap of subject and obligation, not a finding that the earlier law and final clause are identical in every respect, nor proof of who first proposed the language.

**Costly punishment is not necessarily incredible punishment.** Pages 841, 851 and 865 draw strong conclusions about ineffective enforcement and the limited value of a punishment mechanism. Domestic opposition and economic losses establish constraints, not the complete absence of political reasons to bear costs. The January tariff notice **POT-02** already shows that Phase One accompanied a reduction of a specified existing rate, rather than abandonment of tariff policy. A later test must distinguish retained tariffs, new tariffs explicitly responding to alleged nonperformance, and restrictions imposed for other purposes. None is interchangeable with an observed Article 7.4 enforcement episode. Nor does a lack of publicly disclosed appeals demonstrate no private consultations under a confidential procedure.

**The dignity argument needs an identified audience and decision.** A commentator's analogy to unequal treaties, a nationalist complaint about Liu He, or a reported negotiator's feeling of humiliation cannot establish how many people held a view, what decisionmakers believed, or whether that view bound their bargaining discretion. Conversely, the highly public final text with precise obligations shows that dignity concerns did not preclude this particular agreement. It does not show they were irrelevant. Compare wording changes, publication choices and the allocation of reciprocal rights while keeping the unobserved draft trajectory explicit.

**The 1990s material is primarily a synthesis of earlier research.** Pages 836–840 and 858–859 rely heavily on Mertha, Mertha and Pahre, Ryan and Zeng. Zhang credits substantial formal concessions and some consequential implementation, including factory closures, while arguing that administrative control remained incomplete. These passages should lead back to those works and the original agreements, not be counted as a fresh independent investigation. Two historical labels should not be inherited: the p. 858 reference to a 1991 MOU conflicts with the article's own 1992 chronology on p. 837, and its references to Wu Yi as premier on pp. 837 and 858 are erroneous. The underlying agreement's dated signature and title should control any retelling.

## Tests this reading makes possible

| Mechanism | Evidence that would support it in this case | Evidence or distinctions that could weaken it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Administrative conduct is harder to contract over than legislative change | Paired provision-level records showing formal amendment but identified implementation discretion, plus evidence explaining why monitoring or appeal failed | Demonstrated, durable improvement in the targeted practice; differences caused by the provision's scope or timing rather than unverifiability |
| Enforcement threats lose credibility because of costs to the enforcing side | An identified violation, documented consideration of a remedy, and decision evidence tying retreat to those costs | Sustained costly remedies; an explanation based on settlement, inadequate evidence or other strategic priorities rather than unwillingness to bear costs |
| Public form creates an independent obstacle | Comparable substantive terms accepted after changes in presentation or reciprocity, supported by contemporaneous instructions or participant evidence | Simultaneous material changes that explain acceptance; precise and public commitments accepted without the predicted blockage |
| Integration acts as a reciprocal commitment device | Specific exposed assets and decisions showing that their vulnerability induced restraint or performance | Exposure used for coercion, pressure to remove dependence, unequal vulnerability or security aims outweighing commercial interests |

Zhang's final recommendation—to use economic integration as reciprocal hostages—deserves this last test rather than automatic endorsement. The assets of private companies exposed to another state's jurisdiction are not necessarily deliberately deposited guarantees of interstate performance. The same interdependence that supposedly deters defection also raises the cost of punishment, which her preceding argument says weakens enforcement. Resolving that tension requires specifying which assets, which decisionmakers, which threat and which future gains matter. Her examples illustrate vulnerability; they do not establish a general causal effect on cooperation or the risk of war. (Pages 860–866.)

For the Solomon update, the usable conclusion is bounded: bargaining over specificity and implementation can reflect problems of information, delegation, credible punishment and public presentation. Those mechanisms offer alternatives to attributing every delay, reopening or preference for flexibility to enduring national behavior. They do not independently settle whether Solomon's claims hold, reconstruct the missing 2019 authorization history, or establish Phase One's performance through 2026. The manuscript can use this argument now while keeping those causal verdicts open.
