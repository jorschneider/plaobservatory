# Jost: crisis institutions, information and the EP-3 inference

Read 16 September 2026. This completes the article reading previously selected in EP319; the appendix remains a precisely bounded selection. [Source register](../../sources/jost-crisis-institutions-register.json). No manuscript, proposition assessment or shared reading ledger changed.

## What this changes

The useful addition is an explanation of why access to an empowered leader may fail to resolve a dispute: the account reaching that leader may not have been challenged by other parts of the government. That is different from an official being unable to authorize a concession. Chapter 4 should keep these two difficulties separate. The institutional explanation deserves more than a generic reference to opaque decision-making, but it does not warrant a verdict that Xi has made Chinese crisis bargaining uniformly less informed or less flexible.

Jost's article studies 26 security crises from 1949 through 2012 and distinguishes integrated, fragmented and siloed information arrangements. Its mechanism concerns bureaucratic access, opportunities to question information and incentives to report candidly. It is not a simple opposition between strong and weak leaders. The early-Mao comparison and the Jiang-era EP-3 case make that distinction central. The conclusion treats Xi-era implications as competing possibilities, not an observed extension of the preceding period. [JCI-01, pp. 47–63, 80–90; JCI-02, sections 2, 3.1 and 5.]

The practical inference is ours: seek a response to a concrete account and proposal through an authorized channel, while separating evidence that the message arrived from evidence that somebody checked it. A second channel is not intrinsically better; an inconsistent offer creates another problem. Nothing in the article supplies a reliable real-time test that tells a visiting negotiator whether resistance is a considered decision or the product of unchallenged reporting.

## How much weight the institutional result can bear

The appendix makes the evidence more discriminating than the label “centralized decision-making” suggests. Its meeting series covers 1950–1989, while the crisis sample continues through 2012. Later-period analysis relies more on organizational descriptions, published research and interviews. Accordingly, the meeting figure must not be described as a systematic record of Jiang's EP-3 deliberations, much less of Xi's. Membership lists establish representation; they do not by themselves establish who spoke candidly or whose account was believed. [JCI-02, pp. 3–13.]

The author explicitly addresses missing meetings and editorial censorship. His defense is that consistent missingness would preserve relative comparisons and that the sources do not appear to suppress military participation systematically. That is a reason to use the series, not proof that omissions were constant across the Cultural Revolution and later decades. The coding also imputes participants in formal meetings from membership lists when attendance is absent, and infers some informal-meeting agendas from the participants' functions. These choices are disclosed and intelligible. They limit how directly the resulting frequencies measure actual cross-checking. Intelligence participation is especially hard to observe. [JCI-02, pp. 8–12, notes 24–29.]

Crisis selection combines ICB, militarized-dispute data and substantive historical judgments. EP-3 is an included “near crisis”; the author supplies a reason for inclusion. The sample is neither all PRC negotiations nor a comparison of countries. An inference about security-information processing cannot become a demonstrated explanation of WTO, climate or commercial bargaining merely because those episodes also involve bureaucracy. [JCI-02, pp. 13–15.]

The three illustrative crises offer useful variation, including two under Mao, but do not isolate institutional design from every change in strategic circumstances. A key editorial discipline follows: present the information mechanism as an explanation with specific supporting observations, rather than using the typology to assign a hidden cause to every refusal. The appendix's personalism tests also have a small sample; failure to find statistical significance does not establish that personal power never matters. [JCI-02, pp. 44–50.]

## EP-3: follow the source chain rather than collapsing it

The inference has several distinct links. Evidence about an initial military report is not automatically evidence of what Jiang believed. Evidence of belief is not automatically evidence that it produced the detention policy. A later recognition of a weak bargaining position is not automatically evidence of which institution corrected the earlier account. These distinctions matter because the article's notes cite different kinds of material at each link: a published military biography; American diplomatic reporting; participant/analytical accounts; and the author's anonymous interviews. Reading the article does not give this project independent access to those interviewees or the biography's underlying records. [JCI-01, pp. 83–85, notes 171–183.]

The strongest newly recovered original is CIA information report **C00785245**, distributed **17 April 2001**, released **3 February 2020**. Its full subject is *Appraisal of the Situation: Chinese Leadership Perspectives on the Effects of the 1 April Collision of a U.S. EP-3 Aircraft and a Chinese F-8-II Fighter*. The source and date-of-information fields are redacted. The document warns that it is an information report, not finally evaluated intelligence. It must not be promoted into a finished intelligence-community judgment or a Chinese leadership minute. [JCI-04, p. 1 heading.]

Paragraph 1 explicitly begins after the crew's 12 April release and forecasts the planned 18 April talks. It reports **likely** recognition inside the senior Chinese leadership that the Chinese pilot caused the incident, while predicting continued demands for American responsibility. It also describes concern about possible American retaliation alongside an expectation that longer-term relations would not be severely affected. This supports reporting a contemporary American account of Chinese assessments—with its qualification preserved. It does not identify when a particular leader learned a fact or show that this information reached him before release was authorized. [JCI-04, p. 1, para. 1.]

Paragraph 3 is particularly useful for negotiating behavior. It says the Chinese recognized that Washington was unlikely to discontinue reconnaissance, but would press the demand to explore the American position while adjusting requests. Its claims about Jiang protecting his image and leaving the military to take a harder line are the report's interpretation; the redacted sourcing prevents an independent assessment of the informant's access. Even so, this is a valuable counterweight to reading every stated demand as an expected attainable result. It is also consistent with the possibility of tactical persistence after recognizing an unfavorable fact. Neither possibility establishes that the initial impasse was wholly tactical or wholly informational. [JCI-04, p. 2, para. 3.]

The report is therefore more helpful for distinguishing public demands from expectations than for proving the cause of the crew's release. It does not observe a specific interagency correction, revised briefing or leadership discussion. The article's late-learning explanation should remain a hypothesis informed by several sources, not a demonstrated sequence supplied by this one document.

## Coding and instrument cautions

1. **The fatality is not a replication error.** Main Table 1 displays one EP-3 death, whereas note 28 excludes a pre-crisis collision fatality from battlefield-cost analysis. Appendix p. 41 explicitly supplies “prior to crisis”; the released CSV has `cost=0` for EP-3. The apparent discrepancy is a table-presentation qualification, not evidence that the calculations included the death. [JCI-02, p. 41; JCI-03.]
2. **A missing assessment is not an accurate assessment.** Scarborough has insufficient evidence in the appendix and a blank assessment field in the CSV. Do not render every non-error row as demonstrated accurate forecasting. No statistical replication or criticism of the author's missing-value handling is claimed here. [JCI-02, p. 43; JCI-03.]
3. **The outcome verdict depends on the stated objective.** Appendix p. 41 codes stopping reconnaissance, obtaining responsibility, obtaining an apology for the collision and improving relations as four unsuccessful goals. Our chapter asks a different, narrower question about ending detention without an American admission of fault. It can describe the crew-release bargain as a functioning partial settlement without treating Jost's different outcome measure as disproved. Conversely, his aggregate failure score cannot erase the limited result that the parties actually obtained.
4. **Two letters must stay distinct.** The article's p. 83 assigns the settlement-language letter to Powell and Qian. The parent's newly recovered April 11 cable reportedly records both Powell–Qian authority correspondence and the Prueher–Tang settlement letter. This pass did not independently reread that cable; the new State-source review must control the manuscript's exact description. Do not correct the attribution by claiming that no Powell–Qian letter existed. The clause quoted/described and the particular instrument bearing it are the issue.

## Recommended bounded manuscript change

Chapter 4's existing phrase “offers an explanation” is defensible, but its following statement can sound more settled than the source chain warrants. A sharper formulation, with the hypothetical and practical consequences separated, would be:

> Jost identifies a different obstacle from lack of authority: military and diplomatic reporting could reach the leadership without being tested against each other. His EP-3 account makes that a plausible explanation for the initial impasse. It does not show which new information, if any, unlocked release. An ambassador confronting an uncompromising demand still has to distinguish a decision his counterpart has made from an account that has not been challenged.

Use the actual State sequence, once independently checked, to carry the release narrative. Replace the stale note's “unacquired” blanket language with the newly established scope, and call C00785245 an unevaluated CIA information report. Keep the pre-release chronology qualification. For the Xi verdict, the gain is a better causal question—whether stronger central control produces broader, more candid information—not a newly established answer. No assessment status should be changed merely because this study offers a plausible institutional mechanism.
