# Relationships, interests and the choice of negotiating rules

**10 September 2026.** Two complete Chinese-language readings add useful challenges to a simple cultural account of Chinese negotiation. Liang Xiaojun treats the relationship itself as a possible interest. Tu Xinquan and Shi Xiaojing argue that negotiators should change institutional formats to overcome deadlock. Neither article establishes what Chinese leaders privately instructed negotiators to do. Reading them alongside Chinese, American and Indian negotiating submissions reveals both substantive disagreement and selective procedural convergence. The [evidence register](chinese-negotiation-and-institutions-evidence.json) records versions, page coverage and primary-source checks.

## Liang: a relationship can be something being negotiated over

梁晓君，〈外交谈判战略浅析〉，《国际政治研究》2008年第2期，printed **108–120**. The publisher catalogue instead gives 112–124; citations here follow the thirteen-page PDF's visible folios. All pages, notes, both tables and the figure were read visually because extraction was unreliable. [Publisher text](https://ccj.pku.edu.cn/Article/DownLoad?id=297793048&type=ArticleFile).

Liang's article is a general argument about diplomatic negotiation. It distinguishes competitive and cooperative strategies, but explicitly acknowledges that real negotiations mix them across stages and issues (112). Historical illustrations include competitive behavior by both Chinese and American negotiators. The article is not an official account of a uniquely Chinese method, and its Korean armistice and other historical examples principally draw on published accounts rather than newly disclosed negotiating instructions (112–114).

Its most useful intervention comes at 118, where note 7 directly cites Solomon's 1999 book, 31–32. Liang questions treating relations mainly as an instrument for obtaining other benefits. A continuing relationship can itself be an interest: preserving future cooperation may justify modifying a current demand. She also recognizes that means and ends can exchange places across successive negotiations. A relationship cultivated in one round can become a resource in another. This is a qualification of a mechanism, rather than a claim that relational pressure never occurs.

The cited Solomon pages really do emphasize drawing foreigners into relationships advantageous to China. However, Liang's criticism should not be extended into a claim that the entire book reduces diplomacy to manipulation. Solomon elsewhere makes tactical effects secondary to state interests and official discipline; his historical scope also distinguishes confrontation from the construction of cooperation. Those qualifications are preserved in **S-C01** and **S-C18** in the [baseline ledger](../../book/context-claims.json).

Liang includes a small original classroom coalition exercise, not just philosophical exposition. The article reports seventeen groups and discusses bargaining outcomes and participants' explanations (115–116). Its students were not a sample of professional diplomats, and the account does not establish controlled assignment or furnish a replication dataset. The exercise can illustrate how preferences over partners complicate a material payoff calculation. It cannot measure Chinese national behavior, or establish that cultivating relationships causes better diplomatic outcomes. The figure and table also contain small internal inconsistencies; no numerical estimate from that exercise is used here.

Her account includes limits to cooperation: domestic audiences may punish compromise, reciprocity is uncertain, and negotiators need to judge their counterpart's intentions (120). It would therefore misread her to equate the cooperative strategy with confidence that goodwill will be reciprocated. Her treatment of face as a general diplomatic interest (119) likewise supplies a rival explanation to national exceptionalism, not proof that cultural differences have no effect.

**Our analytical use:** code a relationship as a possible object of bargaining, an instrument, or both. Evidence for the first should include a costly adjustment made to preserve a specified future benefit. A banquet, an invocation of friendship, or a flattering description of a leader does not decide among these explanations. In the India case, the key question becomes what each government required before expanding the wider relationship—not simply whether the leaders appeared friendly. An additional test would compare concessions by officials with similar authority but different investments in a relationship; the present cases do not supply that matched sample.

## Tu and Shi: changing the forum is part of negotiating strategy

屠新泉、石晓婧，〈重振WTO谈判功能的诸边协议路径探析〉，《浙江大学学报（人文社会科学版）》51(5), **211–226**, September 2021; online 12 August. DOI: **10.3785/j.issn.1008-942X.CN33-6000/C.2021.01.154**. The full article, footnotes, 28 references and English abstract were read; consequential pages were visually checked. The institutional server's 2025 folder is not the publication date. [Institutional full text](https://ciwto.uibe.edu.cn/docs/2025-06/d355aacc923447b0a67bf3f077c07583.pdf).

The authors explain deadlock through membership diversity, domestic adjustment costs and the difficulty of reaching a broad package. Their proposed response is to negotiate among willing members on a narrower range of issues while retaining a route into the WTO system. They distinguish arrangements that extend benefits to nonparticipants from agreements that bind or benefit only participants. Their recommendations include clearer procedures, openness to later participation and protections for developing members (211–223).

This is institutional and normative analysis, not a reconstruction based on private instructions or original negotiator interviews. It is especially useful as evidence of an available Chinese scholarly argument for procedural flexibility. It is weaker as proof that plurilateral negotiations are generally less political than regional agreements, or that outsiders' losses are negligible. Those are consequential propositions requiring comparisons of actual rule content, adjustment costs and excluded interests. The authors themselves recognize unresolved questions about the legal incorporation of joint-statement outcomes; their proposed governance arrangements should not be described as rules already adopted by the WTO.

A source check also changes one element of the article's US–China contrast. At 224, the authors attribute the **2020 Trade Policy Agenda and 2019 Annual Report** to the Biden administration. The cited document is a Trump administration report. Its title page names Robert Lighthizer, and printed 19 explicitly identifies the administration and advocates arrangements enabling more effective plurilateral agreements. Printed 15 describes US engagement in the e-commerce initiative. This corrects the attribution and complicates a clean contrast between Chinese institutional engagement and American abandonment; it does not establish that the United States consistently supported every WTO function. [USTR report, title page and printed 15, 19](https://ustr.gov/sites/default/files/2020_Trade_Policy_Agenda_and_2019_Annual_Report.pdf).

**Our analytical use:** separate a preference about *who negotiates and under what decision rule* from a preference about *what rules the negotiation should produce*. Governments can agree on a smaller negotiating group and disagree sharply on data regulation, subsidies or development exceptions. Counting participation, or describing a country as pro- or anti-multilateral, loses that distinction.

## What the primary negotiating positions show

China's **2019 WTO reform proposal**, WT/GC/W/773, already combined consensus language with a call for pragmatic flexibility in developing new rules (1.10–1.11). It supported e-commerce negotiations and advancing investment-facilitation discussions, while specifying boundaries: its e-commerce priorities emphasized trade in goods and related services and preserved regulatory interests in security, privacy and data (2.20–2.25). The communication expressly reserved the ability to submit further positions. It is a negotiating proposal, not a final agreement. [Complete eight-page Chinese submission, 13 May 2019](https://docs.wto.org/dol2fe/Pages/SS/directdoc.aspx?filename=q:/WT/GC/W773.pdf&Open=True).

The **February 2026 Chinese position paper**, WT/GC/W/989, makes the procedural formulation more explicit. Paragraph 8 supports decisions proceeding at different speeds and flexible plurilateral initiatives, alongside consensus and inclusive safeguards. Paragraph 9 is open to discussion of subsidy rules and industrial policies while preserving development policy space and respect for different economic systems. These paired clauses matter: an offer to discuss industrial policy is not acceptance of another government's preferred discipline. The two-page document was read completely. Its date is 18 February 2026, with a communication dated the 17th; the ministry hosting path contains an earlier folder date. [Chinese position, paragraphs 7–10](https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/cms_files/filemanager/676898164/attach/202511/5ccc1f093ee54112b5b6b107a5e442fc.pdf).

The comparison supports a bounded claim: procedural flexibility was already part of China's stated WTO position in 2019 and remained present in 2026. It does **not** demonstrate a new conversion in 2026, the adoption of the proposed reforms, or what concessions China would accept to secure them. Nor can an official statement by itself distinguish learning from an adjustment to greater economic weight and changing coalitions.

A contrasting primary text prevents treating the developing-country grouping as a single negotiating position. **India, Namibia and South Africa's April 2021 communication**, WT/GC/W/819/Rev.1, challenges the incorporation of joint-statement outcomes without the required multilateral procedures. It distinguishes changes to schedules from changes to rules, questions using the Information Technology Agreement and telecommunications negotiations as precedents for new disciplines, and worries about diversion from agriculture and development mandates. It also contests the mandate for the negotiations themselves, including the parallel domestic-regulation and e-commerce processes (annex 29–38); its objection is not confined to incorporating a finished agreement. Its main text also identifies possible routes forward, including consensus for an Annex 4 agreement or amendment of the governing rules. It does not simply prohibit informal discussion among willing countries. All eight pages, the explanatory annex and notes were read. [Communication, main paragraphs 1–13 and annex 19–39](https://docs.wto.org/dol2fe/Pages/SS/directdoc.aspx?filename=q:/WT/GC/W819R1.pdf&Open=True).

This is the sponsors' legal and institutional argument, not an adjudicated ruling. It nevertheless supplies a real counterposition to the practical and legitimacy case advanced by Tu and Shi. China and India can share concern for development and policy space while preferring different ways of conducting particular negotiations. Likewise, the United States and China could both participate in e-commerce discussions while seeking different rules: compare the 2019 Chinese priorities with USTR's 2020 emphasis on rules concerning data flows and source code. Coalition membership should be recorded by proposal and issue, not inferred from a general geopolitical label.

## Implications for the update

These readings strengthen the case for revising the book around **interests, authority and institutional choice**, while retaining its observations about relationships as hypotheses to test. Liang supplies an alternative interpretation of why preserving a relationship can matter. Tu and Shi supply an explicit account of choosing negotiating formats to make agreement possible. The primary texts show governments making those procedural choices in public, with substantive reservations attached.

That raises questions identified by Freeman about institutional experience (**F07**), reduced Foreign Ministry coordination (**F09**) and counterpart-specific practices acquired through repeated interaction (**F13**). These documents do not establish reduced ministry coordination or the acquisition of particular substyles. Nor do they independently prove that individual Chinese negotiators learned a new skill or that Xi-era control erased institutional specialization. Those questions need personnel, mandate and decision evidence. Nor should detailed WTO submissions be used to disprove **S-P07**, which concerns exploratory indirection in particular exchanges: a detailed published proposal can coexist with indirect private probing.

The revision suggested here is concrete. A modern chapter should ask what future relationship the parties value, which institutions can alter their obligations, how negotiators choose among forums, and which substantive issues they reserve. It should use “Chinese negotiating behavior” to identify whose conduct is being studied, rather than let that label explain the conduct before the records are examined.

## Coverage

The two scholarly articles are complete readings of the versions specified above. The 2019 and 2026 Chinese communications and the revised April 2021 Indian–Namibian–South African communication were read in full. The 2020 USTR report was read only at its cover/title pages and printed 15 and 19. Solomon's printed 31–32 were rechecked against the supplied book, alongside the prior book audit. No full audit of the agreements, reports or historical works cited by these authors is implied. Full source copies and rendered pages remain outside Git.
