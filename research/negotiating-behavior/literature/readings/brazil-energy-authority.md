# Zhang and Chen: Brazil, coordination and the limits of an authority claim

Research completed 15 September 2026. This is a critical reading and a bounded primary-source check, not a new completed bargaining case. Source IDs and acquisition details are in [the accompanying register](brazil-energy-authority-register.json).

## Finding

Zhang and Chen give useful evidence that institutional expansion need not give working-level officials greater influence over strategic decisions. They do not reconstruct how Beijing authorized an oil company's offer to Brazil. The distinction matters because Taylor and Garlick use the article more specifically than its methods permit: they describe interviews with MFA officials involved in Brazilian oil deals, then present the original authors' analysis as though it were the officials' own statement.

The best supported addition to this project is a separation of three questions: who can propose cooperation, who approves its strategic direction, and who negotiates and authorizes a particular transaction. The article and the primary documents illuminate the first two unevenly. A Petrobras filing supplies concrete terms for the third, but does not connect those terms to Chinese internal instructions. It would therefore be wrong to use the successful transaction either to prove company autonomy or to prove comprehensive central control.

## What kind of evidence is this?

The authors call their exercise descriptive and preliminary. Their institutional framework distinguishes strategic, managerial and technical layers, asking whether rules, coordinating bodies and repeated exchanges help sustain a partnership. The empirical material combines public documents with six convenience-selected, off-the-record informants. Three Chinese international-cooperation officials visiting Macau were interviewed for about an hour each; three Brazilian participants in late-2019 training activities supplied informal conversations. This is a study of bilateral institutional development, not a transaction-level study of oil bargaining. [BE-01, pp.340–346]

The interview list matters. The methods and bibliography identify Chinese officials from Commerce, Education, and Science and Technology; the Brazilian informants are officials associated with foreign affairs and foreign trade/economy, plus a tourism-company chief executive. No oil-company negotiator is identified. Nor does the article supply interview transcripts, company instructions, a bargaining diary or a comparison of offers before and after an intervention. These omissions delimit the question the evidence can answer; they do not show that central instructions were absent. [BE-01, pp.345–346, 360]

The informants' complaints are nevertheless substantive. Officials can be responsible for executing a programme while having little influence over the choice of programmes. An Education official's example concerns joint research laboratories and the exclusion of scientists and institutions from strategic decisions. Brazilian interlocutors report pressing for wider involvement, and a tourism executive describes being referred by the Macau Forum to Brazil's embassy in Beijing. These accounts identify a possible gap between having an institutional contact and obtaining a voice in decisions. They do not reveal the permissible concession range of CNPC, CNOOC or Sinopec. [BE-01, pp.356–357]

There are two further limits to the argument. First, the institutional sequence starts well before Xi: the strategic partnership, COSBAN and the first action plan cannot establish a distinctive Xi-era change. Second, continuity through Bolsonaro's initial hostility is consistent with an institutional contribution but does not isolate it. Economic dependence, domestic interests and presidential calculation remain alternative explanations for that continuity. A functioning committee can preserve an opportunity to negotiate without having caused the resulting agreement. [BE-01, pp.346–355, 357–359]

## The citation chain needs correction

Three separate issues should not be collapsed into a general accusation of unreliability.

1. **Informant identity is internally inconsistent.** At pp.349 and 356, the article labels Anonymous 1 as an MFA official. The methods at pp.345–346 and the reference entry at p.360 instead identify the relevant official as Ministry of Commerce. The evidence does not permit us to choose which label is correct. Attribute the centralization observation to an anonymous Chinese official whose affiliation is inconsistent in the article.
2. **Oil-deal responsibility is not established.** Taylor and Garlick p.202 characterize the interviewees as MFA officials involved in Brazilian oil deals. Zhang and Chen identify international-cooperation experience with Brazil, not participation in the negotiation of an oil contract. The oil-auction paragraph at p.348 is a different piece of the article; proximity does not connect it to the later interview testimony.
3. **Quotation and interpretation have been merged.** On Zhang and Chen p.356, the short quotation attributed to Anonymous 1 ends before the sentence beginning “It is the top leaders”. The following sentences are the authors' unquoted analysis. Taylor and Garlick introduce that passage as what the officials said and print it as a block quotation. Its correct evidentiary status is the original authors' synthesis, not a verbatim interview transcript. [BE-01, pp.345–346, 348–349, 356, 360; BE-02, p.202]

These corrections do not reverse the original finding into decentralization. They reduce the confidence and specificity of the inference. The study still contains reported working-level dissatisfaction and a documented institutional hierarchy. It cannot carry the stronger claim that testimony from energy negotiators establishes centrally fixed company offers. Any existing use of Taylor and Garlick that depends on that particular claim should be qualified at the source-chain level.

## Primary checks: a route for proposals is not a concession mandate

The 2010–2014 Joint Action Plan is a stronger source for formal routing than either article's diagram. The text is dated and effective on 15 April 2010; the embassy posted it on 22 April. Article 3 gives COSBAN the main decision-making role within its competence, while expressly directing subcommittees to identify new areas and propose ideas. It also establishes reports, focal-point review and recommendations. Annex I places the energy/mining contact at NDRC's Department of Foreign Capital and Overseas Investment and Brazil's mining/energy ministry. Article 6 addresses implementation problems and company participation. [BE-03, Articles 3, 6, 15; Annex I]

This structure makes a blanket inference that all proposals originate at the top especially precarious. A public mandate to submit proposals and an informant's sense of exclusion from strategic decisions can coexist: proposing is not approving. We have neither a record showing a subordinate proposal winning approval against a superior's preference nor a record showing the hierarchy rejected it. The action plan identifies a route a negotiator might use, rather than proving how much room any participant had to bargain.

NDRC's report of the 17 May 2019 energy/mining video meeting demonstrates that the route was used. It lists relevant Chinese and Brazilian agencies, embassies, companies, financial institutions and industry alliances as participants who spoke. The report identifies discussion of mining, oil and gas, electricity and renewables. It gives no concrete proposal, price, instruction or decision. It is evidence of coordinated access and expressed input, not evidence that company preferences prevailed or that company representatives merely recited centrally settled terms. [BE-04, complete body; published 20 May 2019]

The two records also warn against turning an organogram into a current contact list. The annex describes the agreed 2010 architecture. The May 2019 meeting confirms activity at that historical point. Neither document verifies the names, titles or competence boundaries relevant to a 2026 negotiation.

## A concrete transaction: Búzios without invented Chinese instructions

The article mentions Chinese participation in Brazil's November 2019 oil auction but does not reconstruct its bargaining. A narrow check of Brazilian originals makes the commercial problem visible. The energy ministry's results table records a winning Búzios consortium of Petrobras, with 90%, and CNODC Brasil and CNOOC Petroleum, each with 5%. It records a fixed signature bonus of R$68.194 billion and a 23.24% offered share of surplus oil. The ministry explains the auction's variable as the state's share of profit oil after specified cost and royalty deductions; 23.24% must not be described as a fraction of all gross production. Its opening paragraph misnames the second awarded area as Atapu, while its results table says Itapu; only the Búzios row is used here. [BE-05, results and preceding explanation]

The auction did not settle every issue. Petrobras's 11 June 2021 SEC-filed announcement describes negotiations beginning after the 6 November 2019 award. Their subject was coexistence of Petrobras's earlier transfer-of-rights contract with the new production-sharing contract. Petrobras, its Chinese partners and PPSA defined development assumptions within the stated Brazilian ministerial parameters and agreed allocations between the two contracts. The announcement also describes compensation owed to the earlier contract and a cash contribution from the Chinese partners. Effectiveness remained subject to ANP approval and payment. [BE-06, complete announcement and table]

That sequence supports a modest but useful transaction map:

| Question | What the acquired originals establish | What remains unknown |
|---|---|---|
| Initial offer | Búzios consortium interests, fixed bonus and offered profit-oil percentage in the 2019 result | Internal selection of the Chinese bid and alternatives considered |
| Constraint | Existing transfer-of-rights entitlement had to coexist with the new contract; subsequent terms required Brazilian approval | Chinese internal approval conditions and any privately imposed ceiling |
| Negotiated decision | A June 2021 coparticipation agreement with development assumptions, allocation and compensation provisions | Who first proposed each term and which party conceded how much |
| Status at the checked endpoint | Signed agreement; effectiveness conditional on ANP approval and payment | Actual satisfaction of those conditions, which this bounded check does not independently establish |

The denominator changes across the documents. Five per cent was each Chinese company's interest in the new surplus-volume consortium, not five per cent of every right in the entire reservoir. The 2021 filing estimates contract allocations of 26.2% and 73.8% on volumes to be produced from effectiveness, while reporting each Chinese partner's resulting reservoir participation separately at 3.667%. Its estimates were tied to an assumed 1 September 2021 effective date and subject to adjustment. These are signed conditional terms reported by Petrobras; they are not a current ownership statement. Exact figures and calculation boundaries are retained in BE-06 rather than used to infer undocumented concessions.

For a negotiator, the episode poses an actionable sequencing problem. An award can create the need for another negotiation because previously granted rights, compensation and regulatory consent still have to be reconciled. A diplomatic committee may help convene the relevant institutions, yet obtaining a place in that committee does not substitute for resolving the commercial and regulatory dependencies. This is an inference from the documented sequence, not a claim that COSBAN caused the Búzios agreement.

## Use in the Solomon update

Use this reading to make the authority question more exact, not to add another example of Chinese rigidity. A counterpart can have permission to discuss cooperation, responsibility to implement it, and no authority to approve the transaction being sought. Conversely, a central strategic objective leaves open how much discretion companies retain over implementation. The present record cannot identify where that discretion ended in Búzios.

The most useful practical inquiry is consequently tied to a decision: which office owns the next approval, what document or evidence does it require, and which unresolved obligation makes that approval necessary? Those questions do not require the foreign party to discover a secret mandate before proceeding. They do require keeping observable acts—participation, proposal, signature, regulatory approval and effectiveness—separate.

Brazil adds a large-country comparison in which the receiving state has its own commercial entitlements and regulatory authority. It cannot establish the flexibility of Chinese negotiators facing US security sanctions, a military crisis or WTO litigation. Its institutional history also cannot measure a Xi effect. The appropriate addition to the manuscript, if any, is a short distinction between coordination and transaction authority, backed primarily by the action plan and company filing. The article is more valuable as a carefully qualified explanation of participation than as evidence of a centrally scripted oil bargain.
