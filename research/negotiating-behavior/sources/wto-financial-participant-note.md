**Author and retrospective setting.** The biography identifies Deng as a People's Bank of China (PBOC) official from 1992, later a China Banking Regulatory Commission (CBRC) official from 2003. He says he participated in dozens of financial-sector multilateral and bilateral talks during 1992–2006. His responsibilities initially covered banking, securities, and insurance; subsequent institutional changes transferred the latter two elsewhere (pp284, 287–289). The chapter says it was written for the twentieth anniversary of accession, suggesting a 2021 commemorative context; it supplies no exact composition, interview, or speech date. This is a participant's retrospective contribution published in 2023, not contemporaneous minutes or an independently verified official chronology.

## A concrete account of mandates and coordination

Deng describes a document-driven chain: foreign governments solicit requests from their banks in China; Geneva negotiators turn those requests into WTO-format demands; China's Geneva delegation sends them to MOFTEC; MOFTEC forwards banking demands to the competent regulator. That regulator studies domestic banks and foreign regulatory practice, prepares high/middle/low offer options, and submits a report and offer through its hierarchy for authorization (pp289–290). He says circulation was restricted even within a ministry outside the reporting line, and drafting, printing, and custody were tightly controlled. Technical continuity favored relatively stable negotiating personnel.

This substantially qualifies a generalized aversion to legal detail: Deng emphasizes exact wording, WTO schedules, specialized rules, and a large accumulated documentary record (pp290, 292). It also supplies a mechanism behind apparent rigidity. Normal sectoral authority came from the responsible ministry's leadership; he specifically identifies the PBOC governor as authorizing banking offers. MOFTEC coordinated the timing and combination of concessions, but the final bargain could require concessions beyond departmental mandates. Deng explicitly presents that as a political-authority problem above the technical negotiating level (p291).

A separate undated anecdote illustrates improvisation within limits. After receiving unexpected nonbank-finance demands, Deng and Zhang Xiangchen prepared responses overnight under existing authority. Long Yongtu called a ten-minute recess to absorb their briefing before replying (p292). This is a more specific explanation of delay than attributing every pause to psychological pressure.

## November 1999: observable propositions to corroborate

Deng says the Chinese delegation included representatives of fourteen departments; he and Wang Zhaoxing represented PBOC. He attributes final coordination to Jiang Zemin and Zhu Rongji, describing Zhu's late-night meetings and direct intervention at the negotiating site (p293). This is the participant's leadership account; it does not expose each leader's private preferences or prove a particular ministry opposed a particular concession.

His banking chronology supplies unusually precise leads:

- **November 10:** Under existing authorization, China offered auto credit by foreign banks alongside resident RMB business after five years, and buyer credit by foreign enterprise-group finance companies when comparable Chinese companies could provide it, excluding interbank-market participation (pp293–294). He dates an earlier auto-credit commitment to **April 23**, which should be checked against the April draft sequence.
- **November 13:** New authorization from a State Council meeting permitted revised technical consultations. Deng describes adjustments to city-opening dates, wording of the two- and five-year customer schedules, and immediate auto-credit access for foreign enterprise-group finance companies as nonbank institutions (p294).
- **That evening:** He says a separate request to Governor Dai Xianglong secured a financial-leasing assurance: future regulations would not impose different market-access or national-treatment restrictions on foreign and Chinese firms (pp294–295).
- **EU comparison:** After the American agreement, Portugal sought to accelerate Zhuhai's RMB opening from three years to one. Deng says he obtained PBOC approval and agreed, judging it not a substantive concession (p295). That judgment belongs to the negotiator and needs comparison with the schedules and affected banks.

Deng describes American luggage, departure threats, and walkouts as tactics that the Chinese recognized and that did not influence them (p293). This is a clear claim to test against American accounts, private Chinese instructions, and offer timing. It cannot establish the tactics' ineffectiveness merely because he asserts it. His account of Chinese exhaustion (p296) also complicates any assumption of effortless Chinese control.

## Implementation: rules became another arena of bargaining

The latter half traces domestic training, regulatory revision, and responses to WTO-member questions (pp297–314). Deng describes borrowing foreign prudential-regulation practices, including a November 2005 U.S. study visit, to justify differentiating locally incorporated banks from branches when opening RMB retail business (pp303–305). He credits that approach with limiting competitive disruption while fulfilling commitments. Those are his causal and compliance claims, requiring independent examination.

The paired foreign objections and Chinese replies on capital requirements, operating funds, and interbank borrowing (pp306–309) are valuable leads for locating WTO transitional-review records. They show continued legal and technical argument after agreement. They do not by themselves prove compliance, bad faith, or agreement reopening.

The next acquisitions should be the dated offer schedules, November 1999 PBOC and State Council instructions, Dai's approval record, MOFTEC coordination papers, the November 24 domestic bank briefing, the 2005 study report, and WTO member questions/replies. The chapter also supplies names and institutional roles for targeted archival and interview preparation, without requiring speculative accounts of hidden factions.

**Quality flags.** Visual inspection confirms that the original printed page, not just the extraction, calls November 18 “two days” after signing, although the chapter's photo dates signing November 15 (pp294, 301). The original also invokes CBRC arrangements in discussing 2001, despite dating CBRC's establishment to 2003 elsewhere (pp289, 302). These are apparent internal source inconsistencies; independent records are still needed to resolve the intended meeting date and responsible regulator. The strongest contribution is the testable authorization chain and dated changes in offers, not the commemorative verdict that the entire process succeeded as intended.
