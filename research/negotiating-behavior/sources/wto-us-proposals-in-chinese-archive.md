# WTO accession pilot: U.S. closing proposals in the Chinese documentary collection

The reproduced American papers show detailed bargaining over equity, control, implementation dates, and safeguard duration between November 12 and 13, 1999. They also show the American delegation announcing a departure deadline and seeking direct intervention by Premier Zhu Rongji. These documents support tests of reciprocal bargaining and leadership access; they do not establish the final agreement or independently prove Chinese motives.

**Separate the source layers.** These are American-authored negotiating documents reproduced in a later Chinese official collection. The letters carry U.S. embassy letterhead, dates, addressee, and signature. The collection supplies selection, arrangement, Chinese headings, translations, and some explanatory notes. The English papers document positions advanced by the American side; their inclusion in a Chinese archive does not turn their allegations into Chinese admissions. The English item 08 has no visible date on its inspected pages; the November 13 afternoon attribution comes from the Chinese heading, supported by the text's reference to a Chinese offer made on November 13.

## The deadline and requested intervention

The **November 12 letter** reports that Shi had requested an American response and indicated flexibility on elements of his proposal. McCahill describes the American view that China had misunderstood its position and that talks had reached an impasse. He asks for direct talks between Barshefsky and Zhu to resolve the bilateral package. This is an American representation of the state of talks, rather than a neutral transcript of Shi's words. The letter ties discussion of sensitive equity issues to satisfactory resolution of product-specific safeguards and non-market-economy antidumping provisions. Printed p. 1067.

The next page announces that Barshefsky's party would leave its hotel at **8 a.m. Saturday**, requests an answer about a Zhu meeting by **7 a.m.**, offers postponement if Zhu can meet Saturday, and says the delegation cannot remain beyond early Sunday morning. This is explicit American deadline pressure and a conditional willingness to extend the visit. The pages do not establish whether the stated scheduling constraint was genuine, tactical, or both. Printed p. 1068.

The **November 14 letter** again declares an impasse after overnight talks. McCahill alleges that the Chinese side had reopened settled issues and introduced new ones, questions whether it is prepared to conclude before the delegation's departure that morning, and asks urgently for another Zhu–Barshefsky meeting. He invokes the shared intent of the senior leaders and Zhu's reported undertaking to meet again if a deadlock arose. Those are documented American arguments and allegations. They are not independent proof that issues had objectively been settled, that reopening them was improper, or that the Chinese team lacked intent to agree. Printed p. 1094.

These letters establish a recurring negotiating move: an American claim of deadlock, a proposed departure, and an appeal to higher Chinese authority. They cannot by themselves establish that Chinese negotiators engineered the impasse to stage a senior intervention. They are useful for testing `S-P23` and `S-P24` precisely because they show the American side actively shaping the schedule and requesting that intervention.

## What changed in the American papers, November 12 to 13

Both papers retain the April 10 schedules and texts except where a new position or option supersedes them. The November 13 paper also preserves items previously agreed in position papers. Consequently, **absence from the shorter November 13 list is not, by itself, withdrawal**.

The November 12 protocol section also conditionally withdraws the U.S. demands for a ten-year textile quota and a general safeguard. Their omission from the next day's enumerated issues should be read with the retention clauses, not automatically as a reversal. Printed pp. 1073 and 1087.

## Equity, control, and the danger of an overly simple concession story

The inspected U.S. papers do **not** support describing all the equity bargaining as a last-minute numerical retreat from 51% to 49% or 50%. No 51% requirement appears in these two closing papers. That does not show that the United States never sought 51% at an earlier stage; it bounds what these documents establish.

By November 12, the American paper already specified 49% for value-added/paging services within a year and 50% for life insurance on accession, while reserving later equity treatment for package discussion. The next day's paper explicitly separates **percentage ownership from management or operational control**. A 50% ceiling accompanied by control is not equivalent to a bare 50% ceiling. Nor are value-added/paging services, basic telecommunications, life insurance, and securities interchangeable sectors.

Likewise, the November 13 paper's NME expiry is **20 years**, whereas its product-specific safeguard expiry is **12 years**. Any account of a different final NME term needs the later agreement or negotiating draft; it cannot substitute a final number for the position actually printed here. The documents show an evolving, cross-issue package, not the final disposition of every term.

## Translation and presentation cautions

The bilingual presentation is useful but is not two independent sources. The Chinese November 12 version adds explanatory notes about April 10 securities, banking, film, and distribution provisions that are not present in the corresponding English closing paper. Those notes are a separate interpretive layer and need their own source check before being used as evidence of the April agreement. Printed pp. 1075–1077 versus English pp. 1072–1073.

Two visible discrepancies reinforce the need to use the English original for exact American terms. The November 12 English auto-maintenance line refers to China's **November 12** proposal, while the Chinese version on p. 1075 prints **April 12**. The November 13 English securities paragraph on p. 1088 specifies **49%** for the asset-management venture and a second **33%** figure for securities ventures; the corresponding Chinese paragraph on p. 1091 omits those particular numbers while retaining the separate 33%-at-accession request. These are observations about the reproduced texts, not allegations of deliberate distortion.

For the pilot, these sources provide direct, dated American offers and objections, plus later Chinese presentation of them. They strengthen tests of document specificity (`S-P05`), revised offers and deferred issues (`S-P12`), deadline ownership (`S-P23`), senior intervention (`S-P24`), and implementation bargaining (`S-P25`). They leave the Chinese response, ultimate compromise, causal influence of pressure, and actual implementation to be established by additional documents.
