# Normalization with a disagreement preserved: December 1978

**Primary-record check, 15 September 2026.** This bounded reconstruction independently reads a sequence underlying Solomon’s normalization-era framework. It examines December 4–15, 1978, with a December 1980 participant memorandum used as retrospective interpretation. The [register](../sources/normalization-1978-register.json) records complete readings, archive provenance and missing documents. These are published American records and an official Chinese version of the final communiqué, not parallel access to both governments’ internal deliberations.

The finding strengthens a qualified part of Solomon’s account while making the baseline more demanding: the governments could conclude an important political agreement while expressly preserving disagreement over a consequential issue. They distinguished diplomatic recognition, embassies, troop withdrawal, treaty termination, arms sales and public explanations. Later examples of separating bargaining objects therefore do not establish that the practice itself originated after normalization. What changes over time must be sought in the instruments, procedures and available choices.

## Before the senior meeting

Woodcock’s December 4 report records Han Nien-lung setting out China’s conditions and handing over a bilingual draft. Han opposed continued arms sales to Taiwan and refused a Chinese commitment to a peaceful resolution, while permitting the United States to express its own hope for one. He distinguished Chinese and American unilateral statements from their proposed common text. The report also records the announcement that Deng wished to meet Woodcock. Its editorial notes locate the separate Chinese draft and American instructions; neither full underlying document has been acquired here. [FRUS159, paragraphs 1, 10–17 and notes 2, 6.](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v13/d159)

On December 11 Brzezinski encouraged Chai Zemin to meet the January 1 target and explained the political usefulness of a senior Chinese visit. He also discussed a possible Soviet summit, provoking a Chinese question about linkage. The record shows Americans managing sequence, high-level access and political meaning. It does not establish that these appeals caused Deng’s next decision. Oksenberg’s covering note inferred that Beijing probably received a report before scheduling the meeting; the Chinese transmission and decision records are absent. [FRUS163, bilateral portion and source note 1.](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v13/d163)

## The objects of agreement moved separately

| Date and record | Observable position or change | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| December 13, Woodcock’s report of his meeting with Deng | Deng accepted the American communiqué draft as a basis while proposing anti-hegemony wording in either the joint text or separate statements. He accepted a four-month withdrawal period, distinct from the one-year treaty-notice period. | The printed report is not independent Chinese minutes. Recognition, withdrawal and the treaty did not share one effective date. |
| Same meeting | Deng conditioned acceptance of the treaty-termination wording on no new arms sales during the one-year period and avoidance of an Article 10 reference. He assigned detailed work to vice ministers and asked for another meeting after American instructions arrived. | Willingness to proceed was not acceptance of arms sales after 1979, nor proof that the Americans could accept every wording request. |
| December 13, Woodcock’s separate assessment | Woodcock inferred that Deng wanted rapid agreement, but explicitly warned against assuming Chinese permission for post-1979 arms sales. He requested instructions on the communiqué and unilateral statement. | The diplomat’s interpretation of Deng’s domestic exposure was an assessment, not a recovered Chinese internal decision. |
| December 14 | Woodcock reported agreement on the communiqué after changes distinguishing mutually agreed principles from the separate positions in the Shanghai Communiqué. Arms-sale wording still required clarification. | Agreement on the communiqué did not eliminate disagreement outside it. |
| December 15, further meeting | Deng opposed continued arms sales but agreed to proceed with the normalization schedule; he warned of a Chinese public response to American statements asserting continued sales. | This was no Chinese endorsement of the US position, and no promise that later public opposition would disappear. |
| December 15, Washington’s instruction | Brzezinski instructed Woodcock to explain that the United States would reaffirm its arms-sales position if asked, while explicitly acknowledging China’s non-endorsement. | The instruction is evidence of the US position and communication plan, not an agreed Chinese concession. |

Sources: [FRUS166, especially paragraphs 9–15, 26–35](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v13/d166); [FRUS167, paragraphs 3–10](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v13/d167); [FRUS168, paragraphs 3–10](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v13/d168); [FRUS170, complete six-paragraph report](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v13/d170); [FRUS172, complete instruction and source notes](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v13/d172).

The instructions and marginal comments are particularly valuable for avoiding a one-sided theory of authority. Carter’s recorded annotations favored accommodating the one-year sales pause but resisted an unrestricted anti-hegemony formulation and recognized that public explanations could not conceal every legal detail. Woodcock could recommend wording and assess Chinese flexibility while still requiring decisions from Washington. The eventual communiqué repeated mutually agreed Shanghai principles; it did not simply adopt every statement previously made by either party. [FRUS166, notes 10–18](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v13/d166); [FRUS168, paragraphs 3–5](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v13/d168).

Woodcock’s December 15 assessment likewise separated a clearly stated position from Chinese consent to it. He expected objections to future arms sales and thought their effect would depend on the broader relationship. Washington replied that Congress, the press and reassurance of Taiwan made silence impracticable. These were differences within the American process over presentation and risk, alongside disagreement across governments over substance. The sources permit examination of an actual message and response, rather than inferring domestic constraints from the completed communiqué. [FRUS169, complete report, especially paragraphs 3–5 and note 6.](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v13/d169)

The final text established relations effective January 1, 1979 and provided separately for embassies and ambassadors on March 1. It stated shared principles and the agreed description of future unofficial US–Taiwan relations without resolving the arms-sales disagreement documented in the closing exchanges. The official Chinese text and the English text transmitted in the December 15 message to Unger are versions of the common instrument, not independent accounts of how it was negotiated. [Chinese communiqué](https://www.fmprc.gov.cn/web/ziliao_674904/zt_674979/dnzt_674981/qtzt/twwt/zylswj/202206/t20220607_10699645.html); [FRUS171, paragraph 2 and enclosed communiqué](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v13/d171).

## What the retrospective explanation adds

Oksenberg’s December 1980 memorandum argues that repeated strategic consultations made normalization more attractive and that the NSC kept presidential support and interagency coordination intact. It also credits particular American officials and defends Brzezinski’s role. These are valuable participant propositions whose advocacy and retrospective character must remain visible. Its own account says Oksenberg did not know everything that occurred inside the White House during the closing days. Repetition of the contemporary cables in this memorandum is not independent corroboration of them. [FRUS327, “The Drive to Normalization,” and concluding assessment.](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v13/d327)

A small inconsistency reinforces the need to reconstruct rather than inherit the narrative: it calls the listed Beijing encounters six sessions while listing seven dates, including October 3. Its editorial cross-references lead back to the actual records. That discrepancy does not refute the strategic explanation; it prevents using the retrospective count as a ready-made bargaining dataset. Deng’s opening in FRUS166, paragraph 3, records his awareness of the December 11 exchange before the December 13 meeting. Receipt before the meeting was scheduled, and the retrospective claim that the exchange primed Deng for agreement, remain separate, unproved claims. The contemporary record establishes knowledge more securely than timing or causal effect.

## Consequences for the update

**S-P24, concrete drafting and senior intervention:** the sequence supports a narrower version of Solomon’s strong prediction in a core original-period case. His printed 149 calls senior intervention “the most reliable sign”; our “can signal” formulation is a qualification, not his exact wording. One successful closing sequence cannot establish predictive reliability or his face-based explanation. Deng resolved some matters, assigned others and recognized Woodcock’s need for further instructions. The signal concerned approaching normalization, not agreement on every US–China dispute. Much of the relevant record was private, so later declassification does not establish that outsiders could have used the signal at the time.

**S-P12 and S-P05, reservations and principles:** settlement coexisted with stated disagreement, and the location and wording of principles were themselves negotiable. The record supports preserving those distinctions. It does not establish the proposed face-saving mechanism or a general preference applicable to every later technical negotiation.

**S-P19 and S-P25, prior understandings and later demands:** subsequent Chinese opposition to arms sales cannot, merely because normalization occurred, be classified as withdrawal of assent to those sales. A historian must identify the particular shared commitment first. This check ends at the agreement and does not decide the later implementation record or the meaning of the 1982 communiqué.

The bounded result is a firmer historical baseline, not a completed Chapter 1. Priority gaps are the full successive communiqué drafts; December 12 and 14 American instruction texts; the full December 15 meeting transcript identified as cable 237; Chinese contemporaneous minutes and authorization; and a contrary original-period episode. The 1980 retrospective supplies useful leads to those records, not permission to fill their contents from its narrative.
