# Solomon, Chapter 3: The Process — annotated reading and research tests

This is a source-reading dossier, not a verdict on Chinese behavior in 2026. It follows Richard H. Solomon, *Chinese Negotiating Behavior: Pursuing Interests Through “Old Friends”*, Chapter 3, in the supplied 2005 printing of the 1999 edition. Its task is to make the chapter's propositions, evidence, explanations, and exceptions available for subsequent testing. The companion [process-claims.json](process-claims.json) contains 25 consolidated propositions, identified as `S-P01` through `S-P25`.

## Coverage and evidentiary status

The entire chapter was read sequentially in extracted page text: printed **pp. 57–156**, corresponding to PDF pages 75–174. The originally specified endpoint, p. 155, falls inside the final personality discussion; p. 156 completes it. Chapter 4 begins on p. 157. Reading included the running prose, the two tables, quoted exchanges, footnotes, short subsections without examples, and qualifications. The reading batches were pp. 57–69, 70–82, 83–95, 96–108, 109–121, 122–134, 135–146, 147–155, and 156. The annotations below preserve their sequence.

This is **full text coverage, not full visual verification**. No Chapter 3 page image was inspected in this pass. The separately supplied image of the equivalent process diagram in the Summary, printed p. 5, was inspected and confirms its four stages. Chapter 3's p. 58 and verbal-conventions table on p. 129 were read as extracted text. Footnote names and dates below are anchors **as cited by Solomon**, not claims that the underlying original memoranda have been independently retrieved or authenticated. Suspect dates and extraction artifacts are listed at the end rather than silently repaired.

The chapter chiefly draws on normalization-era U.S.–PRC encounters, especially the Shanghai Communiqué, diplomatic normalization, and the 1982 arms-sales communiqué. It includes memoir recollections, author recollections, interviews, press accounts, older adversarial encounters, and occasional commercial comparisons. These evidence types have different strengths. A memorandum can establish that words were spoken; it does not by itself prove the speaker's purpose, the instruction behind the words, or the effect on the other side. A retrospective impression of manipulation should remain distinguishable from a documented decision to manipulate.

Solomon often blends cultural interpretation with practical explanations: relationship obligations, face, and political language appear alongside limited communications, centralized authorization, bureaucratic resistance, geopolitical interests, and leadership conflict. The update should test these explanations separately. The presence of a practice does not establish its Chinese uniqueness or demonstrate that it caused a concession.

## Sequential annotations

### Framework and opening moves, pp. 57–75

**Process framework and Table 1, pp. 57–58 — S-P01.** Solomon organizes bargaining into opening moves, assessment, endgame, and implementation. The framework comes from experienced American officials' descriptions, illustrated by three major agreements. Treat it as a proposed sequence, not a law: the same episodes recur under several headings, and the endgame explicitly permits continued bargaining or failure. Counting each retelling as independent evidence would inflate support.

**Opening Moves, p. 59.** The opening may begin before participants recognize formal bargaining. Relationship-building encounters establish expectations later invoked at the negotiating table. This makes the start of a case an empirical choice: record exploratory contacts and visits, but distinguish them from substantive offers. Otherwise almost any prehistory could be retrospectively assimilated into the model.

**Identify and Cultivate the Right Interlocutor, pp. 59–61 — S-P02.** The preferred American combines sympathetic strategic views with access to the president. Solomon traces Kissinger, Brzezinski, Haig, and later alternative interlocutors; Mao–Nixon, February 21, 1972, illustrates attention to Kissinger. Ray Cline's public treatment is a contrasting effort to exclude an unwelcome channel. Political access and shared anti-Soviet interests explain selection alongside guanxi; the chapter acknowledges that all governments prefer friendly officials.

**Controlling the Ambience, pp. 61–65 — S-P03.** Beijing venues permit hospitality, historical spectacle, scheduling, and protocol to shape mood and fatigue. Kissinger's October 1975 experience shows hospitality delaying substantive work. Yet communications and proximity to decision makers are also explicit practical advantages. Staged-tour exposure during Nixon's visit and the wrong university song for Ford demonstrate fallibility; repeated visits can produce suspicion instead of gratitude. The 1953 Dean account supplies an adversarial contrast, not proof of a constant hospitable style.

**Establish a Favorable Agenda, pp. 65–71 — S-P04.** Earlier adversarial agenda battles sought prejudicial wording; normalization involved more negotiated framing. The Stoessel–Lei exchanges of January 20 and February 20, 1970, and the Kissinger–Hilaly messages through May 1971 trace Taiwan-focused proposals meeting a broader American agenda. The final discussion did include international security. Withholding other topics failed to force Ford into normalization in 1975. Agenda limits also protected relationships with allies and reflected negotiators' narrow instructions.

**High Principles (Versus High Demands), pp. 71–75 — S-P05.** Broad commitments precede concrete terms and become standards against which later conduct is judged. Deng–Bush, May 8, 1982, and Huang Hua–Woodcock, July 7, 1978, anchor the claim. Solomon offers relationship stabilization, uncertainty about foreign objectives, and bargaining advantage as explanations. Crucially, p. 72 concedes agreements apparently inconsistent with initial principles; p. 74 records American refusal to accept them. Neither acceptance nor rigidity should be assumed from the ritual invocation of principle.

### Assessment and facilitating maneuvers, pp. 75–96

**Period of Assessment, pp. 75–76 — S-P01, S-P11.** Long negotiations allow exploration and testing, but the chapter itself lists slow decisions, political timing, and substantive irresolution as alternative causes. The duration of Geneva/Warsaw, normalization, and arms-sales talks cannot alone establish tactical delay. A later study needs dated proposals, response intervals, decision constraints, and identifiable opportunities for agreement.

**Our Guests Always Speak First, pp. 76–77 — S-P06.** Zhou–Kissinger, July 9, 1971; Huang Hua–Woodcock, July 5 and 14, 1978; and Geng–Brown, January 7, 1980, show efforts to obtain the American position first. The contrasting host/guest rationales make this an observable convention. Footnote 21 supplies an exception: Chinese negotiators may speak first when instructed to establish principles or delimit the permissible discussion.

**Projective-test Diplomacy, pp. 77–79 — S-P07.** Suggestive, underspecified formulations invite the counterpart to supply concrete content. Stoessel–Lei Yang, February 20, 1970, and the Mao–Kissinger and Zhou–Kissinger exchanges of November 12–14, 1973, illustrate this. The evolving Japan formula shows that an attractive analogy need not specify all eventual requirements. Test ambiguity's direction and effects; a vague formulation may also be a sincere exploratory proposal before either side knows the solution.

**Facilitating Maneuvers, p. 79.** This umbrella groups practices intended to preserve a constructive relationship and prevent difficult issues from breaking it. It is analytically important that the same repertoire can facilitate exchange as well as pressure the counterpart. Coding every indirect act as hostile manipulation would erase this distinction.

**Intermediaries, pp. 79–81 — S-P08.** Pakistan supplied trusted, secure communication and reassurance of intent during the opening. Solomon contrasts more guarded use of Romania and preference for Edgar Snow over Theodore White. Selection combines trust, access, shared interests, and simple availability. Kissinger–Hilaly, December 9, 1970, is a principal anchor. The 1975 Chinese-American physician message rests on Solomon's recollection, not a named documentary citation.

**Indirect Communications, pp. 81–84 — S-P08.** Sensitive demands can travel below the top leader while informal lunches explore ideas without commitment. The Reagan–Zhao Cancun meeting and follow-on Huang–Haig exchanges illustrate leader-level insulation; Zhang–Hummel, December 4 and 22, 1981, anchors informal arms-sales discussions. Footnote 36 explicitly notes a similar Soviet-American practice. Channel differences are evidence of communication design, not automatically of deception or uniquely Chinese culture.

**Ambiguous but Loaded Language, pp. 84–87 — S-P07.** Poetry, age, third-country analogies, and indirect criticism carry proposed additional meanings. Zhou–Nixon, February 23, 1972; Vance–Huang Hua, June 2, 1978; and Muskie–Geng Biao, May 28, 1980, are useful anchors. Several interpretations are retrospective and explicitly tentative. Preserve literal content separately from inferred signals, particularly the interpretation of Zhou's health references and the proposed Cambodia visit date.

**Self-Deprecation, pp. 87–88 — S-P09.** Admission of limited achievement or minor error can encourage rapport or preempt criticism. Mao–Nixon, February 21, 1972, and the apology to George Bush in the Deng–Kissinger record of October 19, 1975, support observation of the behavior. The inference that self-criticism is primarily tactical remains separate from evidence of politeness, sincerity, or an ordinary effort to correct an error.

**Humor, pp. 88–89 — S-P09.** American recollections describe easy rapport and verbal play, including Qiao's teasing of Kissinger at their November 13, 1972, dinner. Solomon sees this mood as increasing the impact of later coldness. Claims contrasting Chinese and Soviet humor depend strongly on participants' tastes and selected encounters; the useful test concerns changes within a relationship and any demonstrable effect on bargaining.

**Playing Dumb (Calculated Misunderstanding), p. 89 — S-P07.** This short subsection labels apparent misunderstanding a way to avoid joining an unwanted issue. It offers no developed, dated episode here. It therefore deserves a weaker evidence assessment than the chapter's quoted cases. Subsequent research must distinguish deliberate evasion from interpretation problems, different assumptions, or an actual absence of instructions.

**Provocation as Facilitation, pp. 90–93 — S-P10.** Solomon links the March 1969 border clashes and the opening to Washington through strategic triangular politics; he explicitly makes Mao's broader coalition purpose conditional. Later anchors include Zhou–Kissinger, July 10–11, 1971, and Deng–Kissinger, November 26, 1974, on a Schlesinger visit. Effects on third-party perceptions are more observable than the full intended design. Do not convert the chapter's conjectural account of 1969 into established causation.

**Stalling, pp. 93–95 — S-P11.** Delay can preserve a relationship until domestic or strategic conditions improve. Huang Hua–Woodcock, November 14, 1977, and the Pu Shouchang–Hummel/Freeman and Zhang Zai–Freeman exchanges of March 18–24, 1982, anchor apparent holding patterns. Solomon links these to leadership conditions, partly through informal remarks and hindsight. Refusal to move may instead reflect substantive limits; the observer's inability to identify an agreement is not evidence that one was available.

**Reserving Position, pp. 95–96 — S-P12.** A party can retain its stated position on unresolved issues while obtaining partial agreement and a more useful relationship. Later endgame examples specify how reservations were recorded. The mechanism is distinct from concealing an objection: an express reservation warns the counterpart that the issue survives the deal. Test whether deferral subsequently stabilizes relations or becomes a platform for greater demands.

**Backing Off (Redefining Demands), p. 96 — S-P12.** Solomon says Chinese political negotiators protect credibility by avoiding formulations they must publicly reverse, while acknowledging that normalization was not a fully specified plan. He also contrasts commercial cases of backing off. This passage limits stronger claims of meticulous advance orchestration and contradicts any presumption that PRC demands never change.

**Leave a Way Out, p. 96 — S-P12.** Apparently unyielding language can retain an escape route. The Sun-tzu analogy supplies a cultural interpretation, but this subsection supplies no detailed case establishing that the military maxim caused diplomatic wording. Treat it as a testable mechanism of compatible face-saving compromises, with the actual text of proposals doing the evidentiary work.

### Pressure, politics, and the press, pp. 96–125

**Pressure Tactics, pp. 96–97.** Solomon distinguishes styles of argument from control over the structure of encounters. That distinction should remain in the coding: a sharp accusation, a delayed meeting, and a material sanction are different observations with different costs and possible effects.

**Playing Adversaries Against Each Other, pp. 97–100 — S-P10.** Alternative American visitors, bureaucratic rivals, and Soviet ties can indicate that Washington is not Beijing's only option. Zhou–Kissinger, July 10, 1971; Kissinger–Huang Zhen, August 18, 1976; and the April 27, 1984, Reagan meetings are anchors. Real diplomatic objectives coexist with signaling. The footnote on the Soviet helicopter crew explicitly says the incident's domestic political dynamics remain unclear.

**Beat Up on One's Friends, pp. 100–102 — S-P14.** Political investment in the relationship makes a friendly counterpart a promising target for pressure. Kissinger's 1975 treatment and Bush's August 1980 and May 1982 visits illustrate the argument; Deng–Bush, May 8, 1982, and Zhao's January 20, 1981, letter are clear anchors. The chapter also acknowledges weakness in reaching skeptical Americans. Test whether friendship produces more pressure, better access, greater concessions, or some combination.

**Your Chinese Friends Are in Trouble, pp. 103–104 — S-P15.** Deng–Haig, June 16, 1981, and Deng–Mondale, November 22, 1981, invoke domestic support and leadership vulnerability. Solomon expressly cannot determine whether the warning is hollow and compares it with American invocation of Congress. Preserve three possibilities: a real constraint, a strategic presentation of one, or an unsupported claim of constraint.

**Threats, pp. 104–105 — S-P13.** The chapter identifies change within its own period: overt threats were less prominent before normalization and increased in the 1981 arms-sales dispute. Limited costly acts can support threat credibility. Yet threats were sometimes implemented and sometimes abandoned. The proper test records the condition, specified consequence, subsequent action, and costs; it does not classify all threats as either bluff or immutable commitment.

**Formal Protests, pp. 105–106 — S-P19.** Repeated protests preserve a position and a record of alleged wrongdoing even without an expected immediate reversal. Examples span the older territorial protests and post-1982 arms-sale announcements. Their frequency is not a direct measure of escalation or policy failure. Later cases should examine whether a protest is cited subsequently and whether its language or accompanying action changes.

**Escalation of Demands, p. 106 — S-P13.** Solomon reads successive Taiwan requirements as a means of keeping the counterpart reacting to a Chinese agenda and making later movement appear concessional. The same sequence might partly represent progressive disclosure of an existing position. An update needs the earliest available private and public formulations, not merely the order in which the American participant learned of each requirement.

**Provocation as Pressure, pp. 106–108 — S-P13, S-P15.** The canceled 1975 performing-arts visit illustrates a limited confrontation over a Taiwan song. Habib–Han Xu exchanges of March 19–25 anchor it. Solomon supplies competing explanations: warning Ford or an internal effort to embarrass the more accommodating leadership. The failure of the visit is clear; the factional purpose remains an interpretation. Record the canceled exchange as a cost, not as evidence that the tactic succeeded.

**Loss of Control, pp. 108–110 — S-P03.** Uncertain scheduling, fatigue, and meal timing can disorient visitors. Kissinger's July and October 1971 recollections provide examples. His own account says an unexplained wait might reflect tactics, leadership review, or both. A participant's feeling of being manipulated is an outcome worth recording, but it cannot identify the cause of every delay.

**Bad Guy–Good Guy, pp. 110–111 — S-P16.** Different tones may divide hard presentation from accommodation. Solomon calls the tactic universal and interprets the Huang Hua–Deng pairing through it. Yet Huang had appeared accommodating to Kissinger earlier, and Deng could be abrasive. Changes within the same person's career are important controls against treating role, instruction, and personality as interchangeable explanations.

**Split the Politician from His Advisers, pp. 111–112 — S-P02.** Physical separation can remove expert cautions and documentation; institutional routing can also displace a less congenial negotiating team. Commercial anecdotes supply the first mechanism; Vance–Huang Hua, September 28, 1977, anchors an inferred preference for White House direction. Separate evidence of an unusual seating or meeting arrangement from evidence that it actually changed a decision.

**The Pressure of Mass Opinion, p. 112 — S-P15.** Solomon notes increased invocation of public feelings in the post-Mao period, while conceding outsiders cannot measure the constraint precisely. His limited-pluralization explanation is time-specific. Later research must examine channels through which public opinion is generated, monitored, suppressed, or translated into policy, rather than infer popular control from a negotiator's rhetorical claim.

**Press Play, pp. 113–115 — S-P17.** Media activity may serve domestic mobilization, international legitimacy, or bargaining influence. Mao's 1945 retrospective argument differs from the purposes of normalization publicity. Zhou's initial underestimation of the television entourage and rapid provision of satellite facilities demonstrate learning. This is evidence against assuming a fixed media capability throughout the baseline period.

**Pump Priming, pp. 115–116 — S-P17.** Interviews and public policy announcements establish subjects to which visitors respond. Carter–Deng, January 29, 1979, connects a Time interview to the opening discussion; Ye Jianying's September 30, 1981, program preceded renewed arms-sales bargaining. Establishing that a topic enters discussion is easier than proving it changes the counterpart's substantive position.

**The Trap of Public Visibility, pp. 116–117 — S-P17.** Public expectations can increase the political cost of an inconclusive visit. Deng's November 1974 needling illustrates pressure, but his December 3, 1975, praise of Ford's visit without a communiqué illustrates its release. Huang's October 30, 1981, press comments also made discussions visible. Publicity is thus adjustable: it can raise stakes or help preserve a relationship after limited results.

**Public Attack, pp. 117–118 — S-P17.** Indirect criticism can pressure officials without treating them openly as adversaries; direct criticism can advertise a limit or defend a leader domestically. The Sonnenfeldt episode and Deng's public rejection of the Vance approach illustrate these uses. The motives remain partly inferred, and publicity alone does not make a stated position irreversible.

**Raising Expectations, pp. 119–120 — S-P17.** Optimistic statements can activate public or business pressure for completion. Lin–Roy, July 25, 1978, records American concern about a disclosure; Deng's November 18 comments and Rockefeller's claims/assets discussions offer other examples. Solomon also says Beijing ultimately respected requested confidentiality. The claims/assets issue remained unresolved until 1979, showing that expectations need not produce an immediate agreement.

**Provocation through the Press, pp. 120–121 — S-P17.** Criticism of American passivity may seek a stronger anti-Soviet response. Kissinger–Huang Zhen, May 9, 1975, anchors a visible reaction. Solomon's further suggestion of influence on the Mayaguez military response is explicitly probabilistic and requires independent process tracing. Reaction, policy adoption, and the Chinese role in causing adoption are separate steps.

**Limit-Setting, p. 121 — S-P17.** Media of differing authority can communicate harder or softer limits while preserving room to move. The chapter describes a historical hierarchy of mainland and Hong Kong outlets. This hierarchy must not be carried into 2026 unchanged. Code ownership, authorization, document genre, and official endorsement for the time of each episode.

**Enticement, pp. 121–122 — S-P07, S-P17.** A vague press suggestion of compromise can elicit a concrete response without a formal Chinese commitment. Ji Chaozhu's March 2, 1982, contact with an embassy officer ties an official cue to a Xinhua commentary. The proposed intent remains hypothetical. Comparing the subsequent private offers is necessary to distinguish a real opening from a projection onto ambiguous words.

**Feedback, pp. 122–125 — S-P18.** Foreign reporting is quickly translated and circulated, shaping both leadership information and internal debate. Zhang Wenjin–Holdridge, January 11, 1982, and Zhang–Freeman, September 9, 1981, anchor reactions to American reporting. Solomon admits uncertainty about editorial selection. His account of a stronger 1983 protest after American coverage suggests a feedback loop, but the domestic mechanism is inferred, not directly observed.

### Words, agreement, and people, pp. 125–156

**The Power of Words, pp. 125–127 — S-P19.** The chapter connects authority, symbolic expression, record keeping, and prior commitments. Its argument about written Chinese and historical political culture is interpretive, not a demonstrated causal chain. Footnote 95 importantly acknowledges mutual overinterpretation: Mao's reading of Nixon's reception of an acrobatic troupe exceeded the intended signal. Historical-document competence and symbolic acuity should not be assumed equivalent.

**Word Games and Table 2, pp. 128–129.** The table assigns possible functions to diplomatic formulations: personal comment, criticism, formal instruction, principled position, adjustment, responsibility, or retaliation. Solomon recognizes many conventions as internationally familiar. Use the table as coding hypotheses, not a deterministic translation key. Context, speaker authority, Chinese wording, and subsequent action must establish whether a phrase actually signaled a binding limit or threat.

**Violation of Relationship Principles, p. 128 — S-P05.** Complaints about Taiwan arms sales seek to convert earlier general commitments into present obligations; Zhao Ziyang's 1983 policy statement supplies a public anchor. Record the agreed text and each side's interpretation. A claim of violation is not independent proof that the other party accepted the obligation being asserted.

**Asymmetrical Need, p. 130 — S-P20.** Assertions of independence and greater American dependence seek to displace the role of supplicant. Solomon relates them to anxiety about reliance on a powerful foreign country. Later tests must measure outside options and issue-specific dependencies independently; rhetorical denial of need may accompany either real leverage or vulnerability.

**Guilt and Debt, pp. 130–131 — S-P20.** Blame for Taiwan and unmet expectations about technology create arguments for corrective concessions. Zhou–Kissinger, July 9, 1971, and Deng–Regan, November 19, 1981, are anchors. The census-computer complaint also concerns an allegedly undelivered undertaking. Separate a dispute about performance from emotional pressure, and assess whether the grievance is supported by the prior record.

**Weakness and Fear, pp. 131–133 — S-P21.** Charges of appeasing Moscow challenge American resolve. Kissinger–Qiao, October 8, 1976, and Huang Hua–Brzezinski, May 21, 1978, illustrate the argument. Kissinger explicitly resisted the premise. Record whether criticism provoked substantive change or merely rebuttal; a forceful allegation can express China's strategic preference without explaining American policy.

**Illogical Policy, pp. 133–134 — S-P21.** Chinese officials argue that American actions strengthen America's adversaries or contradict declared goals. Deng–Kissinger, October 20, 1975, and Geng–Brown, January 7, 1980, provide examples. This is a recognizable form of persuasion used internationally. An update should assess the argument's substantive merit as well as any attempt to pressure, rather than assume every logical criticism is a stratagem.

**Devaluation, pp. 134–135 — S-P20.** Denying urgency or the value of an outcome can protect a bargaining position. Zhou–Kissinger, July 10, 1971, concerns the UN seat; Qiao–Bush, November 4, 1975, concerns normalization. Solomon contrasts such claims with apparent Chinese desires for progress. Test rhetoric against costly behavior and private instructions; neither asserted indifference nor an analyst's disbelief establishes the true valuation.

**Sharpening Differences, pp. 135–137 — S-P22.** Open disagreement can reassure allies and domestic constituencies while making limited common positions credible. The Shanghai Communiqué drafting and Qiao–Bush, October 31, 1975, illustrate this. The chapter distinguishes the political circumstances of successive episodes and credits compromises by both sides. Public friction should not be equated automatically with the abandonment of cooperation.

**Debunking, pp. 138–139 — S-P21.** Ridicule can puncture a counterpart's rationale and demands. Deng's September 5, 1974, congressional meeting challenged alarming assessments of a Soviet attack; Deng–Vance, August 24, 1977, rejected the normalization presentation. These examples also show Chinese resistance to perceived American pressure. The analytical unit should be a reciprocal exchange, not Chinese rhetoric detached from what elicited it.

**Personal Abuse, pp. 139–140 — S-P16, S-P21.** Solomon contrasts the generally courteous normalization exchanges with older invective and intermittent later accusations. Mao–Kissinger, February 17–18, 1973, and Kissinger–Huang Zhen, August 18, 1976, are anchors. He attributes some harshness to domestic polarization. Neither hostile tone nor a friendly style alone establishes willingness to settle; compare tone with authority and offers.

**Distorting the Record, pp. 140–143 — S-P19.** Solomon identifies stronger Chinese restatements of the American Taiwan wording and a contested assertion of a commitment to end arms sales. Kissinger–Huang Zhen, January 8, 1977; Huang Hua–Woodcock, August 11, 1978; and Han Nianlong–Woodcock, December 5, 1978, allow precise checks. Preserve original bilingual texts, the speaker's paraphrase, and each party's rebuttal. The passage also qualifies general praise for meticulous translation and credibility.

**Time Pressures, pp. 143–147 — S-P23.** Scheduling and apparent patience can create or exploit deadlines. Huang Hua–Woodcock, July 5 and 14, 1978, anchors resistance to regular meetings. Crucially, both sides pressed each other in February 1972; the United States let the February 28, 1982, target pass; and the Hong Kong example recognizes British initial time leverage. Delay, patience, and bargaining power must therefore be measured separately.

**End Game, pp. 147–151 — S-P24, S-P12, S-P05.** Concrete drafting and senior intervention can accompany rapid agreement after prolonged testing. The 1971 announcement, December 1978 settlement, and Wan Li–Samuel Pierce, August 14, 1982, support the pattern. The claims/assets repudiation is an explicit failure case. Reserved positions survive settlement; preference for less legalistic political documents is explained by flexibility and guanxi. Senior participation is a probabilistic indicator to test, not a guarantee of a deal.

**Implementation, pp. 151–153 — S-P25.** Chinese officials press counterparts to honor earlier words, but the chapter lists Chinese nonperformance or reversal too: claims/assets, Liu Huaqing's planned visit, and grain trade. Qiao–Bruce, April 2, 1974, anchors implementation pressure; Deng–Brzezinski, May 21, 1978, even acknowledges a new administration's right to reconsider. Classify binding obligations, expressions of intent, conditions, and unilateral expectations before coding a violation.

**Personalities, pp. 153–156 — S-P16.** Solomon explicitly restores individual variation after presenting general patterns. Mao's freedom to set themes differs from Zhou's deference and conciliatory presentation; Qiao's manner varied with political circumstances; Huang appears as Deng's harder counterpart in 1978–1982. These are American-facing portraits, not complete psychological assessments. The unresolved Mao–Zhou succession relationship illustrates the limits of inferring domestic authority from foreign conversations. Compare individuals across roles and time rather than assign their personal manner to China permanently.

## Applying the chapter to the 2026 investigation

Each later case should first establish the issue, counterpart, relative interests, alternative options, institutions involved, initial positions, concessions, agreement text, and implementation. Only then should the 25 propositions be tested. Preserve failure, reversal, and negotiated compromise alongside examples of effective pressure. A recurring tactic can persist while its frequency, means, underlying cause, or effectiveness changes.

For every attributed tactic, retain five distinctions: observed act versus inferred intent; Chinese demand versus mutually accepted obligation; rhetoric versus material behavior; episode-level effect versus final outcome; and individual style versus institutional incentive. The strongest comparative design also records American and other-country use of similar practices. Without that comparison, this chapter is evidence of what China did, not what makes China distinctive.

The most consequential updating tests concern the preference for general political documents; the role of personal access; centralized authority and domestic constraints; the use of public and private channels; the survival of issue reservations after agreement; and the link between growing material leverage and coercive demands. These are research priorities, not claims that change has already been established. The full case corpus and Freeman's separate baseline should determine the eventual classifications.

## Source-verification queue

Several extracted passages contain date or citation mismatches. They should be checked against page images and then, where possible, original records before publication as precise chronology: p. 74 describes a 1972 remark but cites Zhou–Kissinger, February 16, 1973; p. 82's Cancun footnote renders the name as Reagan–Zhang, while p. 123 gives Reagan–Zhao for October 21, 1981; p. 118's narrative dates the Sonnenfeldt example to 1974 while its footnote cites April 1976; p. 117 extracts a date as November 206; p. 146's December 12 narrative differs from its December 11 footnote; p. 147's February 25–20 date is unusable without visual checking; and pp. 149–150 call an arms-sales endgame 1978 while the Wan Li footnote is dated August 14, 1982. The p. 149 December 4 reference also warrants comparison with p. 146's December 5 account.

These issues do not invalidate the chapter's general descriptions. They do mean that this dossier is an annotated reading and hypothesis inventory, with explicit provenance limits, rather than an independently verified edition of the underlying diplomatic record.
