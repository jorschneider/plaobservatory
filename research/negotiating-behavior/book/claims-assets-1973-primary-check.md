# A senior intervention that did not finish the bargain

**First pass: 15 September 2026. Current assessment updated: 16 September 2026.** Solomon himself includes the failed 1973 private-claims/blocked-assets arrangement as a counterexample to his discussion of agreement making (printed 148, 152). The first pass read selected original exchanges and complete American decision memoranda from 1972–1977; its ten-document [register](../sources/claims-assets-1973-register.json) remains unchanged. It established what the senior intervention accomplished and what remained unresolved, without independently establishing the Chinese leadership struggle Solomon presents as a cause of reversal.

The September 16 follow-up adds two contemporary Lin–Habib transcripts, from November 1974 and October 1975, and reacquires a previously identified published April 1977 record. The exact June 14, 1974 Chinese reply and the attachments to Solomon’s December 31, 1973 memorandum remain unrecovered. The [follow-up memo](../manuscript/development/claims-1974-archive-followup.md) and [separate register](../manuscript/development/claims-1974-archive-followup-register.json) preserve the added reading scopes, scans, hashes and access limits. Earlier findings below are retained, with the first pass’s evidence boundary identified and a dated account of the new evidence. The baseline proposition files have not been changed.

## What the proposed settlement had to do

The American objective was to obtain compensation for nationalized American property while disposing of Chinese assets blocked by the United States. Nixon’s August 30, 1972 instruction preferred a Chinese lump-sum payment, possibly in installments, in return for unblocking assets. If that was not negotiable, the Americans should propose retaining the blocked assets to compensate American claimants. The instruction required a further negotiating scenario prepared by State with Treasury and submitted for presidential approval. There was an American authorization process before the encounter with Zhou, not simply an unconstrained envoy bargaining with a constrained Chinese bureaucracy. [NSDM188, complete text and notes, FRUS XVII/249.](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v17/d249)

A February 1973 exchange records Zhou and Kissinger advocating a comprehensive political treatment and relegating difficult details to further contacts. Kissinger offered a confidential channel through which Zhou could flag technical obstacles. Both men expressed confidence that a political understanding could simplify the work. This selected passage is evidence of their approach, not the complete February offer and acceptance. The American 1977 review describes the February result as an agreement in principle on mutual assignment of claims; the actual successive draft instruments have not been acquired. [FRUS XVIII/10, printed 111–112](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v18/d10); [FRUS XIII/29, Vance memorandum, “Background.”](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v13/d29)

The problem in November was not only how much money to transfer. Some Chinese deposits in third-country banks had already been returned to China. Washington regarded those repayments as contrary to its blocking measures; Zhou regarded them as legal. The proposed settlement also raised the definition of Chinese nationals and the treatment of bond claims against earlier Chinese governments. In the November 12 meeting Kissinger described excluding the third-country funds as unacceptable because the resulting payment to American claimants would be too low for Congress. His changing percentage estimates within that exchange should be treated as negotiating representations, not a reconciled accounting. [FRUS XVIII/57, printed 364–367.](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v18/d57)

## The November 13 offer and its limits

Zhou’s next discussion separated three questions. He objected to adopting terminology associated with the former American treatment of China as an enemy. Kissinger admitted that he was insufficiently acquainted with that issue to decide it and would consult his legal advisers. Arthur Hummel explained that the executive could decline to support old bond claims but could not simply prevent citizens from approaching the courts. The boundaries of American authority were spoken across the table. [FRUS XVIII/59, printed 411–413.](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v18/d59)

On the third-country funds, Zhou proposed returning the $17 million, favoring a direct payment to the US government over routing it back through the banks. He connected the route to a substantive concern: repayment through the banks could appear to recognize the validity of the American blocking measures. Kissinger said that the sum mattered because it would lift the prospective compensation toward a politically acceptable proportion of the claims. He distinguished taking the settlement’s terms to Congress from separately publicizing the $17 million component. The two also discussed keeping China’s prospective most-favored-nation treatment separate from the Soviet case. [FRUS XVIII/59, printed 413–416.](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v18/d59)

This was a consequential proposal by a senior Chinese leader. It was not a completed, signed instrument settling every question. Kissinger promised to consult the legal officials and make a further proposal within two weeks. His November 19 report to Nixon described progress, additional technical exchanges and an effort to reach final resolution in about a month. That contemporary description is more limited than treating the whole matter as a finished settlement awaiting mechanical execution. The source note identifies the separate Hummel counterpart-talk records in Nixon NSC files, Box 87; those records remain unacquired. [FRUS XVIII/62, “Bilateral Technical Issues,” printed 439, and note 12.](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v18/d62)

## What supports reversal, and what supports its explanation

The September 15 first pass relied on later American decision memoranda for the withdrawal. Solomon’s book cites a Chinese message of June 14, 1974; that exact message remains unacquired. The May 1977 Vance memorandum states that Zhou’s $17 million offer was withdrawn in 1974 and not reinstated in the latest talks. Brzezinski repeats that finding in his covering memorandum. These are evidence of the American government’s understanding of the withdrawal, with access to the negotiating record, but not two independent witnesses to a Chinese authorization decision. [FRUS XIII/29, both memoranda and notes.](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v13/d29)

By October 1975, a direct exchange with Deng documents the continuing impasse. Deng said the two sides remained at their original positions; Kissinger said the latest American proposal had changed, and Qiao characterized the change as too small. Deng rejected any settlement stating that Chinese were required to observe American laws. The record establishes the objection and disagreement about the significance of the offer. It does not disclose whether the objection arose from a leadership struggle, bargaining calculation, legal concern, or some combination. [FRUS XVIII/125, “Bilateral Relations,” printed 802–804.](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v18/d125)

The internal-political explanation nevertheless has a contemporary provenance. On December 31, 1973, Solomon himself warned Kissinger that political and bureaucratic resistance threatened the durability of the new relationship. His memorandum connected difficult counterpart talks to wider Chinese political uncertainty, drawing on reporting that remains partly redacted and on attachments not printed in the published volume. It also acknowledged a basis for Chinese suspicion about the settlement’s mechanics. This is valuable evidence of how Solomon formed his interpretation while in government. It cannot independently corroborate that interpretation merely by reappearing in his later book. The underlying Chinese documents, reporting sources and decision that withdrew the offer remain unavailable here. [FRUS XVIII/65, complete published memorandum and notes.](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v18/d65)

## September 16 update: a disputed condition and a new package

The November 26, 1974 counterpart discussion now supplies a contemporary attributed Chinese explanation of withdrawal. Habib described Zhou’s $17 million proposal as accepted, governmental noninvolvement in old bonds as resolving that question, and asset identification as the remaining technical task. He tied the definition to American legal requirements and protection against litigation. Lin refused this account of an agreement awaiting execution. He identified the Chinese June 14 reply as answering an American memorandum of December 22, 1973, and said the American demands concerning designated nationals and old liabilities remained unacceptable. [CLA-F01, printed 7–8 / physical 46–47](https://www.fordlibrarymuseum.gov/sites/default/files/pdf_documents/library/document/0331/1553934.pdf).

Lin then made the disputed conditionality explicit. He said China had offered the money on an understanding that the pre-1949 liabilities could easily be settled, but the December response did not conform to the American November commitment. He linked that alleged failure to withdrawal of the offer. Habib denied that the November and December statements differed, denied requiring Chinese acknowledgment of the old liabilities, and undertook to examine the matter again. The complete first-session record ends without resolving the disagreement. This is evidence of Lin’s explanation and Habib’s competing interpretation; without the underlying documents, it does not establish a broken American promise or the complete Chinese decision process. [CLA-F01, printed 8–10 / physical 47–49](https://www.fordlibrarymuseum.gov/sites/default/files/pdf_documents/library/document/0331/1553934.pdf). The complete ten-page meeting was read; its header and decisive printed pages 7–9 were visually checked.

On October 22, 1975, Habib proposed withdrawing the American statement of March 21, 1973 and aide-mémoire of October 27, 1973 on the bond issue. He also offered alternative placement of asset-identification language in the principal agreement or accompanying letters, describing it as symmetrical, and sought to bring Zhou’s monetary offer back into the package. Lin explicitly cited the June 14 letter as withdrawing that offer, maintained the bond position and rejected the proposed definitions as imposing American law on Chinese affairs. Habib ended the day’s claims discussion but at the close still asked the Chinese to study the proposals. This documents a proposed accommodation and continued refusal, not actual rescission of the earlier statements or proof that the proposed alternatives were legally equivalent. [CLA-F02, printed 4–5, 10–11, 16–17 / physical 7–8, 13–14, 19–20](https://www.fordlibrarymuseum.gov/sites/default/files/pdf_documents/library/document/0331/1553943.pdf). These complete claims exchanges and immediate context were read; header and printed pages 4–5 and 11 were visually checked. Solomon attended the meeting, which limits independence from his later account.

The additional evidence therefore changes the explanation available to the reader. Withdrawal is supported by Lin’s contemporary statements as well as the later American review. The alleged unmet assurance must be considered alongside Solomon’s political diagnosis. Neither record supplies Chinese instructions or excludes internal political pressure; neither permits treating the conditions attached to the offer as already settled. The [April 29, 1977 entry, CLA-F03](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v13/d27), provides the exact archival lead for telegram 985 from Beijing, June 14, 1974, while recording Han Hsu’s subsequent claim of continuity. That later claim is not the missing letter, and the editorial note’s term “agreement” is not proof that a new bilateral agreement had been concluded.

## Delay and authority on both sides

Hummel’s February 14, 1974 informal letter to the acting liaison-office chief supplies a useful check on one-sided institutional explanations. He describes Kissinger’s close control, competing demands on his attention and delayed or withdrawn instructions on several matters. He also reports Han Xu saying that the Chinese liaison office had not received word of Kissinger’s November conversations. These observations concern the wider handling of the relationship; they do not prove that an American delay caused the claims arrangement to fail. They show why an observer should investigate both communication systems before assigning every delay to Chinese tactics. [FRUS XVIII/71, complete letter, especially opening, items 2–4 and final paragraphs.](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v18/d71)

The May 1977 review likewise refuses an easy political/technical distinction. Vance explains why disputed definitions materially changed the money available to claimants. His prediction of congressional rejection was a constraint asserted by the executive, not an observed congressional vote on this China proposal. He recommended suspending further approaches until Washington decided its normalization strategy, while avoiding public linkage that could make normalization depend on the claims issue. Carter approved deferral. Brzezinski’s broader interpretation emphasized the political relationship, but the attached analysis demonstrated precisely why goodwill did not itself allocate every asset. [FRUS XIII/29.](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v13/d29)

## Current consequences for the baseline — September 16, 2026

This case qualifies **Solomon’s strong predictive formulation associated with S-P24**: senior participation and concrete movement were compatible with failure to finish. Solomon already included the failure, but at printed 149 he still calls senior intervention “the most reliable sign” of imminent agreement and links that prediction to a leader’s face. The tension lies within his account. Neither newly recovered exchange estimates the diagnostic’s comparative reliability; S-P24 remains unresolved in the current proposition assessment. Our narrower authorization heuristic is a qualification of that wording, not simply repetition of an already modest claim. The frozen proposition JSON summarizes the claim too mildly as “can signal”; it remains unchanged as a record of the initial extraction. It also sharpens **S-P25**: Lin pressed an alleged earlier American commitment while defending withdrawal of a Chinese monetary offer as a response to nonfulfillment. That is contested reciprocity, not proof of an asymmetric rule allowing only China to revise its commitments. Retraction of an offer, abandonment of an agreement in principle and nonperformance of a signed instrument require different evidence; the record does not establish that the whole settlement had been concluded.

For **S-P05**, Zhou’s concern with the payment route gives precise content to the relationship between political principle and technical form. Calling it opposition to legalism would miss his attempt to avoid validating a contested legal claim. The Lin–Habib exchanges likewise show that “technical” was one side’s description of questions the other treated as conditions of the political bargain. Kissinger’s own preference for broad political treatment further cautions against making that preference an exclusively Chinese trait.

The case does not validate **S-C19**, the general diagnosis that rigidity usually indicates leadership conflict. A contemporaneous analyst believed internal politics mattered, but the available evidence does not isolate that cause. Lin’s stated substantive reason is an alternative explanation to assess, not proof that internal politics had no effect. The strongest attainable finding is narrower: a senior leader could propose material movement while the authority, language and domestic acceptance needed for the full bargain remained unresolved.

The reconstruction ends with the 1977 decision to defer. It does not narrate the eventual 1979 settlement or assess its payments. Priority missing items are the February and March 1973 proposals, November 1973 counterpart talks, the December 22, 1973 American memorandum and related drafts, the June 14, 1974 Chinese message, the unprinted attachments to Solomon’s December 31 memorandum, and Chinese instructions authorizing either Zhou’s offer or its withdrawal. A later chapter may pursue those records; their missing contents are not supplied by inference.

## September 16 addendum: the second meeting and earlier bond instructions

A further bounded pass followed the three missing-instrument leads above. Its [memo](../manuscript/development/claims-missing-instruments-followup.md) and [register](../manuscript/development/claims-missing-instruments-followup-register.json) preserve five additional substantive records and two metadata-only records. Those are the new pass’s counts; the earlier ten-document register and three-record follow-up remain unchanged. The new evidence clarifies the disputed protections and the American approval problem without resolving Chinese internal causation.

The complete November 28, 1974 Lin–Habib meeting (**CLA-I01**, printed 1–11 / physical 35–45) records Habib’s return after reviewing the dispute. Lin objected that American non-espousal of old bond claims left private claimants free to pursue them; Habib said the bonds were outside the settlement. On asset definitions, Habib wanted protection against owners suing Washington over assignment, while Lin objected to categories inherited from the hostile measures of 1950. They also disagreed about what a package had to include. The header and complete claims section, printed 6–11, were visually checked. The memorandum expressly says it was reconstructed from Oscar Armstrong’s notes after the stenographic pad was mislaid: it is an official contemporary record, not a surviving verbatim transcript. Lin’s account of the December memorandum, bank interventions and the reasons for withdrawal remains attributed. [Official Ford scan.](https://www.fordlibrarymuseum.gov/sites/default/files/pdf_documents/library/document/0331/1553936.pdf)

Two complete released-cable reproductions supply earlier American wording. **State 105075, May 31, 1973 (CLA-I04)** quotes the March 21 statement: the old bonds were excluded from the proposed settlement; Washington would not espouse the claims, leaving direct negotiation to bondholders or their representatives and the governments concerned. **State 248454, October 18, 1975 (CLA-I05)** expressly retransmits **State 209591 of October 24, 1973**, whose instructions additionally offered actively to discourage attachment of Chinese property. This is a recovered 1973 instruction through a later retransmission, not a new 1975 concession. Neither assurance promised to extinguish the claims. The texts support the substantive distinction Lin raised without proving his allegation that Washington demanded acceptance of all old liabilities. They were acquired from public mirrors of released State records, without an official-host scan comparison; their detailed provenance is in the new register. [State 105075](https://wikileaks.org/plusd/cables/1973STATE105075_b.html); [State 248454](https://wikileaks.org/plusd/cables/1975STATE248454_b.html).

Two official records reinforce the limits on explanation. In the same-day senior meeting, Kissinger accepted the principle that American law did not apply to China and promised work on a compatible definition; he supplied no adopted wording (**CLA-I02**, selected claims exchange, FRUS XVIII/98, printed 628–629). A complete four-page November 1975 State briefing (**CLA-I03**, physical 3–6) explicitly labels its explanations for China’s stance speculative and lists several alternatives, including internal rivalries, normalization, commercial priorities and precedent for other claims. It also recommends consultation with key congressmen before formalizing any unexpectedly accepted settlement. That is a State recommendation, not proof of presidential approval or subsequent action. [Senior meeting](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v18/d98); [briefing paper](https://www.fordlibrarymuseum.gov/sites/default/files/pdf_documents/library/document/0358/035800374.pdf).

The **June 14, 1974 Chinese reply, December 22, 1973 American memorandum and Solomon’s unprinted December 31 attachments remain unrecovered**. The newly located metadata for **Peking 985** identifies microfilm **P850038-1564** and says released/unclassified, while reporting unavailable electronic text; its reference, **State 126228 of June 13**, has the same access limitation and microfilm **P850038-1561**. These are specific retrieval leads, not the missing note or evidence of continued classification. The earlier cable quotations do not supply the complete March draft, establish identity with the October 27 aide-mémoire, or fill the December gap. No Chinese instructions authorizing the offer or withdrawal have been acquired. The new records therefore leave **S-P24 and S-C19 unresolved** and do not establish **S-P25’s asymmetry**: the dispute about an unmet assurance is sharper, but its adjudication and the internal cause of withdrawal remain open.
