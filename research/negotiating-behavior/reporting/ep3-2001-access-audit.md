# EP-3, 2001: primary-source access audit

Research checked 15 September 2026. This is an acquisition and reading-scope audit, not a completed causal case or a verdict about Chinese negotiating behavior. The bounded public-document sequence runs from 3 April to 3 July 2001. Later recollections and archival administrative records have separate dates and evidentiary roles.

**The public record now supports a precise starting account of what was demanded, what the released letter said, and which issues remained open. It does not yet support a reconstruction of successive drafts or either leadership's private instructions.** The first pass read twelve contemporary official documents or transcripts in their entirety or, for the June 7 Pentagon briefing, in the complete EP-3 section. Eleven were preserved as source HTML/PDF captures; the Pentagon text is preserved as browser extracts from a nongovernmental mirror. A participant interview transcript and a six-page scholarly article were also read completely. The subsequent case-writing pass added a twenty-one-page contemporary US legal memorandum and enclosures, plus selected Jost article and appendix readings, bringing the register to twenty-one entries. Four archival administrative records establish a concrete acquisition route, but **zero underlying documents from the newly processed NSC release were obtained**.

## What the letter comparison permits

The English release expresses sorrow for the Chinese loss, then separately for entry and landing without verbal clearance. It also says that the full picture remains unclear and describes the American account of a severely damaged aircraft undertaking emergency procedures. The crew clause records a US understanding and expectation of prompt departure; the following meeting is scheduled for April 18. Its agenda includes causes, prevention and a plan for prompt aircraft return. It acknowledges China's intention to raise reconnaissance missions. **Acknowledging an item for discussion is not agreeing to terminate those missions.** [EP303]

The MFA delivery report calls the document a **致歉信** and renders both expressions with **深表歉意**. Its selected account omits several English passages, including the proposed aircraft-return agenda and emergency context. Because it does not purport to reproduce the whole letter, those omissions cannot by themselves be described as mistranslation of a full text. This is authoritative evidence of how China publicly presented the letter, not proof of a mutually authenticated bilingual instrument. [EP304]

Sun's first exchange is especially useful: the question itself distinguishes the demanded **道歉** from **深表歉意** in the language ultimately received and asks whether China compromised. Keeping those Chinese terms avoids importing the English letter's characterization into the question. His response stresses an official US letter, humanitarian departure and continuing negotiations. Later answers preserve China's responsibility allegation and investigation rights. Thus the semantic issue was visible within the Chinese public record; it should not be reduced to two audiences unwittingly reading different words. [EP306]

A full Chinese rendering attributed to the US State Department's International Information Programs was located on a historical mirror, but retrieval failed. It remains an access lead, not a text counted as read. Even if obtained, a US translation would have a different status from a Chinese government translation or a jointly authenticated text. The acquired MFA excerpts meet the narrower need for authoritative Chinese public rendering; a complete authoritative PRC version, original signed copy and authenticated draft series remain missing.

Fleischer's chronology places delivery of a copy of the signed letter around 05:00–06:00 Washington time, consistent with the MFA's 17:30 Beijing event. His account attributes approval of everything in the letter to Bush. The choice of ambassador as signatory consequently does not establish lack of presidential authorization. Neither publication supplies the actual clearance chain or evidence of how proposed language changed. [EP304, EP307]

## Preserve three bargaining tracks

**Responsibility and future reconnaissance.** The April 3 Chinese statement combines a detailed allegation about the mechanics of collision with broader objections to reconnaissance and sovereignty. China's April 18 announcement maintains these demands after crew departure had been authorized. That persistence matters: a release agreement need not settle the dispute over who caused the incident. This audit does not adjudicate the competing collision accounts or international-law claims. [EP301, EP308]

**Crew departure.** Bush initially publicly demanded both people and aircraft. The April 11 arrangement treats the crew's departure as immediate business and aircraft return as a subject for the forthcoming meeting. Tang announces departure after formalities and cites both the letter and humanitarian grounds. These texts establish separation in the announced arrangement; they do not establish the exact moment that either side privately authorized that separation, or whether a particular word was the decisive concession. April 11 authorization must also be distinguished from the subsequent physical journey home. [EP302–EP305]

**Aircraft disposition.** The record gives several distinct milestones: April 29 permission to inspect, May 29 agreement in principle on removal in pieces, June 7 technical planning, and the July 3 Chinese completion announcement. The Pentagon section identifies practical contingencies including runway weight limits and estimates several weeks before components leave. It also reports no communications hindrance from the arrangements agreed by its team. Those particulars make it unsafe to infer a deliberate stalling tactic from elapsed time alone. None rules out political leverage; identifying it requires the terms, competing proposals and decision record. Payment negotiation remained open in July. [EP309–EP312]

The companion case follows proposals across these three tracks. A single label such as “China won an apology” or “the United States secured release” conceals the scope of what each instrument accomplished and leaves the counterfactual untested.

## Two complete accounts, with unequal kinds of access

**Joseph Prueher, interviewed by James Green, published May 6, 2019 (EP313).** The entire [Georgetown transcript](https://uschinadialogue.georgetown.edu/podcasts/joseph-prueher) was read; the recording was not audited. Prueher had direct negotiating access. His account supplies leads about Powell's approvals, Lieberthal's advice and alternate Chinese channels. But his remembered letter language—“sorry you didn't hear us”—differs from the release's reference to absent verbal clearance. His claim that permission to fly the aircraft out existed before a changed US approach requires contemporaneous verification. Particularly relevant to this project, he explicitly credits studying Solomon: this interview is evidence of Solomon's influence on a participant's interpretive framework, not an unprimed confirmation of the book. [EP303, EP313]

**Peter Hays Gries and Kaiping Peng, “Culture Clash? Apologies East and West,” 2002, pp.173–178 (EP314).** All six pages and fifteen notes were read through the [author-uploaded text](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/261693139_Culture_Clash_Apologies_East_and_West). The initial PDF download failed; the case-writing pass subsequently obtained the [authors' Berkeley laboratory copy](https://culcog.berkeley.edu/Publications/2002Cultureclash.pdf) and visually checked its first page. The essay applies earlier social-psychology findings to contemporary public commentary, without an EP-3 decision-maker experiment or original archival reconstruction. Its attention to face and group identification on both sides is a useful corrective to national exclusivity. Its cultural mechanism remains a hypothesis here: China's own public statement argued physical culpability as well as context and consequences. Public rhetoric cannot alone establish which reasoning governed leaders' concessions. [EP301, EP314]
