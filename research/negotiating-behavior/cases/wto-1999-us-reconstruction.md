# WTO 1999: provisional reconstruction from U.S. evidence

Evidence cut-off: 5 September 2026. This file reconstructs the **U.S.–China bilateral accession negotiation**, especially the failed April closure and November 15 agreement. The bilateral agreement, U.S. permanent normal trade relations legislation, and China's eventual WTO accession are distinct events. This is an initial process dossier to be paired with the Chinese documentary reconstruction, not a verdict about timeless Chinese behavior or a completed history of accession.

The evidence already supports a consequential correction to an outcome-only account: negotiations involved uncertainty about **American authorization and follow-through as well as Chinese authority and concessions**. The research question is therefore not simply why Beijing alternated firmness and compromise. It is how both governments formed mandates, communicated them, managed public commitments, and revised their understandings of what had already been agreed.

The 1998 memcon's annotation that Jiang stopped reading prepared text is a useful observation about how a conversation unfolded. It should not be turned into a general claim that spontaneous language is always more authoritative than a prepared position. Both could carry instructions; only comparison with subsequent orders and behavior can distinguish them.

## Participant accounts that must remain separate

**Barshefsky, interviewed March 2005.** Her WTO narrative presents the April failure chiefly as an American political decision against closing a sufficiently strong package. She says she warned Chinese counterparts beforehand, thereby preserving credibility. Her November account connects Sperling's presence with reassurance that Washington would not reverse itself again, and emphasizes her departure threat and Zhu's intervention. She also portrays November's substance as very close to April's, with a telecom ownership retreat. These are important first-person claims, particularly where she was present, but also assign credit and responsibility. Her sweeping description of post-bombing non-contact fails the literal test against the July call and September pre-summit contacts. [US-BARSHEFSKY-2005](https://millercenter.org/the-presidency/presidential-oral-histories/charlene-barshefsky-oral-history), WTO sequence beginning with the transition from GATT to WTO and continuing through implementation.

**Summers, interviewed October 2002.** He also judges the April episode poorly, but is more critical of USTR's combination of negotiation, domestic positioning and publicity. His account describes attempted public harvesting of concessions without closure as damaging to the Chinese side. He then recounts the October reassurance mission and a private discussion with Zhu. The interview's reference to contrition over the previous spring is followed by an interviewer prompt about the bombing; do not silently convert that into a precise quotation solely apologizing for the April trade decision. His account is independent testimony to his own mission, but is not necessarily independent observation of every event he attributes to USTR. [US-SUMMERS-2002](https://millercenter.org/the-presidency/presidential-oral-histories/lawrence-summers-oral-history-2002), China/WTO discussion.

**Their April public account differed.** At the April 8 briefing, Barshefsky defended the need to settle the outstanding issues and said Zhu accepted the formulation preserving progress. Asked whether publication sought to mobilize business, she rejected that characterization and compared it with other accessions. Sperling acknowledged internal differences while referring to a combined NEC–NSC memorandum recording advisers' positions. That reference gives us a concrete archival target. Public solidarity can coexist with private disagreement, but the later accounts should not erase what the same actors said at the time. The original decision memorandum is more discriminating evidence than selecting whichever retrospective story best fits an argument. [US-APR08-BRIEFING](https://usinfo.org/usia/usinfo.state.gov/regional/ea/uschina/whwtobrf.htm).

**Goldberg and Kantor answer different questions.** Goldberg's embassy recollections are strongest for meetings he observed in 1998; his later narrative blurs the 1999 bilateral settlement and accession under the next administration. Kantor's account provides earlier evidence about the interaction of trade negotiations, U.S. domestic politics and the broader bilateral relationship. Neither should be counted as another eyewitness to Barshefsky's final November encounter. [US-GOLDBERG-2011](https://adst.org/OH%20TOCs/Goldberg-Robert.pdf), pp. 45–50, 54–56; [US-KANTOR-2002](https://millercenter.org/the-presidency/presidential-oral-histories/michael-mickey-kantor-oral-history), China/MFN/IPR passages.

## Discrepancy ledger and the documents that could resolve it

| Disputed proposition | Competing possibilities | Required check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| April was a finished bargain rejected for U.S. politics | A closeable bargain can still have unresolved clauses; alternatively unresolved clauses may have required meaningful additional Chinese authorization. Neither the public statement nor hindsight alone resolves the counterfactual. | Decision memo with advisers' positions; authorized offers; meeting record; attachments; Chinese approvals. |
| Publishing concessions was deliberate pressure | It may have been intentional constituency-building, ordinary disclosure applied without adequate attention to Chinese politics, or publication of a version containing contested assertions. These explanations can partly coexist. | Draft and publication timestamps, distribution instructions, press-office correspondence, Chinese objections identifying particular clauses. |
| November repeated April | “Same deal” may refer to overall commercial scale rather than exact provisions. Berger's claimed improvements and the telecom differences require separate comparison, not a binary verdict. | A sector table covering ownership, management rights, geographic access, transition periods, remedies and enforcement language in each dated text. |
| The departure threat caused agreement | It may have forced a decision, signaled the limit of U.S. authority, or coincided with previously arranged leadership clearance. | Chinese instruction/meeting chronology alongside U.S. reports before and after the threat; distinguish logistical action from retrospective theatrical emphasis. |
| Zhu overruled obstructionist subordinates | What Americans experienced as obstruction might include genuine mandate limits, opposition from sector authorities, bargaining tactics, or unresolved translation/legal issues. | Draft referrals, approvals, Chinese interagency submissions, and testimony from negotiators responsible for individual clauses. |
| Personal credibility saved the negotiation | Personal assurances may have mattered, but appointments, written mandates and institutional commitments could have supplied the credible signal. | Trace what counterparts actually cited when changing their offers or authorizing a meeting. Do not infer motive solely from a friendly exchange. |

The [Chinese reconstruction](wto-1999-chinese-reconstruction.md) draws on selected official retrospective histories and published negotiating documents. Its dates for leader messages and Zhu's final interventions are explicit comparison targets, not silently imported as facts independently corroborated by American archives. Differences of one day can reflect time zones or publication dates; differences over authority, baseline texts and outstanding clauses are substantively more significant.

## Provisional implications for updating Solomon

This case should test Solomon's propositions at the level of a **negotiating episode**, not score the book by whether an agreement was eventually signed. A delayed concession only becomes evidence of a tactic after examining mandate limits, substantive costs, domestic coalitions and the other side's actions.

First, the early documents make **internal coordination** an essential competing explanation for apparent rigidity. The next comparison must separate a negotiator who cannot change instructions from one deliberately withholding an authorized concession. A leader's intervention can fit either account; the intervention alone cannot decide between them.

Second, the failed April closure makes **American credibility** observable rather than background. Reconstructing Chinese responses requires establishing what Beijing reasonably understood about Washington's readiness. The controlled comparison is not a hypothetical uniformly decisive United States; it is the same negotiation before and after an apparent American reversal, with security shocks and substantive changes separately recorded.

Third, **publicity changes bargaining conditions**. The disputed publication of concessions supplies a concrete test of whether information reaching ministries, commercial actors and publics constrained subsequent offers. To make that argument, recover the actual circulation and reaction evidence. The mere existence of an internet posting cannot establish its readership or political effect.

Fourth, **a professional legal and sectoral negotiation can still involve relationship management**. The question is what each contributes. Technical schedules may anchor commitments while personal relationships transmit assurances or permit re-entry after a rupture. Treating either as the whole explanation would lose the interaction the case can reveal.

Finally, this is a study of behavior toward the United States in a particular accession setting. It does not yet establish a general change toward “large countries,” still less an evolution through 2026. The comparative design should carry the same questions into another major counterpart and a later period: who authorizes movement; how commitments are documented; how publicity changes room to maneuver; what leaders personally resolve; and how implementation disputes reopen the bargain. Those comparisons should begin only after the 1999 baseline has enough documentary support to distinguish observation from a participant's preferred story.
