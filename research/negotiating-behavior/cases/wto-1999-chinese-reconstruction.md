# WTO accession, 1999: Chinese records and the status of a draft

This is a first reconstruction of selected exchanges, not a completed history of accession. The strongest evidence here is a signed April memorandum and a dated Chinese November counterproposal reproduced in MOFCOM's documentary collection. The collection's surrounding narrative and a financial negotiator's later account supply context and hypotheses, with their different evidentiary status preserved.

**Sources:** `CN-WTO-G1`, `CN-WTO-B3`, and `CN-WTO-PARTICIPANTS` in the [source register](../sources/chinese-source-register.json). General volume 1 is cited as **G1**; bilateral volume 3, total volume 17, as **B3**. Links open the official [G1 PDF](https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/dl/sms/zhj_1_1.pdf) and [B3 PDF](https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/dl/sms/sbj_3_17.pdf). In the examined main text, PDF pages are printed pages +35 for G1 and +7 for B3.

## April: considerable agreement with specified reservations

The Chinese editorial narrative attributes the April failure to American political misjudgment after China had made extensive concessions. It portrays Clinton as worried about Congress and the consequences for Democrats, while Zhu Rongji says China does not have to join immediately or at any cost. This is the published Chinese reconstruction of their conversation, not a contemporaneous transcript independently verified here. G1 pp.251–252; B3 pp.143–146.

The useful move is to go beyond that explanation to **10 April 1999**, B3 item 04, printed p.191/PDF198. A reproduced English *Memorandum of Understanding* bears the signatures of Long Yongtu and Peter Scher. The catalog calls this a signature page, but it contains substantial operative language as well as signatures. It establishes:

- A wide range of market-access commitments had been agreed, including goods schedules and most services, subject to **“verification and rectification.”**
- Banking, including consumer auto finance, securities, audiovisual services, and textiles/apparel still required discussion.
- Some protocol or working-party commitments had been agreed, while the duration of dumping and product-safeguard provisions remained unresolved.
- The negotiators would verify schedules and protocols, resolve identified outstanding issues, and continue work on other aspects of the accession package.
- The MOU and attachments were not to be released publicly at the time. The page is now publicly reproduced by MOFCOM; this describes its historical circulation status.

Attachments 1 and 2 are named but not reproduced with this page. That absence is consequential. The MOU defeats both an assumption that nothing had been agreed and an assumption that every public American claim already represented final bilateral consent. It does not itself tell us which detailed provision was inside an attachment, remained qualified, or was reopened later.

The collection's narrative describes the schedules as requiring checking and approval. The signed English wording is checking and correction, which should be retained in any precise account. That difference is a reason to cite the original language, not evidence by itself that either party deliberately distorted an agreement.

The **10 April joint statement**, B3 item 05, p.192/PDF199, supports Chinese accession in 1999 and directs continued negotiation over remaining issues. Its final sentence still seeks an agreement on strong commercial terms. Thus, when the editorial narrative celebrates removal of a commercial-condition qualifier, the careful observation is about the qualifier's placement relative to the support-for-accession sentence; it is not the disappearance of commercial conditionality from the whole text. The following page reproduces the Chinese newspaper publication. These are the same statement's versions, not independent accounts of the bargain.

## Suspension and resumption: distinguish trade talks from all relations

G1's narrative records China's suspension of the accession talks following the embassy bombing, an exchange of presidential letters, technical contact in early September, and the political announcement of resumption at Auckland. It links the pause to dignity and describes China as regaining initiative. These descriptions of purpose and advantage belong to the editors. The dates, communications, and terms of resumption need corroboration from the original letters and counterpart records. G1 pp.258–260/PDF293–295.

This distinction matters when testing retrospective statements about a total absence of contact. A suspension of WTO bargaining is a narrower proposition than no Sino-American communication. The [American reconstruction](wto-1999-us-reconstruction.md) records evidence for that distinction.

The September discussion also concerned the baseline itself. In the Chinese account, the sides disagreed over whether the earlier American April list or the newer Chinese position represented the appropriate reference point. China sought PNTR, changes to safeguard demands, and relief from high-technology export controls; Barshefsky rejected treating export controls as part of her WTO mandate. These are testable claims about issue linkage, bargaining authority, and draft status, not simply evidence of an unwillingness to honor agreements. G1 pp.263–265/PDF298–300.

## November 12: a conditional, detailed Chinese offer

B3 item 07, printed pp.1078–1086/PDF1085–1093, reproduces **中方的一揽子建议**, dated **12 November 1999**, in Chinese and English. All nine pages were visually read in this pass. The English version explicitly identifies Chinese as the authentic language and itself as a reference translation.

The opening refers to US documents and proposals of April 10, April 24, November 3, and November 11. It makes the Chinese package valid only if a final bilateral agreement is concluded in the current negotiation. This is explicit written conditionality. Later movement away from one element would require a careful account of package acceptance, not an automatic finding of reneging.

| Mobile services | 25% after one year; 49% after five years | 1079; English1084 |

| Life insurance | Up to 50% foreign equity in a joint venture on accession | 1079–1080; English1084 |

These are **offers**, not the terms of accession and not evidence of implementation. They also show why a single number for telecommunications is misleading. Service category, percentage, phase-in, geography, and control must be recorded separately.

Life-insurance equity of 50% appears in a dated Chinese offer on November 12. That is a firm chronological constraint on any account locating the first appearance of that figure in a dramatic final meeting on November 15. It does not settle when the United States accepted it, whether control remained disputed, or what was exchanged for it.

There is also substantive movement within a stated limit: the banking offer accelerates a city opening while reserving other regulatory restrictions. The document establishes specific movement alongside retained restrictions, but does not show who authorized it or how it was presented publicly.

## November 12–15: compare offers before evaluating brinkmanship

The [American proposals reproduced in the same collection](../sources/wto-us-proposals-in-chinese-archive.md) provide the immediate counterpart. The US November 12 paper already includes 50% life-insurance equity on accession and 49% value-added/paging equity within one year, while reserving later treatment for the final package. The November 13 paper expressly seeks management or operational control alongside specified equity terms. It proposes a 12-year product-specific safeguard and a 20-year expiry of the NME antidumping provision. China's November 12 three-year proposal is therefore far from those American demands. B3 pp.1071–1077,1087–1093.

The sequence is more informative than asking which negotiator looked tougher. It shows a developing package with unresolved exchanges among ownership, control, implementation dates, and safeguard duration. It also documents American departure deadlines and requests to meet Zhu, rather than leaving the timing entirely in Chinese hands. B3 letters pp.1067–1070,1094.

The Chinese editorial chronology places Zhu meetings on November 13 and November 15, and signing at 15:50 on November 15. The sequence includes repeated technical consultations, ministerial meetings, interruptions, and renewed talks. The chronology's precise times were checked visually at G1 pp.265–266; the narrative is repeated at B3 pp.1027–1038. Repetition within this collection is not corroboration from a second source.

These materials support the occurrence of senior intervention and reciprocal pressure. They cannot establish that a Chinese leader's appearance was pre-scripted theater, that a US departure threat caused the final concession, or that the Chinese side felt no pressure. Each causal claim requires evidence about instructions and available alternatives.

## What a sector participant adds

邓红国's [financial-negotiation account](../sources/wto-financial-participant-note.md), printed pp.284–315 in the 2023 participant volume, supplies a specific proposed explanation of rigidity and movement: sector regulators formulated authorized offers, MOFTEC coordinated concessions, and exceptional endgame decisions could exceed normal departmental mandates. He describes fresh State Council authorization on November 13 and a separate request to PBOC Governor Dai Xianglong over financial leasing that evening. These are firsthand retrospective claims about his work, not acquired copies of those instructions.

Deng's assertion that American luggage and walkout tactics did not influence the Chinese side should be preserved alongside American causal accounts, not adopted as the resolution of their disagreement. His description of exhaustion also prevents treating China's negotiators as costless controllers of the process.

The published Chinese proposal's geographical and regulatory detail makes his approval-chain explanation plausible and testable. Corroboration would come from the dated banking schedules, the relevant PBOC and State Council records, and participants who handled the requests. Similarity between his recollection and an offer is not enough to establish every step in the chain.

## Provisional implications for the book update

The evidence justifies testing Solomon's propositions at a finer level: not whether a senior leader intervened, but who requested intervention and what authority it unlocked; not whether a position changed, but whether it was an accepted commitment or conditional package; not whether talks ran late, but what the negotiators were doing and who controlled the deadline.

The dated proposals are strong evidence against an unqualified account of Chinese negotiation as a preference for relational understandings at the expense of detailed written bargaining. They do not demonstrate that relationships had become irrelevant or that this change applies beyond accession. Technical expertise, political authority, relationship language, and strategic objectives coexist in the same episode. The cross-period verdict remains open until the later cases are reconstructed with comparable care.
