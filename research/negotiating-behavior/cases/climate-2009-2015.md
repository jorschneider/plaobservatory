# Negotiating cooperation without identical obligations: US–China climate bargaining, 2009–2015

Research date: **15 September 2026**. Last substantive event examined: **Paris adoption, 12 December 2015**. This is a bounded comparison for the Solomon update, not an assessment of implementation through 2026. The accompanying [move ledger](climate-2009-2015-moves.json) and [source register](../sources/climate-2009-2015-register.json) separate observed moves, public positions, retrospective claims, and legal outcomes. No new interviews or requests for closed archives have been conducted.

The clearest development in this record is **more formal and specific international procedures alongside continued national discretion**. The distinction between national ambition and external scrutiny was already visible in 2009. Subsequent arrangements extended and specified scrutiny, added joint political endorsement, and developed common procedures with differentiated application. That combination requires a more precise account than either enduring Chinese inflexibility or a transition from obstruction to leadership. It also requires examining American adaptation: a negotiation could advance when Washington accepted nationally different contributions and Beijing accepted external scrutiny without international allocation of its national target.

This case connects cooperative bargaining to the project's accession and trade studies. Its value is variation within one issue and counterpart: a difficult summit producing only a political accord, subsequent institutional work, a bilateral announcement, and a multilateral treaty. Its limitation is equally consequential. Changes in global participation, domestic policy costs and agreement design occur together. The sequence cannot isolate learning, leadership personality, relative power, or national culture as the cause.

## 1. Copenhagen: draw the boundary around the refusal

The Chinese account published on 24 December 2009 by accompanying Xinhua and People's Daily reporters records two positions together. In meetings on 17 December, Wen Jiabao presented China's mitigation target as unconditional and **“不容谈判”**—not open to negotiation—while allowing discussion of transparency. The same account describes Wen seeking a political document and coordinating with Brazil, South Africa and India, the other BASIC countries. This is an official account defending Chinese conduct, not an authenticated negotiating instruction. Nevertheless, its own description is inconsistent with an indiscriminate refusal to bargain. The proposed national ambition, external information requirements and form of the international instrument were separate objects. [CLI01, sections recounting 17–18 December meetings.][cli01]

The process allegations need their own evidentiary track. That report says China learned of a smaller leaders' meeting without having received an invitation, and that Wen sent Vice Foreign Minister He Yafei to protest the procedure. It later describes Obama joining a BASIC meeting around 18:50 on 18 December, after an earlier bilateral encounter and an understanding that the leaders would meet again. These claims establish the government's explanation for attendance and protocol decisions. They do not establish the complete invitation history, deliberate American humiliation, or deliberate Chinese obstruction. No invitation logs, full recordings or contemporaneous Chinese instruction sheets have been recovered for this dossier. [CLI01, passages beginning with the 17 December evening dinner and the 18 December 18:50 meeting.][cli01]

The principal American contemporaneous source here is narrower than a reconstruction of those rooms. At his 22:30 press conference on 18 December, Obama described an agreement among major economies and emphasized the combination of recorded actions and international consultation and analysis. He acknowledged that the package was insufficient and not legally binding. He was speaking before the final plenary outcome and said his negotiating team remained to complete the work. His presentation therefore cannot establish that the Conference of the Parties had already adopted the accord. It also identifies a constraint on the American side: he wanted commitments his country could actually keep. [CLI02, printed pp. 1823–1828, especially monitoring and agreement questions and final note.][cli02]

The actual decision resolves the status question. Decision 2/CP.15 **takes note** of the Copenhagen Accord; it does not adopt it as a consensual COP agreement. Paragraph 5 of the accord distinguishes domestic measurement, reporting and verification of developing-country actions; international consultation and analysis of information on implementation; and international measurement, reporting and verification for actions receiving international support. Sovereignty language qualifies scrutiny rather than deleting every international procedure. The appendices separately list developed-country targets and developing-country actions. The correct comparison is therefore not verification versus none, or agreement versus failure, but which scrutiny applied to which action and what institution had accepted it. [CLI03, pp. 4–9, especially p. 6 para. 5.][cli03]

A contemporary Chinese expert interpretation helps test whether this is merely a later Western reading. He Jiankun, identified as deputy chair of the National Expert Committee on Climate Change and a Tsinghua professor, explicitly described accepting international consultation and analysis as a flexible adjustment while resisting equivalent international verification. He also listed the consultation guidelines among issues still requiring negotiation. His essay argues China's case and does not disclose access to the decisive meeting; its account of the distinction can be checked against the accord. His separate claim equating a carbon-intensity reduction with the same percentage deviation from an emissions baseline is not adopted here: those are different measures without an explicit counterfactual. [CLI04, sections 3–4; methodological concern in section 3.][cli04]

This makes Copenhagen a useful test of Solomon's senior-intervention signal. Leaders worked on wording and obtained a partial political package; they did not thereby obtain the broader legal settlement or the assent of the whole conference. The apparent result depends on the outcome being predicted. Senior involvement may indicate authorization for one package while leaving multilateral adoption beyond either leader's authority. Calling the entire episode a failure misses that movement; calling it a completed global agreement misses the reservation of authority elsewhere.

## 2. Who could authorize movement?

Björn Conrad's fully read 2012 article offers an institutional explanation worth testing. Drawing on interviews conducted in Beijing between February 2009 and July 2010, including officials and delegation advisers, he argues that Wen's foreign-policy team displaced climate specialists near the summit's end and prioritized protecting the premier and China's diplomatic position. The underlying interview transcripts, exact mandates and delegation decision records are unavailable; anonymous interviewees cannot be counted as separately verified witnesses. This is evidence for a plausible authority discontinuity, not proof of leadership factional conflict. [CLI05, pp. 437, 443–445 and notes 7–8.][cli05]

Conrad also identifies a possible substantive reason for resisting a global long-term target: its relationship to developed-country reductions could imply a residual burden on developing countries. Without the exact conditional draft and instructions, that remains an alternative to irrational obstruction. His p. 453 quotation of accord paragraph 5 omits the intervening consultation-and-analysis provision. The resulting discussion of limited international verification must not be extended into a claim of no external scrutiny; formal verification and consultation were deliberately distinct. [CLI05, pp. 445, 453; compare CLI03 para. 5.][cli05]

The methodological implication goes beyond this particular scholar. A public refusal plus a change in personnel cannot diagnose Solomon's stronger proposition that rigidity usually reflects leadership conflict (S-C19). Substantive distributional interests, perceived procedural exclusion, division of expertise and protection of a leader are competing explanations. An internal disagreement is not necessarily a conflict within the supreme leadership. What would discriminate is a dated instruction overturned by another authority, evidence of the alternative approved package, and an independently recoverable account of why the reversal occurred.

The same caution applies to successful diplomacy. A stable specialist channel can improve communication without being delegated authority to alter national commitments. Todd Stern's retrospective account describes efforts to establish his standing as the US envoy and his relationship with Xie Zhenhua. It supplies a participant's account of how authority became legible across governments. It cannot establish the full distribution of Chinese approval powers. [CLI06, transcript opening discussion of Xie and protocol.][cli06]

## 3. Between the summits: specifying procedures and building channels

Cancun in 2010 supplied a consequential intermediate step. Decision 1/CP.16 set out international consultation and analysis for developing countries' biennial reports, including technical analysis in consultation with the country and a facilitative sharing of views. It excluded discussion of the appropriateness of domestic policies and specified a non-intrusive, non-punitive process respectful of sovereignty. Developed-country international assessment and review was separately described. This was institutional specification of an externally visible process under bounded authority, not simply an unchanged promise to publish domestic claims. [CLI07, paras. 44, 60–64, printed pp. 8–11.][cli07]

The Durban mandate, adopted on 11 December 2011, called for a protocol, another legal instrument or an agreed outcome with legal force applicable to all parties, to be completed by 2015 and implemented from 2020. This does not mean identical national targets. It does mean that negotiations for a broadly applicable legal arrangement preceded the 2013 leadership-period comparison. A narrative in which Xi personally initiated China's willingness to negotiate the eventual architecture would need to explain this earlier mandate. The present dossier establishes the mandate, not China's private reasons for accepting it. [CLI08, entire Decision 1/CP.17, pp. 2–3.][cli08]

The April 2013 bilateral statement designated a Climate Change Working Group, co-chaired by Xie and Stern, reporting to the leaders' special representatives at the Strategic and Economic Dialogue. Its July report identified five initiatives, responsible agencies, planned implementation documents and an October deadline. The subjects included vehicle emissions, smart grids, carbon capture, emissions-data management, and building and industrial efficiency. It also proposed more frequent policy exchanges about the multilateral negotiations and domestic climate policy. [CLI09, entire statement.][cli09] [CLI10, introduction, five initiatives, policy dialogue and working-group role.][cli10]

These documents permit a limited but concrete inference about instrument choice. Cooperation was organized around specified technical tasks, agency responsibilities and repeated reporting, alongside political negotiation. Such channels could supply information on feasibility and reduce uncertainty about implementation costs. Whether they actually changed either country's acceptable target requires the reports, cost estimates and decision memoranda produced inside them. Announcing a working group is neither evidence that a demonstration project operated nor proof that trust increased as promised.

The hydrofluorocarbon initiative shows another kind of design choice. The June leaders' understanding, reproduced in the July Chinese report, and the US September follow-up sought to use Montreal Protocol institutions to reduce HFC production and consumption while leaving emissions accounting and reporting within the climate framework. The September language envisaged a contact group addressing costs, assistance and alternatives. It was an agreement to pursue a particular institutional route, not a completed global amendment or agreed phase-down schedule. [CLI10, final policy-dialogue paragraph; CLI11, September 6 agreed paragraph.][cli11]

Freeman's recognition of specialized, active bargaining is useful here. However, multiple Chinese ministries and an NDRC co-chair do not demonstrate declining coordination by the Foreign Ministry (F09). The documents affirm an allocation of tasks under a senior bilateral structure. They do not measure contradictory instructions or identify the final authority for every issue. Nor does negotiating across two regimes prove learning rather than a strategic decision to exploit available institutional machinery.

## 4. The 2014 announcement: reciprocal endorsement, different national actions

The November announcement must be read in both languages and on its own terms. The US intended an economy-wide emissions reduction of 26–28 percent below 2005 by 2025, with best efforts toward 28 percent. China intended its **carbon dioxide** emissions to peak around 2030, with best efforts to peak earlier, and non-fossil energy to reach about 20 percent of primary energy consumption by 2030. These are not matching reductions, equivalent greenhouse-gas baskets or equal legal obligations. A percentage-point comparison would be analytically meaningless without modeling coverage and baselines. [CLI12, para. 3; English pp. 1–2 and complete Chinese para. 3.][cli12]

The announcement also paired differentiated responsibility with **“考虑到各国不同国情”**, rendered in the official English text as “in light of different national circumstances.” The principle survived while its application acquired a formulation accommodating variation. Both governments publicly endorsed an ambitious Paris outcome and stated that their announcement should encourage other countries' contributions. Political credit for cooperating, and the reputational consequences of a public national intention, became part of the package. The text demonstrates coordinated signaling; it does not demonstrate that China exchanged a particular increment of ambition for an American increment. [CLI12, paras. 2–4.][cli12]

Stern's 2019 interview describes a US proposal developed in early 2014, confidential exchanges over roughly nine months, and joint announcement being conditional on both governments being comfortable with the other's ambition. This is a valuable account of reciprocal endorsement, not a recovered sequence of offers. It is also fallible: his recollection folds additional Chinese target components into 2014 and refers to energy intensity. The dated announcement contains the peak and non-fossil targets; the September 2015 statement records carbon-intensity and forest-stock components. Exact terms must come from those texts. [CLI06, discussion beginning with Kerry's early-2014 request; CLI12 para. 3; CLI15 para. 12.][cli06]

The multilateral reception supplies contrary evidence to any automatic bilateral breakthrough story. The Lima decision adopted the national-circumstances formulation, yet kept arrangements for intended nationally determined contributions without prejudice to their legal nature and content. Its information list was qualified rather than a uniform mandatory template. Contemporary Earth Negotiations Bulletin reporting observed that the optimistic bilateral signals had not immediately transformed negotiating positions. The repeated phrase is evidence of textual convergence; exclusive bilateral authorship or decisive causation would require draft histories and accounts from the other negotiating groups. [CLI13, paras. 3, 8, 13–16; CLI14, “Enabling construction.”][cli14]

## 5. September 2015 and Paris: accept review, continue negotiating its boundaries

The September joint statement was more specific about institutional design. It supported an enhanced transparency system covering action and support, with flexibility for developing countries needing it in light of their capacities. The Chinese wording, **“依能力而需要灵活性”**, matters: flexibility was tied to capacity and need, not expressed as a blanket exemption from the system. The statement also retained differentiated treatment of finance and different national mitigation instruments. Bilateral consensus on this direction was a public position for the Paris negotiations, not an already adopted treaty article. [CLI15, paras. 3–4, 8, 11–12.][cli15]

The same document illustrates why an obligation ledger is necessary. It reported domestic actions, announced future plans, endorsed negotiating objectives, and identified separate financial pledges. China's planned national emissions-trading system was a future undertaking in that statement, not proof of operation in 2015. The American Green Climate Fund pledge and China's South–South fund were different channels. Nor was China's language on controlling high-carbon public investment identical to the stated US restriction on financing conventional coal power abroad. Treating every paragraph as an interchangeable concession would erase the agreement's construction. [CLI15, paras. 11–16.][cli15]

The Paris negotiation did not simply implement a settled US–China bargain. Earth Negotiations Bulletin recorded China and Saudi Arabia objecting that a proposed facilitative dialogue might become an ex ante review, while the EU and US described it as informing later contributions. On 5 December China again emphasized developed-country leadership and balance across the Durban mandate. Subsequent ministerial consultations were chaired by countries including Brazil and Singapore; they addressed how differentiation would operate across mitigation, transparency and finance. On 12 December the COP adopted the result. This observable process preserves both cooperation and continuing disagreement. [CLI16, transparency discussion; 5 December COP proceedings; differentiation consultations; adoption.][cli16]

The resulting legal architecture is consequential. Article 4.2 requires parties to prepare, communicate and maintain successive nationally determined contributions and to pursue domestic measures aimed at their objectives; it does not impose an internationally binding obligation to achieve each stated national result. Binding procedural duties, substantive ambition and available enforcement mechanisms must therefore be distinguished. Article 13 establishes reporting and review, with capacity-related flexibility; Article 15 makes the compliance mechanism facilitative and non-punitive. Developed-country finance remains differentiated in Article 9. China accepted adoption of a treaty text containing common procedures without uniform substantive commitments or an external authority to prescribe its national target. Adoption of the text on 12 December 2015 is distinct from subsequent consent to be bound; ratification falls outside this case’s substantive cutoff. [CLI17, Articles 4, 9, 13, 15; English and selected authentic Chinese scan passages.][cli17]

The inference should remain at that level. The observed instrument differs from a preference exclusively for broad political documents. But the legal form alone does not show Chinese preferences were transformed, that American demands prevailed, or that procedural obligations delivered adequate emissions reductions. A government may accept more specific rules because the rules preserve discretion on the most costly obligation. Testing that explanation requires tracing proposals and acceptable alternatives, not deciding who won by reading the final treaty backward.

## 6. What the case changes in the Solomon–Freeman framework

These are case-specific judgments against the separately audited claim texts, not verdicts on Chinese behavior generally.

| Proposition | Verdict in this case | Reason and remaining test |
|---|---|---|
| S-P05: principles and flexible political documents constrain the counterpart while preserving discretion | **Narrowed to specified conditions** | Differentiation persists through changing instruments, including a treaty. Discretion concerns particular obligations, not necessarily vague documents. Asymmetric invocation requires implementation evidence. |
| S-P12: partial agreement with positions reserved | **Retained within scope** | Copenhagen and later work separate settled arrangements from unfinished rules. The claim about avoiding conspicuous reversal remains untested. |
| S-P17: public communication shapes expectations and openings | **Retained within scope** | Joint announcements deliberately addressed third countries as well as domestic audiences. Effects on their actual concessions remain unresolved. |
| S-P24: concrete drafting and senior intervention signal approaching agreement | **Narrowed to specified conditions** | Copenhagen moved toward a partial package; leaders could not supply other parties' assent. The intended agreement and relevant authorizing institution must be specified. |
| S-C19: rigidity usually signals leadership conflict | **Unresolved** | Competing accounts support institutional and substantive alternatives; no recovered Chinese leadership-conflict record establishes the diagnosis. |
| F07/F11/F13: institutional learning, specialist memory and acquired substyles | **Unresolved as causal claims** | Active specialists and sustained channels are observable. The case does not isolate learning or compare matched behavior toward other counterparts. |
| F09: expanding technical negotiations reduce MFA coordination | **Unresolved** | Assigned technical responsibility is not evidence of lost coordination. |
| F14: learned incremental bargaining with Americans | **Unresolved** | A succession of announcements is not a sequence of opening demands, reservation positions and concessions. |
| S-P25: implementation remains an arena for asymmetric demands and accommodation | **Unresolved here** | Negotiating further rules is observable; this dossier does not examine the later performance and adjustment record needed to test asymmetry. |

A provisional additional proposition, **CLI-N01**, is that major-power cooperation may be enabled by separating ambition, legal obligation, scrutiny and mutual endorsement into different bargaining objects. The decisive within-episode test is whether separating scrutiny from national target-setting changed an otherwise unacceptable package. Records showing acceptance had already been authorized, or was driven by another concession while that separation made no difference, would weaken the proposed explanation. Cooperation reached elsewhere without separation would limit generalization but would not disprove this enabling mechanism. This is a mechanism to test against other cases, not a new national-character generalization.

Hao Zhang's fully read 2022 review usefully distinguishes regime-based, leadership and two-level explanations, and proposes more attention to transnational actors. It is a literature review rather than an independent negotiation reconstruction. Its broad passive-to-leading periodization cannot substitute for dated moves; its reference to Hu Jintao speaking at Copenhagen conflicts with the contemporary record of Wen. The review is therefore used to frame rival explanations and further reading, not as independent corroboration of the events above. [CLI18, pp. 50–60, especially Table 1 and pp. 53–55.][cli18]

## 7. What must be recovered next

The highest-value missing material is specific: the competing Copenhagen invitation records and successive transparency drafts; instructions to the NDRC specialists and He Yafei; Stern–Xie drafts and clearance records for the 2014 announcement; the bilateral proposals preceding September 2015's transparency language; and Chinese, American and third-country notes on the final Paris differentiation package. Interviews should ask witnesses first to reconstruct their own chronology, then confront these precise documents and apparent discrepancies. Neither an official narrative nor an anonymous-interview synthesis gives Solomon-equivalent access.

The source base has distinct chains. The paired 2014 and 2015 language versions are one negotiated document each, not independent witnesses. The Chinese accompanying-reporters account is an official publicity chain; He Jiankun is a separate expert argument, with access to particular rooms unestablished. Obama is a contemporaneous public participant account; Stern supplies later first-person process evidence. Conrad contributes Chinese-side interviews whose independence cannot be audited. UNFCCC decisions establish adopted language and institutional status. Earth Negotiations Bulletin contributes on-site reporting, not a complete transcript of private meetings. The register records these limits individually.

Both scholarly works and the Stern transcript were read completely. The short 2013–2015 bilateral texts and Chinese 2009 accounts were read completely; large UNFCCC compilations were read only at the listed decisions and clauses. Consequential Paris wording was also checked visually in authentic English and Chinese UN Treaty Series scans. Several UNFCCC downloads returned blocking pages despite apparently successful HTTP responses: preserved browser extracts are identified as extracts, not full PDFs. Later implementation, reversals and renewed cooperation require a separate extension before this bounded finding can become a claim about behavior through 2026.
