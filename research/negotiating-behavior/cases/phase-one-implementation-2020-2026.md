# Phase One after signature: implementation becomes another negotiation

Research date: 8 September 2026. This is a bounded process study, with a latest substantively examined public statement dated **2 July 2026**. It does not establish a complete 2026 chronology or a final disposition of the renewed US investigation. The [source register](../sources/phase-one-implementation-register.json) records exact reading coverage, versions, and limitations. Most recent statements are negotiating parties’ accounts, not independently verified implementation records.

The provisional finding is **continuity in bargaining over reciprocal conditions, combined with much more elaborate legal and administrative machinery than Solomon’s principal historical cases**. Signature did not end bargaining: implementation reviews, licensing decisions, purchases, tariff relief, and later investigations became connected negotiating arenas. This does not show a timeless Chinese propensity to evade agreements. Outcomes vary within the same agreement; US objectives and instruments also changed.

## What continued after the agreement

The paired August 2020 releases establish an operating senior channel. Washington described Lighthizer, Mnuchin, and Liu He discussing structural implementation and purchases, with both sides recognizing progress. Beijing’s shorter account emphasized macroeconomic coordination and conditions conducive to continued implementation. These are differently selected accounts of the same call—24 August in Washington, 25 August in China—not two separate negotiating rounds. Neither supplies minutes or unresolved proposals. [USTR, 24 August 2020](https://ustr.gov/about-us/policy-offices/press-office/press-releases/2020/august/statement-call-between-united-states-and-china); [Chinese government/Xinhua, 25 August](https://english.www.gov.cn/statecouncil/liuhe/202008/25/content_WS5f4465b0c6d0f7257693aefa.html).

The October 2021 pair makes the reciprocal demands clearer. Tai’s account identifies outstanding implementation issues and state-led, nonmarket practices. China’s account records its demand to remove additional tariffs and sanctions and its defense of its development model and industrial policy. There was an acknowledged conversation; there was no publicly documented acceptance of a common expanded agenda. [USTR, 8 October 2021](https://ustr.gov/about-us/policy-offices/press-office/press-releases/2021/october/readout-ambassador-tais-virtual-meeting-vice-premier-china-liu-he); [Xinhua, 9 October](https://www.news.cn/politics/leaders/2021-10/09/c_1127939844.htm).

After the purchase period ended, MOFCOM invoked the pandemic, recession, and supply-chain disruption while reporting normal team communications; it did not announce a quantified extension or a concluded second phase. In March 2022, Tai distinguished unfulfilled purchase commitments from industrial practices outside Phase One and argued for domestic capacity and allied cooperation alongside continued dialogue. Thus the counterparty’s objective was changing too: correcting individual implementation problems was increasingly nested within a wider competitive policy. [MOFCOM, 13 January 2022, South China Morning Post and Reuters questions](https://video.mofcom.gov.cn/xwfb/2022/1/ef17c1d2f07c4ef0b005cfd1cafceffe579.html); [Tai, March 2022, China section](https://ustr.gov/about-us/policy-offices/press-office/speeches-and-remarks/2022/march/testimony-ambassador-katherine-tai-senate-finance-committee-hearing-presidents-2022-trade-policy).

China’s April 2025 white paper retrospectively reports six senior calls, five deputy-minister quarterly meetings, and fourteen director-general monthly meetings or consultations during 2020–2021, plus working contacts. It also asserts neither party formally activated dispute resolution during those years. These attributed counts are not independently corroborated here. USTR’s later acknowledgment of consultations corroborates a channel’s existence, not the Chinese count or the absence of confidential appeals. The white paper’s sections II–III also frame Chinese financial opening as autonomous reform and advance reciprocal complaints about US restrictions. [White paper, II(5), II(7), III](https://chinawto.mofcom.gov.cn/article/ap/p/202504/20250403576920.shtml).

Ordinary implementation consultations, confidential Chapter 7 appeals, and a US statutory investigation must therefore remain separate entries in the chronology. The parent project’s bilingual agreement reading confirms that unforeseeable events trigger consultation under Article 7.6.2, rather than an express automatic waiver; Article 7.6.1 concerns existing international-agreement rights. The white paper’s reference to 7.6.1 for unforeseeable events is a miscitation, not a missing clause. [Agreement, Chapters 6–7](https://ustr.gov/sites/default/files/files/agreements/phase%20one%20agreement/Economic_And_Trade_Agreement_Between_The_United_States_And_China_Text.pdf).

## Measure implementation without collapsing it into intention

The purchase shortfall is substantial, but the denominator matters:

| PIIE measurement of 2020–2021 | Observed | Benchmark | Share |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Covered goods, US export series | $210.1bn | $352.2bn | 60% |
| Covered goods, Chinese import series | $235.3bn | $380.5bn | 62% |
| Covered goods and services, US series | $290.8bn | $502.4bn | 58% |

The first two rows use different official series; neither is all bilateral goods trade. The tracker’s monthly paths illustrate annual targets, rather than contractual monthly deadlines. Article 6.2.6 itself anticipates both official series and consultation over conflicting assessments. [Bown, goods tracker, sections I–IV](https://www.piie.com/research/piie-charts/2020/us-china-phase-one-tracker-chinas-purchases-us-goods); [agreement, Article 6.2.6](https://ustr.gov/sites/default/files/files/agreements/phase%20one%20agreement/Economic_And_Trade_Agreement_Between_The_United_States_And_China_Text.pdf).

The final row comes from Bown’s broader analysis. Its headline about none of the additional $200bn means exports failed to exceed the aggregate pre-trade-war baseline, **not that China purchased nothing**. Note 8 identifies a services-coverage limitation: the estimates omit commercial-presence sales even though some agreement categories include them. Detailed subsector targets were not public; the article estimates their allocation. Its discussion of pandemic losses, aircraft supply problems, and tariffs identifies alternative explanations, not a causal decomposition establishing each factor’s contribution. No underlying-data replication was performed here. [Bown, 2022 analysis, body, notes 5 and 8, appendix](https://www.piie.com/blogs/realtime-economics/2022/china-bought-none-extra-200-billion-us-exports-trumps-trade-deal).

A proper behavioral test must distinguish deliberate withholding, commercially unattractive purchases, unavailable supply, and administrative obstacles. Equally, the agreement’s quantified two-year obligation should not be silently extended: Article 6.2.3 projects a trajectory for 2022–2025; it does not repeat the 2020–2021 numeric obligation. [Agreement, Articles 6.2.1–3](https://ustr.gov/sites/default/files/files/agreements/phase%20one%20agreement/Economic_And_Trade_Agreement_Between_The_United_States_And_China_Text.pdf).

Structural implementation provides a within-agreement comparison. USTR’s January 2025 report acknowledges removal of foreign ownership caps and some wholly foreign-owned financial licenses. It also records continuing licensing difficulties and slow, opaque agricultural-biotechnology approvals. The finance passages separate permission to own from permission to operate; the agriculture passages distinguish occasional approvals from a predictable process. This is evidence against both blanket fulfillment and blanket nonimplementation. It does not identify whether remaining delays originated in a senior negotiating decision, regulatory capacity, competing ministries, or substantive safety judgments. [USTR, *2024 WTO Compliance Report*, printed pp. 12, 47–48, 65–66 / PDF pp. 20, 55–56, 73–74](https://ustr.gov/sites/default/files/files/reports/2025/2024USTRReportCongressonChinaWTOCompliance.pdf).

## An old agreement becomes an instrument in new bargaining

USTR initiated a separate Section 301 investigation on 24 October 2025, published 28 October. It covers implementation across structural provisions and purchases, commercial burdens, and possible responses. Its consultation request is under Trade Act section 303(a); the notice does not establish use of a Chapter 7 appeal. Initiation and preliminary allegations are not a final determination. The notice uses a purchase benchmark above $535bn and a shortfall above $217bn; those are **not numerically interchangeable** with Bown’s earlier estimates, and their construction remains unreconciled. Its background also misdates signature as December 2019; signature occurred in January 2020. [Federal Register notice, printed pp. 48733–48736, especially 48735](https://ustr.gov/sites/default/files/files/Issue_Areas/Enforcement/Section%20301/Published%20Phase%20One%20Initiation%20Notice.pdf).

The December hearing reveals disagreement inside the US coalition. American Soybean Association representative Josh Gackle acknowledged implementation problems but feared a new investigation could damage current negotiations; Renewable Fuels Association representative Geoff Cooper supported investigation and potential countermeasures. Seed-industry testimony supplied a procedural complaint about biotech reviews. When asked about specific seed-IP theft, its representative distinguished vulnerability from known instances. The transcript establishes testimony, not the underlying allegation’s truth. Moreover, an average approval-time commitment must not become an absolute deadline for every application. [Hearing, opening and first panel, pp. 1–35; especially 18–19, 25–28, 32–35](https://ustr.gov/sites/default/files/files/Issue_Areas/Enforcement/Section%20301/Hearing%20transcript%20full%20without%20index.pdf).

This debate occurred after the White House had announced a new soybean package alongside tariff and other reciprocal measures. The November fact sheet’s suspension of maritime-investigation measures should not be mistaken for suspension of every Section 301 proceeding. Old enforcement and new deal-making were proceeding together. [White House, 1 November 2025](https://www.whitehouse.gov/fact-sheets/2025/11/fact-sheet-president-donald-j-trump-strikes-deal-on-economic-and-trade-relations-with-china/).

MOFCOM’s complete 25 February 2026 response claims Chinese fulfillment, attributes difficulties to US restrictions, and says Phase One was repeatedly discussed during five 2025 consultation rounds. It favors carrying forward newer understandings and warns against additional Section 301 tariffs. China was contesting responsibility and the appropriate next step while continuing to invoke the agreement; it was not publicly treating the document as simply extinguished. [MOFCOM, 25 February 2026](https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/xwfb/xwfyrth/art/2026/art_c24476d86cec4869b95082708db09a91.html).

## What the 2026 comparison adds

The May announcements describe additional machinery and demands. The White House announced Trade and Investment Boards, agricultural purchases of at least $17bn annually with specified timing, additional to the soybean arrangement, and market-access measures. This is an official US account of commitments, not delivery evidence or a jointly signed text. [White House, 17 May 2026](https://www.whitehouse.gov/fact-sheets/2026/05/fact-sheet-president-donald-j-trump-secures-historic-deals-with-china-delivering-for-american-workers-farmers-and-industry/).

China’s 16 May response labels the results preliminary and says details remain under negotiation. Its 20 May explanation says the Boards’ design remains to be agreed and presents agricultural expansion as a guiding target linked to reciprocal tariff reductions and access. It lists US-side steps on dairy and seafood detention and recognition of Shandong’s avian-influenza-free status. These are concrete counterparts to grievances already listed in the April 2025 white paper, making them a traceable example of implementation complaints carried into a subsequent bargain. Neither statement establishes that every promised administrative step occurred. [MOFCOM, 16 May](https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/syxwfb/art/2026/art_0e8dac7e772e4339bf622ddc7717e5e1.html); [20 May, sections II–III, VII–VIII](https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/syxwfb/art/2026/art_3e89743a8d5d4a95bccdda685830afbd.html); [white paper, III(2)](https://chinawto.mofcom.gov.cn/article/ap/p/202504/20250403576920.shtml).

Reuters’ contemporaneous reporting introduces suppliers, prices, quotas, and trader expectations into the implementation question. Those expectations are useful competing mechanisms, not proof that future purchases will occur. Its reported beef registrations offer a specific administrative development to verify against the register, rather than treating all summit announcements as equally executed. [Cao and Thukral, Reuters, 18 May 2026](https://www.investing.com/news/commodities-news/explainerwhat-do-chinas-new-us-farm-purchases-mean-for-global-trade-4694760).

A concrete design process followed the summit. On 2 June, USTR opened public consultation, with comments due 10 July and responses 27 July. The linked notice asks about Board meeting frequency, changes to eligible products, and bilateral data sharing. Product questions request eight-digit customs codes, average 2022–2024 trade values, and effects on consumers, workers, and producers. It envisages negotiated tariff changes for equal-value non-sensitive goods, conditional on agreement and legal and security constraints. [USTR release, 2 June 2026](https://ustr.gov/about/policy-offices/press-office/press-releases/2026/june/ustr-seeks-public-comment-scope-and-operation-mechanism-promote-balanced-and-reciprocal-trade-china); [linked notice, PDF pp. 5–11, especially II(A–B)](https://ustr.gov/sites/default/files/files/Press/Releases/2026/June%202%20FRN%20FINAL%20for%20upload.pdf).

This documents an external input channel into US design, qualifying any categorical claim that business or public participation was absent. It does not establish stakeholder membership of the intergovernmental Board, actual influence on its rules, or equivalent access on the Chinese side. Those require submitted comments and subsequent decisions. The solicitation also supports a narrower institutionalization verdict: an announced channel was undergoing operational design; its effectiveness had not been demonstrated.

On 2 July, MOFCOM repeated the guiding-target formulation and emphasized enterprises’ commercial decisions. The reporter’s question says $17bn includes soybeans; the answer does not confirm that arithmetic. Do not silently substitute the question’s premise for China’s position or the White House’s different description. Public framing differs; private disagreement over agreed language remains unproven. [MOFCOM, 2 July 2026, Reuters question and He Yadong answer](https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/xwfbzt/2026/swbzklxxwfbh2026n7y2r/).

## Implications for revising Solomon

Retain [S-C18](../book/context-claims.json): interests and substantive constraints remain essential alternatives to tactical explanations. Retain S-C20 as a question about interactive institutional constraints: the hearing documents American disagreement, but provides no evidence Beijing deliberately produced it. Update S-C13 by treating the degree of legal specificity and administrative implementation as variables. Detailed rules and continuing bargaining coexist; preference for broad principles cannot summarize this case.

S-C10–12 remain hypotheses requiring internal process evidence. A leaders’ meeting followed by regulatory action does not establish that prior delay was a staged maneuver, missing authorization, or bureaucratic resistance. The next discriminating work is a small matched set of successful and delayed licenses: reconstruct applications, information requests, decisions, negotiating references, and delegated authority. In parallel, reconcile purchase baselines and acquire the actual 2026 implementing instruments. The strongest currently supported change is in the institutional setting and available instruments, not a demonstrated transformation in an invariant national negotiating character.
