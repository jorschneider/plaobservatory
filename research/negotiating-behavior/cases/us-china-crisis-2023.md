# Communication as a bargaining object: the 2023 balloon crisis and the uneven return to dialogue

Research date: 15 September 2026. Substantive event horizon: 3 February–30 November 2023, with the 5 August 2022 suspensions as the necessary baseline. This is a bounded comparison with the [2001 EP-3 case](ep3-2001.md), not a history of the entire Biden administration. Source identifiers resolve in the [source register](../sources/us-china-crisis-2023-register.json). Chinese quotations below are translated by this project unless otherwise specified.

The 2023 episode shows that access to communication could itself become a disputed good. Washington wanted military contact insulated from political disagreement; Beijing publicly conditioned particular contacts on an acceptable relationship and American conduct. Yet there was no single switch controlling all communication. Diplomatic meetings proceeded while defense contacts remained restricted, and agreement to restore military mechanisms in November did not settle the disputes that had made them valuable. The strongest finding is selective bargaining over channels, with different thresholds for different institutions. The evidence does not establish that withholding contact extracted a general American retreat, that Xi secretly ordered every refusal, or that personal diplomacy alone produced the reopening.

## The baseline matters: several channels were already suspended

On 5 August 2022, after Nancy Pelosi's Taiwan visit, the Chinese Foreign Ministry announced eight countermeasures. The first three cancelled arrangements for theater-commander calls, Defense Policy Coordination Talks, and Military Maritime Consultative Agreement meetings. Other items suspended cooperation on migrant repatriation, criminal judicial assistance, transnational crime, counternarcotics and climate talks. The list identifies differentiated mechanisms, not a severance of diplomatic relations. It also supplies a dated Chinese declaration that suspension served retaliation for an American political act. Calling November 2023 simply the reversal of the balloon rupture would erase this earlier bargaining baseline. [C23-01, entire eight-item announcement.](https://www.fmprc.gov.cn/zyxw/202208/t20220805_10735604.shtml)

This distinction changes the comparison with Solomon. The problem was not merely whether two diplomats trusted one another enough to begin a negotiation. Existing mechanisms, with established institutional identities, could be withheld or restored. Their availability was part of the negotiation. At the same time, a cancelled mechanism does not establish that every contact between the two military organizations ceased. The Chinese defense spokesman would explicitly reject that proposition in May 2023.

## February: incompatible explanations, two refusals, and continuing diplomatic contact

The first consequential Chinese statement, issued on 3 February, acknowledged the airship's Chinese origin and expressed regret. It described a civilian craft used for meteorological and other research, whose limited control capability and exposure to westerly winds had taken it far off course. Its explanation was **不可抗力**—force majeure—and unintended entry, with a promise to continue communicating. This was a qualified expression of regret tied to an account of accident. It neither conceded a surveillance mission nor accepted the American account of responsibility. Treating it as a full apology would invent convergence that the text does not contain. [C23-02, complete question and answer.](https://www.mfa.gov.cn/fyrbt_673021/dhdw_673027/202302/t20230203_11019482.shtml)

The American refusal came before the shootdown. In the State Department's 3 February account of Blinken's call with Wang Yi, Blinken acknowledged China's regret but characterized the balloon's presence as an irresponsible violation of sovereignty and international law that undermined his planned Beijing visit. He postponed travel while affirming diplomatic engagement and a visit when conditions allowed. The United States thus also made a particular diplomatic encounter conditional. The asymmetry lies in which contacts each side subsequently sought to protect, not in a categorical contrast between an American willingness and a Chinese unwillingness to use access politically. [C23-03, complete readout, official text preserved in reprint.](https://www.globalsecurity.org/wmd/library/news/china/2023/02/china-230203-state01.htm)

On 4 February, Austin announced that American aircraft had destroyed the balloon over territorial waters off South Carolina. His statement identified it as a surveillance platform, said Biden had authorized a shootdown on Wednesday once it could be accomplished without undue risk to people, and explained the delay by danger from falling debris over land. China's 5 February response protested the use of force, repeated its civilian and accidental-entry explanation, invoked American statements about the absence of danger to people on the ground, and reserved further responses. These were rival public justifications. They establish neither a mutually accepted legal characterization nor a bargain over the balloon's disposition. [C23-04, entire Austin statement; C23-05, entire MFA response.](https://www.globalsecurity.org/military/library/news/2023/02/mil-230204-dod01.htm) [Chinese response.](https://www.mfa.gov.cn/web/ziliao_674904/1179_674909/202302/t20230205_11019861.shtml)

The defense-call refusal is unusually clear because Beijing acknowledged it. On 9 February, Defense Ministry spokesman Tan Kefei said the American use of force had failed to create the proper atmosphere for military dialogue; China therefore did not accept the proposal for a call between defense ministers. The statement links an observable refusal to a publicly stated condition. It does not specify an apology formula, compensation, an operational concession, or an internal approval process that would make a call acceptable. We can identify conditional access without reconstructing an undisclosed price. [C23-06, complete statement.](http://www.mod.gov.cn/gfbw/qwfb/16200510.html)

Ellen Knickmeyer's contemporary AP report, read in its WTAP republication, attributes the attempted call after the shootdown to the Pentagon and distinguishes the defense communication arrangement from an always-open telephone between ministers: it involved a request for an encrypted call or videoconference. Its striking recollection that a telephone simply rang concerned **March 2009**, not a reporter's observation in February 2023. Named former officials and outside specialists offer competing explanations involving political permission and signaling. Those interviews illuminate possibilities; they do not expose the 2023 Chinese instruction. [C23-22, full report.](https://www.wtap.com/2023/02/10/it-just-rang-crises-us-china-hotline-goes-unanswered/)

Diplomatic access nevertheless continued. Both governments record a Wang–Blinken meeting at Munich on 18 February. The short Chinese account calls it informal contact at American request and says Wang demanded that Washington address the damage caused by using force. The longer American account records Blinken's demand that the airspace violation not recur, warnings about support to Russia, and an insistence that diplomatic dialogue remain open. Their accounts corroborate the encounter and continuing dispute, not agreement on fault. They also directly rule out a narrative in which all senior Chinese-American communication ceased until June. [C23-07, four substantive paragraphs; C23-08, both paragraphs.](https://www.globalsecurity.org/wmd/library/news/china/2023/02/china-230218-state01.htm) [Chinese Munich account.](https://www.mfa.gov.cn/zyxw/202302/t20230219_11027107.shtml)

## What the public record cannot tell us about Xi

A CNN report preserved by Citizen Digital on 11 February reported an American assessment, conveyed in a congressional briefing, that Xi and senior Chinese leaders might not have known about the mission over the United States. The underlying source was familiar with that briefing; the published account did not disclose the intelligence establishing Chinese knowledge. Its interviewed specialists disagreed over whether centralization implied poor information flow, limited delegation, or little room for unauthorized action. The uncertainty belongs in the proposition itself: **American officials assessed possible leadership ignorance; this case has not independently established what Xi knew or when.** [C23-20, full CNN report, especially opening and closing assessment passages.](https://citizen.digital/article/washington-believes-the-balloon-shot-down-over-the-atlantic-on-saturday-is-part-of-an-extensive-chin-n314171)

Christopher S. Chivvis and Senkai Hsia's March 2026 Carnegie study adds retrospective interviews with 33 former Biden officials. Its methods section expressly disclaims access to Chinese internal decision making. Its assessment that Xi was probably unaware rests on former American officials and American reporting, not a newly disclosed Chinese record. The useful additional process evidence concerns American preparations: former officials describe bargaining over the strategic channel's agenda, secrecy and public statements; Sarah Beran and Jake Sullivan explain their intended purposes. This is a valuable participant-based policy study, but its multiple citations should not be counted as independent Chinese corroboration. Here the read scope is printed pages 7–8 and 100–115, and notes 592–718, not the whole report. [C23-21, methodology pp. 7–8; knowledge claim p. 101/note 602; preparations pp. 102–104/notes 625–641.](https://assets.carnegieendowment.org/files/Chivvis_US-China-202603.pdf)

Kevin Chen Xian An's contemporary RSIS paper, read in full, usefully identifies different conceptions of crisis communication and presents centralized permission and strategic withholding as possible mechanisms. Its causal interpretation draws on public reporting and earlier studies, rather than interviews inside the balloon decision. Its proposition that a quick call could have prevented the crisis is therefore a counterfactual to test, not an observed result. We retain its alternatives without assuming that a working hotline would have resolved the sovereignty dispute or prevented either government's response. [C23-23, complete commentary and linked-source apparatus.](https://rsis.edu.sg/rsis-publication/idss/ip23017-a-crisis-in-communication-rethinking-how-beijing-and-washington-communicate/)

## May and June: a strategic channel opens while defense access remains contested

The paired records of the 10–11 May Sullivan–Wang meeting in Vienna offer a firmer observable basis than accounts of either side's psychological conversion. Both identify substantive exchanges, Taiwan-related concerns, Ukraine and continued use of the strategic communication channel. Washington frames this as responsible management of competition; Beijing emphasizes removing obstacles and arresting deterioration. Agreement to continue the channel coexisted with different descriptions of the relationship it would manage. [C23-09, complete White House readout; C23-10, complete Chinese readout, published 12 May.](https://bidenwhitehouse.archives.gov/briefing-room/statements-releases/2023/05/11/readout-of-national-security-advisor-jake-sullivans-meeting-with-chinese-communist-party-politburo-member-and-director-of-the-office-of-the-foreign-affairs-commission-wang-yi/) [Chinese text.](https://www.fmprc.gov.cn/zyxw/202305/t20230512_11075777.shtml)

Later that month, the two defense establishments still did not agree to the proposed Austin–Li Shangfu meeting in Singapore. Carla Babb's 30 May VOA dispatch quotes Pentagon spokesman Pat Ryder identifying the early-May invitation and its rejection, while reporting that Li had been under American sanctions since 2018. The Chinese Defense Ministry's own 31 May response says that contacts had **not been interrupted**, but insists on principles, bottom lines, American corrective action, and appropriate conditions. It does not name a specific sanction to be lifted. Consequently, the existence of sanctions, Chinese complaints about obstacles, and a refusal to meet must not be silently converted into a demonstrated single-condition bargain. The separate [Kissinger visit audit](../reporting/kissinger-2023-intermediary-audit.md) examines the July intermediary episode. [C23-11, complete Q&A; C23-12, full dispatch.](http://www.mod.gov.cn/gfbw/qwfb/16227655.html) [VOA report.](https://www.voanews.com/a/china-declines-us-offer-for-defense-talks-in-singapore-this-week-/7116032.html)

Blinken finally met Qin Gang on 18 June and Wang and Xi on 19 June. Qin's published account specifies several limited agreements: maintain senior exchanges, continue consultations on guiding principles and joint working groups, and encourage educational and other exchanges while discussing additional passenger flights. Qin accepted an invitation to visit the United States at a mutually convenient time. These are different commitment stages. An accepted invitation is not a completed visit; consultations on principles are not an agreed charter; discussion of flights is not operation of additional services. [C23-13, complete readout, especially the five “双方同意” paragraphs.](https://www.mfa.gov.cn/web/gjhdq_676201/gj_676203/bmz_679954/1206_680528/xgxw_680534/202306/t20230619_11099462.shtml)

Wang's 19 June account demanded an end to unilateral sanctions and suppression of Chinese technological development. Xi rejected great-power competition as the answer to the relationship and called for American assurances to become action. These statements show how broad principles framed concrete objections; they do not establish that Blinken accepted the requested policy changes. Blinken's same-day public account reaffirmed continued national-security restrictions and the American Taiwan policy, including the Taiwan Relations Act, three communiqués and Six Assurances. His disavowal of support for Taiwan independence did not amount to acceptance of Beijing's broader demands. The Chinese account's attribution of five assurances to Blinken should remain visibly an attribution, alongside the American statement of its own position. [C23-14 and C23-16, complete Chinese readouts; C23-15, opening statement and Q&A.](https://us.china-embassy.gov.cn/zgyw/202306/t20230619_11099675.htm) [Xi readout.](https://www.mfa.gov.cn/wjdt_674879/gjldrhd_674881/202306/t20230619_11099942.shtml) [Blinken transcript.](https://www.globalsecurity.org/military/library/news/2023/06/mil-230619-state04.htm)

Asked directly about military and crisis communications, Blinken said China had not agreed to move forward and that there had been no immediate progress. He nevertheless defended continued diplomacy. Thus the June visit is a particularly valuable unsuccessful observation within a broader reopening: even access to Xi did not announce the military agreement Washington sought. It limits the inference that a senior encounter reliably signals agreement across issues, and shows that Washington accepted continued engagement while a major request remained outstanding. [C23-15, Kylie Atwood Q&A and final follow-up question.](https://www.globalsecurity.org/military/library/news/2023/06/mil-230619-state04.htm)

## November: specified mechanisms, one documented tradeoff, and unsettled policy

At the 15 November Woodside summit, the two public records converge much more concretely. They identify resumption of high-level military communication, Defense Policy Coordination Talks and Military Maritime Consultative Agreement meetings, and telephone conversations between theater commanders. The Chinese account states a basis of equality and respect. This is evidence of agreement on identified mechanisms; it is stronger than general praise for dialogue. But the records do not supply a guaranteed response time, enforcement provision, or unconditional promise to keep every mechanism functioning through any future dispute. [C23-17, military-communication paragraph; C23-18, paragraph beginning “两国元首同意推动和加强”.](https://bidenwhitehouse.archives.gov/briefing-room/statements-releases/2023/11/15/readout-of-president-joe-bidens-meeting-with-president-xi-jinping-of-the-peoples-republic-of-china-2/) [Chinese summit account.](https://www.mfa.gov.cn/ziliao_674904/zt_674979/ywzt_675099/2023nzt/xjpfmgjxzmyshwtscxytjhzzdsscldrfzshy/zxxx_136234/202311/t20231116_11181125.shtml)

The summit also included counternarcotics cooperation, an intergovernmental AI dialogue and intended expansion of passenger flights and exchanges. One adjacent concession can be documented without inventing a comprehensive hidden package. A Commerce rule removed the Ministry of Public Security's Institute of Forensic Science from the Entity List, effective **16 November**, although published on 17 November. The notice records the removal and review procedure; it does not itself explain the diplomatic exchange. Contemporary reporting quotes named State Department spokesman Matthew Miller explaining that the listing had obstructed precursor-chemical cooperation and that the Chinese steps warranted delisting. This supports a specific American tradeoff connected to counternarcotics. It does **not** demonstrate that delisting purchased military calls, or that every summit deliverable depended on the same bargain. Nor does it establish the subsequent effectiveness of drug enforcement. [C23-24, 88 FR 80131–80132; C23-25, full report, Miller passages.](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2023-11-17/pdf/2023-25557.pdf) [Contemporary report.](https://www.voanews.com/amp/us-lifts-sanctions-on-chinese-institute-to-seek-fentanyl-cooperation-/7358446.html)

The same paired summit texts retain substantive disagreement. Xi called for an end to arming Taiwan and removal of unilateral sanctions; the White House reiterated opposition to unilateral changes in Taiwan's status quo and continued measures to protect sensitive technology. Their shared language endorsed communication and management of differences, while further discussion of relationship principles remained welcome. The documents do not show a comprehensive settlement of Taiwan, technological competition or the balloon's legal status. Reading shared phrasing beside the unresolved demands is more revealing than either declaring a reset or dismissing the mechanisms as empty ceremony. [C23-17, Taiwan, technology and principles paragraphs; C23-18, corresponding passages.]

Finally, distinguish announced resumption from completed operation. On 30 November, Wu Qian repeated the four military mechanisms in future-oriented terms and said the defense departments were communicating and coordinating implementation. The official English translation likewise says they **will resume**. This is evidence of follow-up work and an implementation commitment, not proof that all four had already met or would answer promptly during the next crisis. This case ends at that dated boundary; it does not borrow later meetings to complete the November record retrospectively. [C23-19, complete Chinese Q&A and official English answer.](http://www.mod.gov.cn/gfbw/xwfyr/lxjzhzt/2023njzh/2023n11y/16270152.html)

| Bargaining object | What the examined record establishes | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| Blinken's planned visit | US postponement on 3 February; visit held 18–19 June | A Chinese admission of espionage or agreed apology as the price |
| Diplomatic and strategic communication | Munich contact in February; Vienna meeting and agreement to maintain its channel in May | Complete diplomatic silence before June; causal sufficiency of one intermediary |
| Defense-minister contact | China acknowledged rejecting the February call; May meeting request remained unsuccessful | An internal mandate or a single, fully specified sanction-removal condition |
| Military mechanisms | No June agreement reported by Blinken; four mechanisms named in November; implementation coordination acknowledged on 30 November | All four operational by summit day; automatic crisis access or a permanent guarantee |
| Counternarcotics | Summit cooperation commitment, specific institute delisting, named US explanation linking it to cooperation | The price of military reopening; realized enforcement effectiveness |
| Political principles | Repeated Chinese appeals; some shared summit language; continued discussions | Acceptance of every Chinese formulation or removal of substantive disagreement |

## What this changes in the Solomon reassessment

**Retained within scope: relationship purpose and selective access.** S-C01 anticipates variation between confrontation and building cooperation. This episode shows simultaneous cooperation and confrontation across channels, requiring a more granular unit than “the relationship.” Public Chinese refusals explicitly connect access to political conditions, while the American postponement supplies a counterpart check. Claims that this is uniquely Chinese require wider comparison.

**Narrowed: principles, authority and the meaning of senior access.** S-P05 and S-P19 direct attention to broad formulations and claims about earlier assurances. They remain useful only with the original and attributed texts kept separate. June's Xi meeting without military agreement is an unsuccessful senior encounter relevant to testing S-P24. It does not by itself disprove Solomon's comparative claim that senior intervention is the most reliable sign of impending closure, or establish how it compares with concrete drafting and other indicators. November establishes leader endorsement of specified mechanisms; without draft instructions it cannot identify when authorization changed or whether leaders resolved a previously unmovable bureaucratic obstacle.

**Unresolved: institutional causes of rigidity.** The records support neither S-C19's strong diagnostic inference from polemics to leadership conflict nor the opposite claim that centralized control necessarily made all decisions coherent. Public statements and US assessments cannot distinguish a deliberate bargaining refusal, internal clearance delays, incompatible interests, or combinations of them. Solomon's own attention to bureaucratic audiences and authority remains relevant; asserting a new Xi-era mechanism requires evidence of the actual approval path.

**Revised mechanism: communication is an issue to negotiate, with an implementation stage.** The 2023 record contains differentiated institutional commitments rather than a simple progression from distrust to personal rapport to settlement. The military and counternarcotics outcomes show why the price and status of each channel must be recorded separately. November's follow-up is not yet evidence for the full asymmetrical implementation bargaining claimed in S-P25: no subsequent Chinese demand for additional consideration is established here. The defensible update is that channels can be preserved, withheld and reactivated selectively while the underlying rivalry continues.

This case rests primarily on public accounts prepared for political audiences. It lacks Chinese internal instructions, bilateral minutes, draft exchanges on military resumption, and direct access to the intelligence behind assessments of Xi's awareness. The 2026 interview study improves American process access but does not close those gaps. Its contribution is a recoverable sequence of bounded moves and a clearer test of competing explanations, not a claim to Solomon-equivalent confidential access.
