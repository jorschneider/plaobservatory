# Agricultural biotechnology approvals: repeated procedural bargains and the limits of public proof

Research date: 10 September 2026. This is a deliberately selected comparison of Enlist E3 soybeans (DAS-44406-6) and Optimum GLY canola (DP73496), with an Enlist renewal as a bounded positive observation. It is not a statistically matched sample or an estimate of the effect of Phase One. The companion [source register](../sources/agricultural-approvals-register.json) records reading limits and source dependencies; the [move ledger](agricultural-approvals-moves.json) separates acts, assertions, and inferences.

The strongest finding is **repeated negotiation over the administration of market access**, before and after January 2020. Chinese import permissions mattered to American planting decisions because companies managed access to several export markets before broadly launching seed. Public records demonstrate that mechanism and document extraordinarily old applications eventually receiving Chinese certificates. They do not disclose why particular dossiers waited, whether a leader ordered their release, or how much commercial delay China alone caused. One measurable improvement—the five-year Enlist renewal—also prevents a defensible account from turning into a narrative of unqualified nonimplementation.

## 1. Define the permission before counting the outcome

A genetically engineered “event” is the particular genetic modification identified in a regulatory dossier; a brand may cover seed incorporating that event. The Chinese documents here authorize **imports for processing**, including feed-related uses under the agreement. They do not authorize Chinese farmers to grow the crops. The historical Chinese import rules distinguish research, production, and processing applications; Article 13 describes origin-country authorization, safety documentation, testing, National Biosafety Committee (NBC) assessment, and ministerial approval for processing imports. This historical version is used to reconstruct the procedure, not offered as a complete statement of law in 2026. [MARA import measures, Articles 4 and 13–16](https://kjs.moa.gov.cn/zcjd/201904/t20190418_6184802.htm).

The operational distinction persists in USDA's 2024 description: foreign import applications move through dossier handling, domestic verification testing, NBC assessment, and a MARA administrative decision. Requests for further information can produce another review cycle. Domestic cultivation additionally involves the relevant variety registration and seed production/operation permissions. Traders' consignment documentation is another layer. This is an American official reconstruction of Chinese administration, not access to either selected application's file. [USDA FAS annual report, PDF pp. 11–16 and 19](https://apps.fas.usda.gov/newgainapi/api/Report/DownloadReportByFileName?fileName=Agricultural+Biotechnology+Annual_Beijing_China+-+People%27s+Republic+of_CH2024-0001.pdf).

## 2. The procedural bargain predates Phase One

The pre-2017 baseline matters. In June 2016, the joint Strategic and Economic Dialogue outcomes already committed China to timely, continuing, science-based review and a decision after NBC safety evaluation. They also paired Chinese regulatory revision with US study of asynchronous approvals and further discussion. Thus, both administrative process and the mismatch between countries' authorization calendars were on the bilateral agenda well before the tariff war. This paragraph of an extensive joint outcome document does not establish implementation. [Treasury, 2016 economic fact sheet, agricultural biotechnology passage](https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/jl0484).

The May 2017 joint 100-day plan became more operational. Eight pending US applications were to reach NBC evaluation by the end of May. China undertook to avoid irrelevant data requests; explain the relevance of additional information in writing; reconvene often enough after resubmission; and issue certificates within twenty working days when the safety evaluation was passed. This was not an undertaking to approve every application by May. Nor does the published clause name the eight, so this inquiry does not silently assign either selected event to that group. The Chinese and English releases are two versions of one negotiated instrument, not independent confirmation of compliance. [Chinese joint text, item 3](https://wjb.mof.gov.cn/gongzuodongtai/201705/t20170512_2599646.htm); [US Commerce counterpart, item 3](https://www.commerce.gov/news/fact-sheets/2017/05/fact-sheet-initial-actions-us-china-economic-cooperation-100-day-plan).

Industry was already contesting performance. BIO's Joseph Damond testified in April 2018 about ten pending applications averaging five years and four months since Chinese submission. That is the **age of pending cases**, not a measured mean duration of completed approvals. His requested remedies included quarterly NBC meetings, fewer duplicative tests, and removal of the origin-country approval prerequisite. This is evidence of organized pressure and specific demands; it is not a neutral audit of every dossier. [BIO testimony, pp. 3–6 and appendix](https://www.uscc.gov/sites/default/files/USCC%20Hearing%20on%20China%20Agriculture%20-%20Testimony%20of%20the%20Biotechnology%20Innovation%20Organization.pdf).

Phase One Annex 16 therefore combined repetition with additions. Its core clock is **an average of no more than 24 months from formal application submission to a final decision approving or disapproving** feed/processing products. It is neither a guarantee that every application receives approval nor an absolute two-year deadline for each dossier. Other provisions cover five-working-day completeness feedback, applicant complaints, written and relevant further-data requests, sufficient NBC meetings, authorization lasting at least five years, year-round applications, advance dossier prescreening, and twenty-working-day administrative completion following a successful NBC evaluation. [Agreement, Annex 16, paragraphs 2 and 4–7, English PDF pp. 41–43](https://ustr.gov/sites/default/files/files/agreements/phase%20one%20agreement/Economic_And_Trade_Agreement_Between_The_United_States_And_China_Text.pdf).

Two qualifications change how its transparency promises should be described. First, prescreening after submission of a US dossier is not an explicit abolition of every origin-country approval requirement. Second, **Chapter 3 footnote 1 expressly excludes proposed or final measures implementing the chapter, its annexes and appendix from Article 8.5's public-comment provision**. It would be wrong to project that general final-provisions rule indiscriminately into agricultural implementation. Annex 16 supplies its own, narrower process obligations. The Chinese text confirms the average-duration language and the chapter-specific exception. [Chinese agreement, PDF p. 23 footnote and pp. 47–51, Annex 16](https://www.gov.cn/guowuyuan/2020-01/16/5469650/files/0637e57d99ea4f968454206af8782dd7.pdf).

### Public correspondence behind the 2017 bargain

A further archival check reaches beyond published outcomes. USDA released 917 pages of correspondence under FOIA in August 2019, subsequently posted to MuckRock. The signed response explains that the requester accepted previously available April 2017–August 2018 records instead of a new, broader search; commercial and personal information was redacted. Only selected chains were read here, not the entire release. [Signed release letter, pp. 1–3](https://cdn.muckrock.com/foia_files/2019/09/17/2019-DA-05664-F20Signed20Final20Response20Letter.pdf).

In May 2 and May 8 emails, Hunt Shipman sought assistance arranging Dow AgroSciences CEO Tim Hassinger's meeting with Secretary Sonny Perdue. On May 9, USDA adviser Rebeckah Adcock requested seed-trade talking points; Shipman supplied documents named as Enlist corn and soybean regulatory summaries and a China backgrounder. Their contents are absent from the acquired files: the soybean filename cannot identify an event as Enlist E3. On May 16, lobbyist Joe Bischoff supplied proposed congressional questions; Adcock challenged their framing after China's announcement. On June 14, Hassinger recounted meeting Perdue the previous month, credited the 100-day arrangement for **Enlist corn** import approval, and promised continued work on remaining traits. Adcock replied that she had passed the news to the secretary. These are acquired emails, not merely a journalist's quotations. [USDA release, part 4, PDF/printed pp. 197–199, 506–509, 603–605 and 205–206](https://cdn.muckrock.com/foia_files/2019/09/17/2019-DA-05664-F20Rony204.pdf).

The published May 17 hearing completes one further link. Representative Rodney Davis used the proposal's premise about US-approved technology facing foreign barriers and risks in global markets, but substantially rewrote the question. He explicitly acknowledged the new 100-day plan and asked how China would be held accountable. Perdue placed biotechnology alongside beef, defended the US safety case, and said Chinese clearance could help persuade other markets; this exchange supplied no additional deadline or enforcement procedure. The observable result is a related public question and response, not verbatim adoption or proof that Adcock caused the changes. [House Agriculture hearing, PDF pp. 64–65 / printed pp. 60–61](https://agriculture.house.gov/UploadedFiles/115-06_-_25545.pdf).

The correspondence establishes access, preparation, feedback and the CEO's expressed causal belief. It does not recover a Chinese decision instruction, confirm the meeting occurred on its requested May 10 date, identify who revised the later congressional question, or make every American official and industry representative an independent witness to the cause of approval. It also shows an administration adviser questioning an industry's preferred public framing, rather than simple unanimity on the American side.

## 3. Enlist E3: the political setting is recoverable; the decisive instruction is not

Contemporary Reuters reporting places the Chinese submission in 2013 and an initially hoped-for US launch in 2015. In May 2018, it reported renewed US pressure to include biotechnology approvals in the emerging trade package, citing people familiar with the discussions. The report did not recover the specific proposed language, and the contacted ministries did not provide confirmation. It nevertheless places this regulatory issue inside actual trade bargaining, rather than inferring bargaining solely from a later approval date. [Reuters, 23 May 2018, syndicated by Business Times](https://www.businesstimes.com.sg/international/us-wants-china-approve-more-biotech-crops-under-trade-deal-sources).

The Chinese primary record then supplies an unusually important correction to the usual chronology. Row 5 of the 2018 import list identifies DAS-44406-6, Dow AgroSciences, certificate 农基安证字（2018）第005号, and a validity period beginning **20 December 2018**. The public announcement appeared on 8 January 2019, during bilateral talks. Therefore, the date on which journalists encountered the decision cannot by itself establish that the January negotiating session caused it. The list discloses neither the NBC recommendation date nor the day the applicant received the certificate. [MARA, 2018 import approvals, PDF p. 1, row 5](https://www.moa.gov.cn/ztzl/zjyqwgz/spxx/201901/P020190108439855980836.pdf).

DTN's same-day original reporting helps recover that gap. Corteva spokesman Gregg Schmidt said the company was awaiting official certificates. Its account also described earlier seed preparation and restricted channels developed while broad commercialization remained blocked. Such preparations show commercial adaptation to uncertainty, but the interview cannot reveal MARA's internal reason for its timing. [Emily Unglesbee, DTN, 8 January 2019](https://www.dtnpf.com/agriculture/web/ag/crops/article/2019/01/08/china-approves-five-crop-traits-e3-2).

On 21 February, Corteva and MS Technologies announced the 2019 US launch after Philippine authorization. Their release distinguished cultivation approvals in the United States, Canada and Brazil from import permissions in China and the Philippines, and referred to the company's commercialization policy. The supply-chain mechanism is concrete: access to importing markets affected when a US seed developer would broadly release a trait. China was a consequential gate, but it was not the last reported gate in this sequence. Announced availability and projected acreage are not independently verified sales or harvested exports. [Corteva/MS Technologies release](https://www.corteva.com/us/press-releases/corteva-and-ms-tech-announce-2019-us-enlist-e3-soybean-launch.html).

This original approval **predates Phase One**. Its later renewal offers a different test. MARA's 2021 list explicitly labels DAS-44406-6 as a renewal, certificate 第319号, valid 17 December 2021–16 December 2026: five years, compared with the original three-year term. The start date precedes original expiry; publication followed it. Without the service record, neither seamless documentary delivery nor an actual interruption of trade can be inferred. The observation is consistent with implementation of the five-year authorization provision. It cannot establish the speed of new-event reviews or prove that the agreement caused the longer term. [MARA, 2021 import list, PDF p. 2, row 18](https://www.moa.gov.cn/ztzl/zjyqwgz/spxx/202112/P020211227585561085049.pdf).

## 4. DP73496: a long Chinese backlog, and a necessary non-Chinese control

The canola case deliberately follows an older application across the agreement. Reuters's January 2019 factbox placed DP73496 among applications still pending and dated submission to July 2012. “Not among this batch's approvals” is not equivalent to a formal rejection. The underlying application and receipt are missing; the reported month is retained as reported evidence rather than upgraded into an administrative timestamp. [Reuters factbox, 8 January 2019](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/factbox-china-approves-gmo-soybean-corn-canola-traits-163125348--finance.html).

MARA's second 2022 import list, published in January 2023, finally records DP73496. Row 6 names Pioneer International, the gat4621 herbicide-tolerance trait and certificate 农基安证字（2022）第053号, effective 5 January 2023–4 January 2028. It is an original processing-import authorization, not domestic cultivation permission or a renewal. The title's “2022” and the effective date's “2023” are not interchangeable. The same list contains other crops and applicants, including Brazilian sugarcane developers, so its entire batch cannot be counted as a set of US concessions. [MARA, second 2022 import list, PDF pp. 1–2](https://www.moa.gov.cn/ztzl/zjyqwgz/spxx/202301/P020230113566699597851.pdf).

This yields approximately ten and a half years between the reported Chinese submission and certificate effectiveness. Yet it does **not** establish ten and a half years of commercial delay caused solely by China. Canada authorized unconfined environmental release and feed use in May 2012; those were Canadian permissions with defined uses. The European Union's decision records a food/feed application on 15 May 2012, a favorable EFSA opinion on 17 June 2021, and Commission authorization on 31 March 2022, excluding cultivation. It also records no opinion from the member-state Standing or Appeal Committees. The decision does not explain the entire decade before approval. [CFIA DD2012-88, background and decision](https://inspection.canada.ca/en/plant-varieties/plants-novel-traits/approved-under-review/decision-documents/dd-2012-88); [EU Decision 2022/529, recitals 1, 3 and 16; Articles 2–3](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dec_impl/2022/529/oj/eng/pdf).

A same-event comparison is more informative here than an unqualified claim that every other regulator acted promptly. European approval arrived before the Chinese certificate but after a very long European process. The roughly nine-month interval between those final decisions is a chronological difference, not an estimate of China's incremental causal effect on launch: actual readiness, other permissions, product development and commercial calendars still matter.

Reporting also separates announcement from implementation. Reuters on 13 January quoted Corteva welcoming authorization while pursuing the remaining approvals. Consultant John Baize speculated that approval could signal goodwill after the Biden–Xi Bali meeting. That is an identified observer's interpretation, not evidence that either leader instructed the regulator. By 26 January, Western Producer's original interview with Corteva's Jeff Loessin described China as the final global authorization needed and explained remaining stewardship and industry communication work. The articles capture different dates and levels of readiness; their company statements should not be flattened into an instantaneous launch on 5 January. [Reuters, 13 January 2023](https://www.agriculture.com/markets/newswire/update-2-china-approves-import-of-bayers-gmo-alfalfa-corteva-canola-after-a-decade); [Sean Pratt, Western Producer, 26 January, updated 27 January](https://www.producer.com/news/chinese-approval-opens-door-for-new-ht-canola/).

Corteva announced commercial launch on 14 February for Canadian and US planting in 2023, with Australian demonstrations in 2023 and full launch in 2024. That planned sequence is consistent with approval enabling a commercially prepared product; the release does not measure realized planting or quantify foregone revenues. [Corteva launch announcement](https://www.corteva.com/ca-en/about-corteva/media-centre/corteva-announces-commercial-launch-optimum-gly.html).

## 5. What remained contested through 2026

ASTA's Sam Crowell told the December 2025 USTR hearing that Chinese approvals averaged 69 months and that two pending products had waited at least nine years. He described uncertain meeting calendars, unexplained data requests and difficulty clarifying technical questions. These are attributable industry allegations, not a disclosed application-level dataset. His explanation of export-market clearances and commingled supply chains supports the mechanism identified in the two cases. It does not isolate Chinese strategic intent. When officials pressed about technology transfer and seed theft, his answer described vulnerability rather than documenting a specific appropriation tied to an approval. [USTR hearing, pp. 8–13 and 25–28](https://ustr.gov/sites/default/files/files/Issue_Areas/Enforcement/Section%20301/Hearing%20transcript%20full%20without%20index.pdf).

USTR's March 2026 assessment says the average-time promise remained unmet and repeats complaints about opaque NBC scheduling and additional information. It also says results of the 2025 NBC meetings had not been published, departing from past practice. That limits public reconstruction; it does not establish that the meetings issued no approvals. Counts of Chinese domestic cultivation authorizations are also not a matched comparison with foreign import dossiers. [2026 National Trade Estimate, printed pp. 82–83 / PDF pp. 90–91](https://ustr.gov/sites/default/files/files/Press/Releases/2026/National%20Trade%20Estimate%20Report%202026.pdf).

China's April 2025 white paper supplies the other government's account of agricultural implementation, listing restored access for meats, dairy and several crops. Its agriculture-access subsection offers no event-level response to the two biotech timelines reconstructed here. It is useful evidence of the public defense Beijing chose to present, not an adjudication of Annex 16. [Chinese white paper, II(3)](https://chinawto.mofcom.gov.cn/article/ap/p/202504/20250403576920.shtml).

After the May 2026 summit, USTR published Ambassador Greer's statement that China had agreed to review biotechnology traits for scientific approval. The published excerpt names no events or deadlines and records no completed certificates. Its significance for this inquiry is that trait approval remained an object of senior economic bargaining six years after the detailed agreement. It is not evidence that an unnamed outstanding application was subsequently approved. [USTR, 18 May 2026, agriculture excerpt](https://ustr.gov/about/policy-offices/press-office/press-releases/2026/may/president-trumps-state-visit-china-delivers-historic-deals-and-greater-market-access-american).

## 6. What this changes in the Solomon update

The observations support the continuing-implementation element of S-P25, without establishing its full claim of asymmetric demands for adherence and accommodation. An important refinement is that officials repeatedly bargained over when expert committees meet, what information may be demanded, how applicants obtain reasons, and how long permission lasts. This is a substantial administrative architecture. A broad preference for flexible principles (S-P05) cannot adequately characterize the resulting instruments. But procedural specificity was already observable in 2017, so January 2020 should not be portrayed as its sudden arrival.

The files leave senior authorization and strategic delay (S-C10, S-P11, S-P24) as hypotheses with unequal support. Senior governments demonstrably negotiated the subject; businesses demonstrably attached value to approvals; events eventually received certificates. The decisive link—an instruction to hold or release an individually identified dossier for a diplomatic concession—has not been recovered. Repeated industry testimony and USTR reports are not separate witnesses to that missing instruction if they rely on the same applicants.

Bureaucratic complexity (S-C12) is visible in the procedure, but interagency conflict or leadership weakness (S-C11) is not established by a long queue. Scientific questions, administrative capacity, applicant responsiveness, commercial choices and political control may coexist. EU delay on the same canola event is a useful warning against diagnosing a specifically Chinese cultural mechanism from elapsed time alone. The appropriate update to Solomon is a more explicit account of regulatory authority operating through interdependent markets, disciplined by his own warning that interests matter more than tactical ingenuity (S-C18).

This inquiry therefore distinguishes an established **transmission mechanism**—foreign import permission affects developers' broad commercialization decisions—from an incompletely observed **decision mechanism** inside China. The next decisive records are dated original submissions and acceptance notices; all versions of requests and responses; commissioned-testing dates; NBC recommendations; ministerial decisions and service dates; and contemporaneous bilateral interventions naming the event. A prompt first-time import approval with the same information would be a stronger counterexample than a renewal or an unverified inference from another country's approval date. Neither selected event supplies the complete post-2020 application cohort, including denials and unresolved cases, needed to independently audit the average-duration promise.
