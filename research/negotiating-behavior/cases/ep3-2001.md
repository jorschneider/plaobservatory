# EP-3, 2001: ending detention while preserving the dispute

Research dates: 15–16 September 2026. The public sequence through July 3 is now supplemented by seventeen complete contemporary State cables (107 physical PDF pages) on the April 1–11 crew negotiations, April 24–30 aircraft arrangements and related senior contacts, and a recovered April 17 CIA information report. The [move ledger](ep3-2001-moves.json), [initial register](../sources/ep3-2001-access-register.json), [drafting-cable audit](../manuscript/development/ep3-drafting-cables.md) and [Jost source-chain study](../literature/readings/jost-crisis-institutions.md) distinguish reported private exchanges from internal instructions and full drafts still missing. These American records do not supply an independent Chinese transcript or a complete crisis archive.

The April 11 arrangement over the American EP-3 crew worked without producing a shared account of the collision. China publicly accepted a letter as an apology and authorized the crew's departure. The released English text expressed regret and sorrow, retained an emergency-landing account and deferred the causes of the incident and return of the aircraft to subsequent talks. The negotiable package had become smaller than the dispute. This is the clearest established feature of the arrangement: **an immediate action could be separated from the responsibility, legal and policy questions that neither government publicly relinquished.** Whether that separation, a particular expression, changing assessments or concern for the wider relationship caused acceptance remains less securely documented. [EP303, paragraphs 2–7; EP305, departure and unfinished-incident passages]

That distinction matters for an update of Solomon. The case supports his attention to partial agreements, reserved positions and public presentation. It also warns against inferring a unified, culturally determined bargaining strategy from a government's firm public line. The documents reveal different jobs performed by public statements, legal counsel, diplomatic correspondence and subsequent technical negotiations. An account that calls all of these “face-saving” loses much of the actual process.

## From competing claims to a limited instrument

The collision involved a US reconnaissance aircraft and a Chinese fighter near Hainan. The damaged American aircraft landed in China; its twenty-four crew members and the aircraft remained there. Those basic circumstances created several distinct objects of negotiation: the people, the aircraft, responsibility for the collision, the permissibility of reconnaissance and emergency entry, and measures to prevent recurrence. This chapter does not adjudicate the flight mechanics or the competing legal claims. Its starting point is that the two governments publicly assigned different meanings to the same incident. [EP301, opening account and positions 1–5; EP302, editorial note]

On April 3, China's Foreign Ministry demanded that the United States accept responsibility, explain itself, apologize and prevent repetition. Its argument was not exclusively about the wider relationship or the consequences of a death: it also made a specific allegation about the American aircraft's movement and linked that allegation to fault. It then advanced legal arguments about reconnaissance in the exclusive economic zone, entry without permission and aircraft immunity. A single generalization that Chinese negotiators cared about consequences while Americans cared about physical causation does not describe this public statement. [EP301, collision account and numbered positions]

Bush's April 3 statement, meanwhile, demanded the return of both crew and plane and warned that the incident could undermine the bilateral relationship. It also reported that the defense attaché had seen the crew and found them in good health. The public demand coupled two objects that would later follow different schedules. It did not specify a deadline that automatically triggered retaliation. Nor can a public demand establish all concessions the US delegation was authorized to consider privately. [EP302, printed p.364]

A contemporaneous internal document helps recover what existed behind American public language. On April 6, acting Defense General Counsel Daniel Dell'Orto sent the defense secretary and deputy secretary a packet titled “EP-3 Incident—Guidance on Legal Issues.” The covering memorandum says Defense and the Chairman's legal offices had developed the papers in consultation with State's Legal Adviser. Its talking points express regret for the Chinese loss while defending the US flight, emergency landing and immunity. The responsibility argument is partly conditional on the reported actions of the Chinese fighter, a qualification that should survive any historical retelling. [EP321, physical pp.1–3, OSD/1809–1811]

This packet establishes a separation between sympathetic language and legal concession inside the US drafting apparatus before the final letter. It also demonstrates some actual interagency coordination: the clearance page attached to aircraft-immunity guidance records approvals from State, Defense, the Joint Chiefs and the relevant regional office. That evidence does not establish agreement on every crisis-management choice. It establishes clearance of a particular piece of guidance. Another page specifies an oral, off-camera response if pressed, showing that the intended audience and manner of communication were themselves managed. A government could maintain an extensive legal argument without putting all of it into its most visible public statement. [EP321, physical pp.9,14, OSD/1817,1822]

The packet is not an early version of the eventual temporal bargain. It treats aircraft and crew status in separate legal sections, but its talking points still demand immediate aircraft release. Nor is it a sequence of Prueher-letter drafts: there are no authenticated intermediate versions linking its wording to the April 11 instrument. Its value is narrower and firmer. It shows what some US officials were preparing to defend and how conciliatory expressions could coexist with those defenses. [EP321, physical pp.2–3,5–8,17–18]

## The reported draft negotiations and the exchange

The new [cable audit](../manuscript/development/ep3-drafting-cables.md) supplies the detailed reconstruction used in Chapter 4. Four observations can now be stated from contemporary reporting. First, Zhou's April 7 claim that China's revised draft had high-level clearance did not prevent later revisions. Prueher objected especially to reduced clarity about release and return timing. Second, repeated American rejection of *apologize* coexisted with acceptance of other changes; the accepted minor April 9 revision remains unspecified. Third, the embassy resisted providing an advance signed copy while seeking sufficient assurance of Tang's response; the exact oral wording remained undisclosed until the encounter. Fourth, the transfer memorandum was revised by phone despite Lu's initial assertion that no time remained for changes. The inserted word *verbal* and removed thanks are reported revisions, not a recovered complete draft pair. [EPD04–06; EPD09, paras 3,6–7,10–14]

At the final meeting, Prueher stated his understanding that the crew would be free, delivered his letter, heard Tang's prepared statement and pressed for clarification of the departure procedures. The embassy recorded the difference between Tang's Chinese *daoqian* and the interpreter's English *very sorry*. Tang anticipated next-day departure, and Zheng confirmed charter-arrival permission. The sequence establishes the relationship between letter, assurance and arrangements more directly than the public outcome alone. It does not reveal the complete internal Chinese decision or prove that any single wording change caused acceptance. [EPD08, paras 3–14]

## What April 11 settled—and what it reserved

The official English release bears Ambassador Joseph Prueher's name and addresses Foreign Minister Tang Jiaxuan. It begins on behalf of the United States government. Its first expression of sorrow concerns the Chinese loss. The next addresses the absence of verbal clearance for entry and landing, within a paragraph describing a severely damaged aircraft following emergency procedures and acknowledging uncertainty about the full incident. It then turns to actions: a meeting, an expectation of prompt crew departure, an April 18 start date, and a proposed agenda. [EP303, complete letter]

These clauses have different statuses. The letter records agreement to meet; it states the US understanding and expectation that the crew will leave; it puts development of a plan for aircraft return on the agenda. Its acknowledgment that China intends to raise reconnaissance is not a promise to stop reconnaissance. Treating all of the clauses as equally binding substantive concessions would expand the arrangement beyond its words. China's own acceptance announcement supplies the counterpart's public action: Tang says the government will allow departure after necessary formalities while maintaining that the incident is not concluded. April 11 authorization is also distinct from the crew's subsequent journey home. [EP303, paragraphs 4–7; EP305]

The Chinese presentation requires equally close reading. The MFA delivery report labels the letter a **致歉信** and reproduces selected passages using **深表歉意**. It is an official Chinese account, but it is not a complete Chinese version of the whole English document. The absent emergency context and aircraft-return agenda therefore cannot simply be presented as mistranslation of a supposedly parallel full text. We have an English diplomatic-letter release and a Chinese official publication selecting and interpreting parts of it. Those are different documentary forms. [EP304, all four paragraphs]

The distinction was not invisible to the Chinese audience. In Sun Yuxi's April 11 briefing, a question explicitly contrasts the demand for **道歉** with **深表歉意** and asks whether China has compromised. Sun replies by stressing the official US letter, China's humanitarian decision and continuing negotiations. Later exchanges reserve the investigation and aircraft disposition and repeat the demand to end reconnaissance. Keeping the Chinese terms is more accurate than importing an English word into the question and assuming the resulting gloss captures the dispute. [EP306, questions 1–4,6]

The two governments could thus proceed while differing publicly over the concession's meaning. That observation supports a mechanism of politically acceptable presentation; it does not establish that translation alone unlocked the agreement. It is also compatible with a simpler strategic account: both sides had reasons to end an increasingly damaging detention dispute and used tolerable language to implement a decision reached for those reasons. To distinguish the accounts, we need to know when release became authorized and how that timing related to successive language changes. A final text cannot supply its own bargaining history.

## Authority, channels and the problem of retrospective explanation

The newly acquired April 7–9 cables establish repeated consultation with Washington and Chinese assertions of high-level clearance. The April 8 report records Zhou's request for a Powell-to-Qian letter confirming Prueher's authority after Washington declined to send a special envoy. The April 11 meeting report records delivery of that authority letter, citing the president, before delivery of the distinct Prueher-to-Tang settlement letter. Fleischer's same-day public statement that Bush approved the wording is therefore supplemented by a contemporary account of the documents handed over. The full authority-letter text and original presidential instruction remain unacquired. [EPD04–06; EPD08, paras 3–4; EP307]

Prueher's 2019 interview supplies valuable leads about Powell's rapid approvals, Lieberthal's advice to move beyond fault and additional Chinese channels. It is also a warning about source independence. Prueher explicitly says he studied Solomon; his recollection is partly organized through that interpretive framework. Moreover, his remembered wording about China not hearing a landing request differs from the released letter's reference to absent verbal clearance. Use the interview to identify questions and witnesses, and the letter to establish what was delivered. [EP313, EP-3 discussion; EP303, paragraph 3]

Tyler Jost's article, now read completely with its notes and visuals, argues that separated military and diplomatic information channels impaired the scrutiny of crisis reporting. This mechanism differs from lack of authority: an empowered official can decide on an account that has not been challenged. The EP-3 narrative draws on Chinese publications, coded interviews, US cables and a CIA source, which are not equally direct evidence of what leaders knew or why they acted. The meeting dataset chiefly covers 1950–1989; it is not a log of Jiang's 2001 deliberations. The article's Xi implications remain competing hypotheses. [JCI-01–03; critical reading linked above]

The recovered CIA document C00785245 is an **unevaluated information report**, distributed April 17, with source and date-of-information fields redacted. It begins after the April 12 release. Its qualified account of likely recognition inside China's senior leadership does not establish when a named leader learned a fact or what caused release authorization. Its further claim that Beijing expected continued US reconnaissance while pressing the demand to stop it is useful as a contemporary account of different demands and expectations, with its uncertain sourcing preserved. It is not a Chinese decision record or a finished intelligence-community assessment. [JCI-04, both pages, paras 1–3]

The letter-identification problem is now narrower. Jost's p.83 and note 167 attribute settlement wording to Powell–Qian correspondence. EPD08 records two letters: an authority letter between those officials, and the Prueher–Tang settlement instrument reproduced in full. Their existence does not make their contents interchangeable. The reproduced letter controls the attribution of the two expressions of sorrow and the emergency-clearance language. [EPD08, paras 3–4,14; EP303]

Jost's appendix codes four Chinese goals as unsuccessful. That objective-based measure asks a different question from whether the narrower crew-release bargain worked. Neither measure should silently replace the other. The apparently conflicting fatality entry also needs its qualification: Appendix p.41 dates the death before the crisis and the released data code crisis battlefield cost as zero, consistent with article note 28. No coding-error claim follows from the abbreviated main table. [JCI-01–03]

The alternatives imply different missing observations. An information-failure account needs the reports reaching Jiang, who could contest them and whether a corrected assessment preceded release authorization. A deliberate-pressure account needs evidence that leaders recognized the limits of their claims but held the crew to obtain a specific concession. A relationship-preservation account needs a decision connecting the costs of continued detention to acceptance of the limited package. Public insistence on responsibility is compatible with all three. Later recollection alone does not reliably select among them.

## Aircraft return was another negotiation

The April 18 announcement of talks names Chinese delegation head Lu Shumin and reiterates responsibility, reconnaissance and prevention demands. Its persistence of disagreement after authorization of crew departure is substantive contrary evidence to a comprehensive-settlement narrative. It does not show that China repudiated the letter: that letter had explicitly preserved further discussion. [EP308; EP303, paragraph 7]

The newly recovered [April 24–30 cables](../manuscript/development/ep3-aircraft-cables.md) provide a private sequence before those later public milestones. On April 24, Zhou described flying the plane out as symbolically difficult but not the core issue. Washington's April 25 instruction authorized discussion of a politically binding document under explicit substantive limits and reserved approval before commitment to signature. On April 29, Zhou delivered a Chinese non-paper without requesting American acceptance and separately announced inspection permission. The embassy reports Chinese officials explicitly ruling out flyout on April 29–30, even while accommodating an accompanying attaché officer and further discussion of inspection days. [EPAIR-01–06]

The April 30 power-supply dispute ended with proposed local discussion, not recorded approval. After contradictory ministry responses, a senior official said the ministry did not understand the technical matter and the PLA specialists had left for Haikou. The Americans identified a power cart but made rental conditional on approval. This shows a practical question moving toward technically competent officers without establishing who could alter the political flyout prohibition. Nor does the April record exclude a subsequent May proposal. It narrows the opposing later recollections without resolving them. [EPAIR-06, para 4; EP313 and DEV-CR01]

The aircraft then passed through distinct stages. On April 29, China announced completion of its evidence collection and permission for US personnel to inspect the EP-3. It also reported discussions of payments and future maritime safety consultations, with final aircraft disposition still to come. On May 29, the MFA announced agreement in principle to removal of the disassembled aircraft by commercial An-124, with technical details unresolved. Inspection permission and agreement in principle were not completed return. [EP309; EP310]

The June 7 Pentagon briefing makes some of the ensuing work visible. Runway load assessment would determine loading and flight requirements; technicians, equipment and local support had to be arranged. Quigley estimated that component removal was still weeks away and reported no communications hindrance under the arrangements his team had reached. These details do not eliminate bargaining pressure, but they supply concrete alternatives to interpreting duration as an effort to exhaust the Americans. The July 3 Chinese announcement reports completed disassembly and return using a US-chartered aircraft while payment negotiations remained open. [EP311, complete EP-3 section; EP312]

Prueher later claimed that an earlier opportunity to fly the repaired aircraft out had been lost after a change in the US approach. Without the underlying permission, terms, dates and authority, the account cannot establish that China revoked an agreed option—or that American bureaucratic behavior alone caused the eventual transport method. It remains a consequential lead. [EP313, aircraft-return passage]

The analytical correction is to define what was being implemented. Returning the people implemented a limited immediate understanding. Deciding how to return the plane addressed an expressly deferred subject. Discussing costs left yet another issue open. If all post-letter negotiation is classified as renegotiation of completed commitments, the historian will manufacture evidence for a tactic simply by ignoring the agreement's scope.

## What survives Solomon, and what this case changes

The baseline propositions are from the [process](../book/process-claims.json) and [context/Freeman](../book/context-claims.json) audits. The findings below apply to this episode; they do not establish frequency across China or change through 2026.

| Proposition | Episode verdict | Evidence and boundary |
|---|---|---|
| S-P12: partial agreement with reserved positions and wording that can preserve compromise | **Retained within scope** | Crew departure coexists with continued responsibility and reconnaissance disputes. Both governments' published texts support the separation; the causal role of avoiding a conspicuous reversal remains untested. |
| S-P04: agenda wording and sequencing matter | **Retained within scope** | A meeting about reconnaissance differs from agreement to end it; aircraft return becomes a future plan. Who first proposed that sequence remains unknown. |
| S-P17 / S-C17: public presentation can shape room for compromise | **Narrowed to specified conditions** | Differing official presentations accompanied compatible immediate actions. Their necessity or causal effect is unproved; the US legal packet shows deliberate audience management too. |
| S-P25: implementation bargaining can combine demands for counterpart adherence with efforts to adjust China's own obligations | **Unresolved** | Later aircraft and payment talks are real, but expressly deferred issues cannot count as reopening already accepted terms. This bounded case does not establish the proposition's claimed asymmetry. |
| S-P08 / S-C05: alternate trusted channels facilitate movement | **Unresolved** | Participant leads identify channels, but their effect is not independently reconstructed. No decisive intermediary is assigned. |
| S-C08 / F05: preparation and better foreign-policy information confer advantage | **Narrowed to specified conditions** | Information collection, internal circulation and authority to challenge it are separate capacities. The acquired record cannot establish that all were present on the Chinese side. |
| S-C11 / F09: institutional divisions constrain bargaining | **Unresolved** | Later scholarship identifies a specific information mechanism; its causal weight depends on contemporary Chinese deliberations still unacquired. US coordination is directly documented for particular legal guidance. |
| S-P23 / S-C16: control of time exploits urgency | **Unresolved** | Detention created urgency, but the later aircraft timetable includes documented engineering constraints. Neither duration nor a retrospective description of late meetings identifies deliberate exhaustion. |

The episode therefore changes the explanatory emphasis more securely than it establishes a new Chinese style. It directs attention from national character to the construction of a limited package, the relationship between spokesperson language and authorized action, and the institutional handling of competing factual claims. It also shows why a qualification already in Solomon matters: confrontation is a different setting from the deliberate construction of a cooperative relationship. Applying the normalization model without that qualification would mistake an extension of scope for a demonstrated continuity or break. [S-C01, book pp.16–24]

Two additional propositions deserve explicit tests. **EP3-N01:** in this episode, separating immediate humanitarian action from unresolved attribution and policy questions may have made an otherwise unacceptable package feasible. Supporting evidence would be a draft or instruction showing that this separation changed a rejection into authorization; evidence that release was already authorized independently, or depended on a different concession, would weaken that mechanism. **EP3-N02:** the informational value of a relationship or diplomatic channel depends on whether its reporting can reach and be evaluated by the officials who decide. Supporting evidence would trace a message through that process; an unused channel, or a decision predating its intervention, would rebut a claimed effect. Neither proposition is a national-cultural law.

The remaining gaps are specific. Keefe's near-contemporary account remains unacquired. State case F-2010-07070 now provides seventeen read cables, including the April 2, April 4 and April 11 encounters, six aircraft records and two further reports of senior contacts. The other catalogue entries, including April 18, remain distinguished from read documents. The complete intermediate settlement drafts, transfer-memorandum versions, authority letter and Chinese internal approvals remain missing. The separate Bush Library 2017-0023-F finding aid still has no underlying asset read here, and its scope excludes Principals/Deputies Committee meetings and dates after April 30. Neither the State cables nor the April 6 DoD packet closes that NSC gap. [EP315–EP321; EPD01–09]

What the present evidence establishes is already substantial: a crisis could be partially resolved through explicitly limited commitments while both governments retained incompatible public claims. What it leaves open is why the leaderships accepted that package at that moment. That uncertainty belongs inside the argument, alongside the recovered terms, rather than behind a confident story about who understood Chinese negotiating behavior best.
