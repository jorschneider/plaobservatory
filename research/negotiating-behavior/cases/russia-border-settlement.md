# China–Soviet/Russian border settlement: agreement, local accommodation and implementation

Research date: **10 September 2026**. Bounded historical comparison, principally 1987–2008. Companion [source register](../sources/russia-border-register.json) records exact reading, provenance and missing documents; [move ledger](russia-border-moves.json) separates public positions from obligations and material implementation. Source IDs below resolve there.

The Russian border case demonstrates negotiated flexibility and sophisticated implementation arrangements well before the 2026 endpoint. It therefore changes the historical baseline against which a 2026 update should measure novelty. China accepted partial settlements, reserved difficult sections explicitly, and separated sovereignty from selected local uses. These were not capabilities first acquired through recent commercial negotiations. What remains unproved is that a distinct Chinese negotiating psychology, institutional learning, or a single change in relative power caused the settlement.

The case also supplies an unusually concrete observation of senior authorization: Putin's October 2004 order approved specified drafts and delegated limited negotiating discretion. That evidence concerns the **Russian** mandate. It tests the informational value of drafting plus authorization, while warning against treating that mechanism as uniquely Chinese. Chinese published treaty texts are much stronger evidence of accepted obligations than of the internal process that approved concessions.

## Reading and evidentiary boundaries

Iwashita's open monograph is the principal process reconstruction. The completed reading comprises the preface and introduction, printed ix–xiii and 1–7; complete chapters 1, 3 and 6, pp. 9–31, 57–90 and 155–175, including notes; complete conclusion, pp. 177–197; and references, newspaper list and interview inventory, pp. 198–205. Maps on pp. 16, 70 and 158–159 were visually inspected. **Chapters 2, 4 and 5 and the index were not read; the whole monograph is not marked complete.** Its July 2004 preface predates the October agreement. A separate, fully read university essay discusses the eventual settlement; its precise publication date remains unverified. [Iwashita, chapter PDFs](https://src-h.slav.hokudai.ac.jp/coe21/publish/no3_ses/contents.html); [subsequent essay](https://www.hokudai.ac.jp/bureau/populi/edition21/churo.html). [RUS-IW-1/2/4/7/8; RUS-IW-UPDATE]

This is substantial field-based scholarship: Russian participant accounts and local reporting are compared with Chinese gazetteers, interviews and repeated visits. Its limits matter. Iwashita advocates cooperation; some island exchanges are expressly conjectures, and some supposed package offers are labeled rumors. His conclusion did not predict the precise final allocation. He also deliberately merges delimitation and demarcation terminologically in his introduction. Here they remain distinct: agreeing a boundary route, ratifying it, locating and marking it in the field, and changing control or access are different events. No full joint-commission minutes, private Chinese negotiating instructions, original 1:100,000 treaty maps, or 2008 descriptive-protocol annexes were recovered.

## 1. Convergence preceded the 1991 agreement; package separation still needs explanation

Iwashita places substantive progress in the talks reopened in February 1987, following Gorbachev's 1986 Vladivostok initiative. His discussion of the disputed Khabarovsk delta describes renewed acceptance of constructive results from 1964, including the main-channel approach, alongside continuing disagreement over this particular area. The precise reconstruction depends heavily on diplomat Boris Vereshchagin's later memoir, which this investigation has not independently acquired. It establishes a serious lead against dating all flexibility to the aftermath of June 1989; it does not authenticate every 1987 offer. [Iwashita, ch. 1, pp. 10–14; ch. 3, pp. 74–76](https://src-h.slav.hokudai.ac.jp/coe21/publish/no3_ses/4000km-4.pdf). [RUS-IW-2/4]

The public package nevertheless changed. Paragraph 7 of the 18 May 1989 joint communiqué sought simultaneous resolution of the eastern and western sectors. On **16 May 1991**, Qian Qichen and Aleksandr Bessmertnykh signed the eastern agreement separately. The [paired 1989 primary-source audit](../literature/readings/fravel-compromise-and-1989-records.md) checks the communiqué in contemporary Chinese and English publications and distinguishes Deng's published historical argument from a binding territorial demand. The change from a simultaneous public objective to staged agreements is observable. Whether post-1989 domestic vulnerability, the improved relationship, technical readiness, Soviet circumstances or some combination explains it requires the intervening draft and instruction record. Fravel's domestic-insecurity explanation is a hypothesis to test, not a motive established by chronology; his treatment explicitly excludes the 2004 compromise from that explanation. [RUS-1989-AUDIT]

The 1991 agreement illustrates what a partial settlement actually committed the parties to. Article III reserved the sections between points 7–8 and 10–11, associated with Abagaitui/Bolshoi on the Argun and the islands near Khabarovsk. Article IV assigned an equal joint commission the practical work of identifying channels, allocating river islands, erecting markers and producing documents and maps. Article V supplied channel criteria, and Article VII protected the demarcated boundary against subsequent natural changes, with consultation for new islands straddling the line. These provisions combine rules with delegated factual determination; they are neither a merely political assurance nor a complete field survey. [1991 Chinese text, arts. III–VII](https://treaty.mfa.gov.cn/tykfiles/20180718/1531876659147.pdf). [RUS-T1991-CN]

Navigation was a separable interest. Article VIII allowed ships of different types, including military vessels, to navigate from the Ussuri past Khabarovsk into the Amur, with practical rules assigned to relevant agencies. Article IX addressed Chinese-flag navigation down the Tumen, subject to arrangements with relevant parties. Such provisions could make a boundary arrangement tolerable without allocating every disputed island immediately. They do not establish that the underlying sovereignty issue had already been resolved. The Russian Federation and China then signed the short western-sector agreement on **3 September 1994**, again providing an equal demarcation commission and separately reserving a three-country junction arrangement with Kazakhstan. [1991 text, arts. VIII–IX](https://treaty.mfa.gov.cn/tykfiles/20180718/1531876659147.pdf); [1994 signed Russian text, arts. III–V](https://treaty.mfa.gov.cn/tykfiles/20180718/1531876718907.pdf). [RUS-T1994-RU]

## 2. Implementation had domestic distributive consequences

The post-1991 problem was not simply whether Beijing or Moscow would honor a sentence. Drawing a line affected farms, river traffic, border installations and regional politicians. Iwashita's first chapter reconstructs resistance by Primorye governor Yevgenii Nazdratenko, conflict around Russian demarcation representative Georgii Rozov, and federal efforts to keep implementation moving. The politics were not uniformly anti-Chinese: regional leaders differed, and federal leaders worked through selective local alliances. The 1997 completion announcement concerned the sectors covered by the 1991 agreement, leaving the reserved islands unsettled. It should not be coded as completion of the entire boundary. [Iwashita, ch. 1, pp. 18–31](https://src-h.slav.hokudai.ac.jp/coe21/publish/no3_ses/4000km-2.pdf). [RUS-IW-2]

An important measurement correction comes from Iwashita's reconstruction of Damanskii/Zhenbao. A legal allocation in 1991 did not necessarily mean an island was physically transferred from Russian control then: his comparison of Chinese local records and Russian participant accounts places Chinese control there much earlier. Exact accounts of the 1969–1970 sequence differ. The secure methodological point is to record the pre-agreement control baseline separately from each state's claim. Nor can changing island counts supply a stable concession denominator: channels, island definitions and counting practices vary. [Iwashita, ch. 3, pp. 57–68](https://src-h.slav.hokudai.ac.jp/coe21/publish/no3_ses/4000km-4.pdf). [RUS-IW-4]

The Menkeseli arrangements make the implementation mechanism more concrete. Iwashita attributes an accommodation proposal to Russian border and regional authorities: Chinese sovereignty could coexist with continued traditional Russian local use. His chapter traces this through reporting and consultations to the 1997 guiding agreement and the detailed instrument signed on **9 December 1999**. That origin story remains an attributed reconstruction; the recovered legal text independently establishes what the governments ultimately authorized. [Iwashita, ch. 6, pp. 164–168](https://src-h.slav.hokudai.ac.jp/coe21/publish/no3_ses/4000km-7.pdf). [RUS-IW-7]

The complete **published twelve-article body** of the 1999 agreement was read. Its two annexes were not published in the recovered reproduction. Joint use meant the sovereign state's permission for established residents of the other country to conduct specified traditional activities. Named Chinese areas were available to Russian users, and a named Russian area to Chinese users; host-state development rights remained. Eligibility was limited, user numbers were not to increase, and activities were confined to haymaking, fishing and farming. The agreement prescribed a **five-year use period**, with commencement and termination dates to be settled diplomatically. It required border documents and host permissions, compliance with host law, inspections, and consultations at least twice annually. Missing annexes prevent verification of exact mapped areas, numerical caps and detailed permit procedures. [1999 agreement, arts. I–XII](https://faolex.fao.org/docs/pdf/bi-63147.pdf). [RUS-USE1999]

This was regulated foreign local access under sovereignty, not condominium or an unrestricted common resource. It supplies a mechanism by which a jurisdictional settlement could preserve some benefits for affected users. It does not prove that every consultation occurred, that all residents received permits, or that the original arrangement continued unchanged after its term. The implementation record needed to answer those questions remains missing.

## 3. Relationship assurances did not eliminate the reserved dispute

Article 6 of the **16 July 2001 Treaty of Good-Neighborliness and Friendly Cooperation** is particularly instructive. It states that the parties have no mutual territorial claims while also expressly directing continued negotiation of the unagreed boundary alignments and maintenance of the status quo pending settlement. Reading only its first clause would manufacture a contradiction when negotiations continued. The actual negotiated language accommodates a broader political assurance and a precise unresolved question simultaneously. Articles 7 and 10 place the relationship within existing military confidence measures and regular contacts; Article 9 provides consultation in threatening circumstances, not an automatic mutual-defense commitment. [Complete 2001 treaty](https://www.fmprc.gov.cn/mfa_eng/zy/gb/202405/t20240531_11367098.html). [RUS-T2001]

The October 2004 joint statement likewise embeds the final boundary agreement within a strategic partnership, sovereignty assurances, economic cooperation and regional security. It describes a balanced settlement and anticipates benefits for local navigation and environmental cooperation. These are jointly accepted public purposes and a sales presentation for the agreement. Their coexistence with energy projects and WTO-related announcements does **not** demonstrate a particular territorial-for-commercial exchange. No private package record recovered here identifies such a quid pro quo. [Joint statement, 14 October 2004, especially section I](https://www.fmprc.gov.cn/eng/zy/gb/202405/t20240531_11367146.html). [RUS-JOINT2004]

## 4. The 2004 agreement had a concrete authorization and a contested domestic reception

The strongest mandate evidence recovered is Russian presidential order **479-rp of 13 October 2004**, reproduced by a private legal publisher. It accepts the foreign ministry proposal coordinated with relevant federal executives and the executives of Khabarovsk, Chita and the Jewish Autonomous Region. It authorizes the ministry to negotiate and sign the attached drafts, allowing changes that do not alter their principles. The attachment contains specific boundary coordinates, a navigation-protocol draft and a memorandum draft. This is much closer to Solomon's combined signal of concrete drafting plus senior intervention than a ceremonial leader meeting. It is a reproduced primary document, not a recovered Kremlin page or evidence of the Chinese authorization chain. [Order and three drafts](https://rulaws.ru/president/Rasporyazhenie-Prezidenta-RF-ot-13.10.2004-N-479-rp/). [RUS-AUTH2004]

Foreign ministers **Li Zhaoxing and Sergei Lavrov**, rather than the two presidents personally, signed the supplementary boundary agreement on **14 October 2004**. Its first article specifies the remaining alignments, including land segments through Abagaitui and Bolshoi Ussuriisky. The equal joint commission again receives field-demarcation responsibilities; the agreement maintains rules for river channels and subsequent natural changes. The published NPC Gazette prints six explanatory schematics contrasting prior claim lines and the settlement. Their labels of approximately **335 km² and 62 km² describe the disputed regions**, not the amounts awarded to China. These schematics should not be confused with the original 1:100,000 maps incorporated by the treaty. [Official published agreement](https://www.mfa.gov.cn/web/wjb_673085/zzjg_673183/bjhysws_674671/bhgjty/ldbjty/202303/P020230320538049906247.pdf); NPC Gazette 2005/5, pp. 379–383, scan provenance in register. [RUS-T2004; RUS-NPC2005]

The settlement reduced China's claim while requiring Russia to relinquish control in selected areas. Calling it simply a Chinese victory or Russian concession collapses two baselines. Calling it a universal fifty-fifty formula also goes beyond the evidence: a roughly divided remaining dispute says nothing automatic about the shares appropriate to another boundary or the value of particular locations. The actual lines and retained interests matter more than an undifferentiated acreage total.

Original Russian reporting documents a divided reception. In October, a Khabarovsk legislative committee sought to oppose the agreement while governor Viktor Ishaev defended protection of important local interests, including the airport approach and agricultural uses. A November report described local protests and uncertainty among officials about the still-unpublished detail. These accounts identify constituencies and competing public claims. Their statements about historical ownership are not independent legal adjudications, and reported petition or protest numbers are attributed estimates. [Kommersant, 22 October 2004](https://www.kommersant.ru/doc/518713); [4 November 2004](https://www.kommersant.ru/doc/521955). [RUS-KOM-LOCAL; RUS-KOM-PUB]

The May 2005 ratification debate reveals further disagreement within the Russian state. Kommersant reported a 307–80 Duma vote and competing arguments from diplomats, deputies, the border service and the General Staff. A military representative disputed the view that China then posed an immediate local military threat; border-service concerns emphasized migration. Another argument alleged Chinese manipulation of river channels. These are reported official claims, not independently established force deployments or hydrological findings. The full official stenogram was not recovered. They are useful because “Russia's interest” was being argued over, rather than transparently expressed by one actor. [Kommersant, 21 May 2005](https://www.kommersant.ru/doc/579607). [RUS-KOM-RAT]

## 5. Ratification, demarcation and handover were separate steps

The NPC Standing Committee approved the agreement on **27 April 2005**; the Xinhua release dated 27 May can otherwise produce a one-month error. Russia's formal record gives Duma passage on **20 May**, Federation Council approval on **25 May**, and Putin's ratification law **52-FZ on 31 May 2005**. The Chinese published treaty records entry into force on **2 June 2005**. None of these dates is the final 2008 handover. [NPC decision reproduced by CCTV/Xinhua](https://news.cctv.cn/news/china/20050527/102420.shtml); [Russian ratification law scan](https://duma.consultant.ru/files/825228); [treaty publication](https://www.mfa.gov.cn/web/wjb_673085/zzjg_673183/bjhysws_674671/bhgjty/ldbjty/202303/P020230320538049906247.pdf). [RUS-NPC-NEWS; RUS-RAT2005; RUS-T2004]

A separate memorandum signed on 14 October 2004 extended existing bilateral cooperation agreements to the newly delimited areas when the supplementary agreement entered into force. The acquired final Chinese text confirms this, rather than merely repeating the Russian draft. The navigation draft, by contrast, envisaged another agreement after demarcation, taking transport, water, environmental and local economic interests into account. The recovered draft alone cannot establish the final later navigation regime. [Signed memorandum](https://treaty.mfa.gov.cn/tykfiles/20180718/1531876873916.pdf); [Russian authorization attachment](https://rulaws.ru/president/Rasporyazhenie-Prezidenta-RF-ot-13.10.2004-N-479-rp/). [RUS-MEMO2004; RUS-AUTH2004]

On **21 July 2008**, Yang Jiechi and Lavrov signed the supplementary descriptive protocol and annexes. The Chinese official report described completion of field-demarcation work but still required domestic approval procedures and an exchange of notes. On **14 October 2008**, the Chinese transcript records that the descriptive protocol and annexes entered into force through that exchange, with a ceremony involving both boundary delegations and relevant central and local agencies. The English MFA translation calls this a “Supplementary Agreement,” which risks confusing it with the already-effective 2004 instrument. The Chinese title resolves the ambiguity. Original reporting corroborates the handover of Tarabarov/Yinlong and the western portion of Bolshoi Ussuriisky. It does not independently audit every boundary marker. [July official report](https://www.fmprc.gov.cn/gjhdq_676201/gj_676203/oz_678770/1206_679110/xgxw_679116/200807/t20080722_9336208.shtml); [October Chinese transcript](https://www.taiwan.cn/xwzx/jrbd/200810/t20081015_761718.htm); [RFA report](https://www.rfa.org/mandarin/yataibaodao/lingtu-10142008152009.html). [RUS-PROTOCOL2008; RUS-QIN-CN; RUS-RFA2008]

## 6. What travels to the Solomon update

| Proposition | Judgment supported by this case | Boundary of inference |
|---|---|---|
| **S-C01 / S-C18:** relationship and substantive interests condition tactics | Relationship normalization accompanied staged compromise and detailed protections for specific interests. | Public friendship language does not identify the decisive private motive or prove emotional attachment caused concessions. |
| **S-P05 / S-P12:** principles, differentiated documents and partial settlement | Rules, explicit reservations and separate sovereignty/use arrangements made partial bargains possible. | Face-saving or deliberate ambiguity is an additional mechanism requiring evidence; express reservation is not hidden noncompliance. |
| **S-P24:** concrete drafting together with senior intervention as a possible signal that agreement is authorized and near | The October 2004 Russian order supplies a specified draft, authorization and bounded discretion immediately before signature. | This is Russian evidence; it neither reveals Beijing's mandate nor establishes Chinese national distinctiveness or general predictive accuracy. Its date does not establish when outsiders could obtain it, so prospective usefulness is unproved. |
| **S-P25:** implementation as a continuing bargaining arena, including the stronger claim of asymmetric adherence demands and adjustment seeking | Demarcation, local access, consultations and later instruments demonstrate the continuing arena. | Consensual accommodation does not establish asymmetric Chinese compliance demands or opportunistic reopening. |
| **S-C09–S-C12; F09:** instructions, superiors and bureaucratic coordination | Multiple agencies and local interests affected implementation; detailed technical instruments predate Xi. | Russian disagreement is unusually visible. Missing Chinese internal records prevent a symmetrical comparison of actual fragmentation or discipline. |
| **S-C19; F07/F13:** conflict, learning and substyles | These remain rival explanations to investigate, not findings generated by delay or eventual success. | No recovered training, decision or institutional-memory record establishes that China learned a new style from this sequence. |

Rising Chinese resources might have changed Russian expectations about the future costs of leaving the dispute open. Yet progress began under Gorbachev before China's later economic predominance, and the final bargain followed changes in Russian federal authority as well as the bilateral relationship. The sources do not isolate relative power from those alternatives. The military context also differed from the India crisis: a normalized relationship and negotiated boundary settlement are not interchangeable with disengagement and patrol arrangements along an unsettled line. Even within “security negotiations,” the object and surrounding conflict must remain specified.

The strongest revision is methodological and historical: **classify the obligation and its stage before attributing a style**. An accepted legal line can coexist with pending physical demarcation, regulated foreign local use and further navigation negotiations. Continuing discussion may implement the bargain rather than undermine it. This both preserves Solomon's attention to implementation and limits an inference of Chinese unreliability drawn solely from the fact that talks continued.

The most valuable next evidence is the 1989–1991 offer and authorization sequence; Chinese instructions for the 1997 and 2004 compromises; the full 1997 guiding agreement and missing 1999 annexes; joint-commission minutes and 1999/2008 boundary maps; consultation and permit records; the official May 2005 Duma stenogram; and Lavrov's 14 November 2004 NTV interview. Vereshchagin, Galenovich and dissenting author Boris Tkachenko are identified but unread firsthand leads. Iwashita's unverified claim of a five-year legal expiry affecting 1997 is excluded from the causal account: no such sunset was found in the treaty provisions actually read.
