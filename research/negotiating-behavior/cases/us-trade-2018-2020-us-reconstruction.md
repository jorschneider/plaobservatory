# U.S. bargaining record, 2018–2020: commitments, drafts and a narrower agreement

Research cut-off: 8 September 2026. This reconstruction distinguishes joint text, unilateral official statements, observed public exchanges and original reporting about inaccessible drafts. It covers the bargaining sequence, not implementation. Source copies and hashes are recorded in the [U.S. source register](../sources/us-trade-2018-2020-us-register.json); originals remain outside git. The evidence is strongest about public choices and weakest about the precise instructions behind China's May 2019 revisions.

## May 2018: an understanding with unfinished terms

The May 19 joint statement identifies Liu He as President Xi's special envoy and lists Mnuchin, Ross and Lighthizer for the United States. It commits to additional purchases, further technical work, intellectual-property cooperation and advancing relevant Chinese legal amendments, including the Patent Law. It specifies neither a purchase total nor tariff suspension. Its closing commitment is to continued high-level engagement. It should therefore be coded as a joint preliminary understanding, not a completed settlement of the trade conflict. [White House joint statement, May 19](https://trumpwhitehouse.archives.gov/briefings-statements/joint-statement-united-states-china-regarding-trade-consultations/).

The next day's public explanations were more expansive and also qualified. Reuters reported Mnuchin announcing that tariffs were on hold while the framework was worked out. In the same story, Kudlow denied that a trade deal had been reached and Lighthizer stressed structural reform rather than purchases alone. Reuters gave named interview venues and quoted these competing emphases; it did not publish a separate signed tariff-pause instrument. This is evidence of publicly communicated U.S. restraint, not evidence that the joint statement contained a clause it lacks. [Dunsmuir and Schneider, Reuters, May 20, syndicated version updated May 21](https://www.business-standard.com/article/reuters/u-s-china-putting-trade-war-on-hold-treasury-s-mnuchin-says-118052000509_1.html).

On May 29 the White House announced that it would impose 25 percent tariffs on $50 billion of Chinese goods, with the final list due June 15, while continuing negotiations. Its June 15 statement confirmed the decision and threatened additional measures against retaliation. These are observable changes in U.S. public policy after Mnuchin's pause announcement. The sources do not establish that Chinese negotiators had received an unconditional, permanently binding promise of no tariffs; equally, an account of only Chinese reversals would omit this consequential American sequence. [May 29 statement](https://trumpwhitehouse.archives.gov/briefings-statements/statement-steps-protect-domestic-technology-intellectual-property-chinas-discriminatory-burdensome-trade-practices/); [June 15 statement](https://trumpwhitehouse.archives.gov/briefings-statements/statement-president-regarding-trade-china/).

## December 2018–April 2019: authorization to negotiate is not approval of a final draft

The White House's December 1 Buenos Aires readout described a leaders' understanding: Trump would defer a scheduled increase on $200 billion of imports while structural negotiations proceeded over approximately 90 days. Chinese purchases were described as substantial but not yet quantified by agreement. This was a U.S. account of a bilateral meeting, not a jointly signed schedule of final concessions. [December 1 readout](https://trumpwhitehouse.archives.gov/briefings-statements/statement-press-secretary-regarding-presidents-working-dinner-china/).

Lighthizer's February 27, 2019 opening testimony publicly treated enforceability and other issues as outstanding and repeatedly qualified the prospect of an agreement. This matters because it predates the breakdown: the U.S. chief negotiator had not represented all major terms as settled. The April 23 White House announcement then authorized Lighthizer and Mnuchin to return to Beijing, identified Liu as China's lead, and listed purchases and enforcement among the subjects still to be discussed. These documents establish leadership of negotiations and their announced agenda; neither discloses Xi's approval of particular draft paragraphs or Liu's authority to finalize them. [February 27 testimony](https://ustr.gov/about-us/policy-offices/press-office/press-releases/2019/february/opening-statement-ustr-robert); [April 23 announcement](https://trumpwhitehouse.archives.gov/briefings-statements/statement-press-secretary-regarding-administrations-trade-talks-china-2/).

The May tariff notice also supplies a useful check on deadlines. It records postponement of the increase to March 2, followed by postponement until further notice. A deadline was therefore a policy instrument that the President could extend, not a mechanically binding external clock. [84 Federal Register 20459–20460, May 9](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2019-05-09/pdf/2019-09681.pdf).

## May 2019: a documented accusation, an unavailable redline

Reuters's May 8 exclusive attributed extensive revisions to a nearly 150-page, seven-chapter draft to three U.S. government sources and three private-sector sources briefed on the talks. Its central reported dispute concerned commitments to change Chinese laws versus implementation through administrative or regulatory measures. It also included an anonymous Chinese official disputing the characterization of backtracking. **The article does not state that Reuters obtained the draft, and this project has not obtained it.** [Lawder, Mason and Martina, Reuters, May 8; complete syndicated copy](https://www.yahoo.com/news/exclusive-china-backtracked-nearly-aspects-084554623.html).

The same reporting described industrial subsidies and biotechnology approvals as continuing sticking points. Consequently, the story cannot support the stronger claim that every unresolved U.S. demand had previously been accepted and was then withdrawn. Nor does it identify a personal Xi instruction, an authenticated sequence of versions, agreed bracket conventions, or an approval record binding either leader to every paragraph. The reported May 3 transmission is a source-attributed event, not an archival cable inspected here.

The accusation itself is independently observable in an official instrument. Section B of USTR's May 9 notice asserts that China retreated from earlier commitments and records the President's direction to increase the additional tariff from 10 to 25 percent. The annex sets conditions for covered imports, including entry and export on or after May 10. The notice proves that the administration adopted that explanation and acted on it; it does not annex the disputed commitments or independently adjudicate the Chinese account. [Tariff notice, 20459–20460](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2019-05-09/pdf/2019-09681.pdf).

The source distinction is essential: Reuters supplied reported details beyond the public U.S. accusation, but six described sources do not necessarily mean six independent readings of a document. Several were explicitly briefed. A later article repeating this exclusive is another transmission of the same account unless it supplies independent evidence. The responsible conclusion is a serious dispute over draft obligations and their implementation form, followed by actual escalation; intention and final authorization remain unresolved.

## September–October 2019: making a partial settlement acceptable

A September 12 Wall Street Journal report described Chinese efforts to separate trade from national-security disputes, citing people familiar with the plan. The complete WITA republication was read; the original publisher page exposes only an opening portion and a later byline including Josh Zumbrun. It is useful contemporaneous reporting about a proposed narrowing, not an authenticated Chinese mandate. [Wei and Deng, WSJ report as republished by WITA](https://www.wita.org/trade-news/china-seeks-to-narrow-trade-talks-with-u-s-in-bid-to-break-deadlock/).

The October 11 Oval Office transcript provides firmer evidence of U.S. acceptance of phases. Trump announced an understanding subject to written completion; Lighthizer said the consequences of unresolved enforcement disputes were still being finalized. Mnuchin said Liu needed further work with his team. Asked about the earlier breakdown, Trump acknowledged that the two sides might have understood the status differently: “We thought we had a deal, and perhaps they didn’t.” [October 11 transcript](https://trumpwhitehouse.archives.gov/briefings-statements/remarks-president-trump-vice-premier-liu-peoples-republic-china-meeting/).

In that exchange, Mnuchin attributed suspension of the planned 25-to-30-percent increase to Liu's request and Trump's approval. Lighthizer distinguished that decision from the December tariffs, which remained undecided, and said Huawei was outside this agreement. Trump expressly defended dividing the negotiations into phases. These statements establish a narrower bargaining structure and differentiated tariff decisions. They do not establish that a fully settled comprehensive accord was merely divided without substantive loss.

The remaining work was not safely reducible to translation or clerical drafting. A process that still had unresolved enforcement consequences and required delegation-level follow-up lacked terms important to whether obligations were acceptable. That inference follows from the principals' own public qualifications, without assuming either culture-driven deception or an entirely innocent misunderstanding. The transcript also supplies evidence of friendly public ceremony alongside explicit disagreement and conditionality; tone alone cannot measure the distance between positions.

## December 2019–January 2020: finality and continued leverage

USTR's December 13 announcement described agreement on intellectual property, technology transfer, agriculture, financial services, currency, purchases and dispute settlement. It also announced retention of 25 percent tariffs on approximately $250 billion of imports and 7.5 percent on approximately $120 billion. Thus the settlement did not entail complete U.S. tariff removal. Those figures should be taken from the tariff authorities and published agreement, rather than reconstructed from rounded or inconsistent ceremonial remarks. [USTR, December 13](https://ustr.gov/about-us/policy-offices/press-office/press-releases/2019/december/united-states-and-china-reach).

A December 15 USTR/Treasury statement denied press reports that American negotiators had offered to halve tariffs across approximately $360 billion of imports. This does not settle whose private account was correct; it makes the dispute part of the evidence. A purported concession reported anonymously should not silently become an observed U.S. offer. [USTR/Treasury denial](https://ustr.gov/about-us/policy-offices/press-office/press-releases/2019/december/statement-ustr-and-ust).

The January 15 signing transcript records the signing and Liu's delivery of Xi's supportive message. Trump publicly described retained tariffs as leverage for subsequent negotiations. Lighthizer praised Liu personally while emphasizing the large interagency team behind the agreement. These are attributable statements by participants, not access to their private authorization records. This pass read the substantive trade discussion, officials' remarks, Xi message, Liu/Cui statements and signing record; it does not claim a complete reading of every ceremonial acknowledgment. [January 15 transcript](https://trumpwhitehouse.archives.gov/briefings-statements/remarks-president-trump-signing-u-s-china-phase-one-trade-agreement-2/).

Comparing the announced objectives with the final settlement is possible. Producing a precise list of May draft deletions is not possible from this corpus. In particular, a subject's absence from the final deal does not prove that its removal represented withdrawal of an already approved May commitment. The agreement-design and implementation workstreams must separately test what the signed obligations require and what followed.

## Implications for the Solomon update

This sequence supports testing Solomon's S-P12 on partial agreements and remaining reservations, S-P23 on reciprocal deadlines, and S-P24 on senior authorization. It also requires S-C18's attention to material interests and S-C01/F13's differentiation among officials. Public U.S. uncertainty, policy reversals and conflicting emphases are necessary comparison evidence, not background noise.

The main revision is evidentiary discipline. Code who proposed, tentatively accepted, authorized, signed and implemented each obligation. Keep those states separate. The observable move toward a narrower settlement shows adaptation by both negotiating teams; the current record cannot determine whether China's May revisions arose principally from domestic resistance, revised leadership instructions, calculated pressure or incompatible assumptions about provisional text. More confident claims require the missing drafts and authorization trail, not more repetitions of the original accusation.
