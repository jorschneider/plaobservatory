# DS379: Chinese legal agency, negotiated procedure, and the limits of a WTO victory

Research date: 15 September 2026. Substantively examined episode: September 2008–October 2012, with selected scholarship published in 2013. This is a bounded case study, not a survey of subsequent WTO jurisprudence or current US trade-remedy law. Source identities, exact reading scopes, hashes, and unsuccessful retrievals are in the [source register](../sources/wto-ds379-register.json).

China's challenge to US anti-dumping and countervailing duties supplies direct evidence of technical and legal agency after WTO accession. It also demonstrates why legal success, bargaining behavior, and commercial relief must be kept separate. China developed detailed arguments, contested administrative methodology, appealed unfavorable findings, and secured important interpretations against the United States. The tribunal nevertheless rejected or could not resolve other claims. The United States implemented new determinations rather than simply abandoning the duties, and China disputed its claim of full compliance.

The clearest negotiated artifact is not the appellate judgment. It is a three-page agreement signed by Chinese ambassador Yi Xiaozhun and US ambassador Michael Punke on 11 May 2012, specifying how a possible compliance dispute would proceed. That document directly establishes Chinese participation in precise legal bargaining. It does not reveal the initial proposals, exchanged concessions, or internal instructions that produced the agreement. [DS379-07, pp. 1–3](https://docs.wto.org/dol2fe/Pages/SS/directdoc.aspx?filename=Q:/WT/DS/379-14.pdf&Open=True)

## The dispute and the observable sequence

The United States began applying countervailing-duty law to imports from China in 2007 while continuing to use its non-market-economy methodology for anti-dumping calculations. DS379 concerned four groups of products: circular welded carbon-quality steel pipe; light-walled rectangular pipe and tube; laminated woven sacks; and certain pneumatic off-the-road tyres. One central question was whether imposing both duties could offset the same subsidy twice. Another concerned when a state-owned supplier or bank counted as a public body whose financial contribution could qualify as a subsidy. These were disputes about specified measures under existing agreements, not negotiations over China's admission to the institution. [DS379-01, case summary](https://www.wto.org/english/tratop_e/dispu_e/cases_e/ds379_e.htm)

## Public bodies: an evidentiary victory with a consequential limit

China's April 2009 executive summary argued that government ownership did not, by itself, transform a legally separate enterprise into a public body. It invoked the Vienna Convention's rules of interpretation and the International Law Commission's attribution framework, distinguishing an entity's governmental authority from ownership of its shares. It also addressed the alternative route by which government entrustment or direction of a private body could bring a transaction within the subsidy rules. This is evidence of structured legal argument, rather than a generic assertion of sovereignty or a refusal to discuss domestic institutions. It is an executive summary of China's submission, however, not the complete brief or proof that the government's own staff originated every argument. [DS379-03, ¶¶2–8, A-2–A-3](https://www.wto.org/english/tratop_e/dispu_e/379r_a_e.pdf)

The Appellate Body's interpretation required a public body to possess, exercise, or be vested with governmental authority. It did not require an express statutory delegation in every case. Sustained governmental functions and evidence of meaningful government control over an entity's conduct could be relevant; majority ownership alone did not establish the required authority. The analysis also left private-body entrustment or direction available. Nor should the report be described as finally declaring all of ILC Article 5 customary international law: its reasoning treated the relevant attribution principles with qualifications and independently grounded its interpretation in the WTO text and context. [DS379-04, ¶¶285–319, particularly 310–318](https://www.wto.org/english/tratop_e/dispu_e/379abr_e.pdf)

Applied to the record, the distinction mattered. Commerce had relied on government ownership to classify the input suppliers as public bodies. The Appellate Body found the determinations inconsistent with the subsidy agreement. Its discussion also records that China had already urged Commerce to apply a five-factor inquiry, including board participation, control of activities, policy functions, and statutory creation, rather than deciding on ownership alone. Chinese agency thus preceded the WTO appeal and operated inside the US administrative process. [DS379-04, ¶¶343–347](https://www.wto.org/english/tratop_e/dispu_e/379abr_e.pdf)

The state-owned commercial banks produced a different result. Commerce's record extended beyond ownership to banking-law requirements, industrial-policy links, and evidence about lending and governance. The Appellate Body concluded that China had not demonstrated inconsistency in the bank determination. It also warned that reliance on an earlier investigation would not ordinarily be enough; the particular overlap in the periods investigated and the challenge actually presented mattered. The decision therefore neither established that Chinese SOEs were generally private nor held that state ownership could never help prove governmental authority. [DS379-04, ¶¶348–359](https://www.wto.org/english/tratop_e/dispu_e/379abr_e.pdf)

This interpretation was contested immediately. At adoption, the United States argued that the tribunal had displaced a workable control standard, collapsed the distinction between government and public body, and made it harder to discipline subsidies delivered through SOEs. These are the losing party's contemporary objections, not findings that override the adopted report. They nevertheless explain why the outcome cannot be narrated as both parties converging on a shared understanding of the rules. [DS379-05, PDF pp. 1–3](https://ustr.gov/sites/default/files/uploads/Mar25%20Stmt%20US%20AD-CVD%20fin.pdf)

## Double remedies: China changed the legal interpretation without winning its entire theory

The economic issue was specific. When an anti-dumping calculation substitutes surrogate-country values for Chinese domestic prices or costs, its normal-value side may omit the effects of Chinese subsidies while the actual export price reflects them. The resulting anti-dumping duty can already offset some subsidization. Adding a countervailing duty against that same subsidization can then duplicate the remedy. Concurrent duties and a double remedy are consequently different propositions. [DS379-04, ¶¶538–544](https://www.wto.org/english/tratop_e/dispu_e/379abr_e.pdf)

China's initial executive summary put the argument categorically: concurrent use of the US non-market-economy methodology and countervailing duties necessarily produced a double remedy. It challenged both the particular determinations and the alleged absence of US legal authority to prevent duplication. The panel excluded the latter, general challenge because the alleged omission had not been identified in the consultation request and its addition materially expanded the dispute. China disputed that jurisdictional objection with precedent-based arguments, but lost. This supplies a concrete institutional constraint on the case, not sufficient evidence of either Chinese procedural incompetence or a deliberate plan to evade consultation. [DS379-03, ¶¶25–35](https://www.wto.org/english/tratop_e/dispu_e/379r_a_e.pdf); [DS379-02, ¶¶14.11–14.42](https://www.wto.org/english/tratop_e/dispu_e/379r_e.pdf)

On the particular investigations, the Appellate Body overturned the panel's interpretation. It read the requirement in SCM Article 19.3 to levy countervailing duties in appropriate amounts together with related subsidy and anti-dumping provisions. The absence of an express domestic-subsidy equivalent to GATT Article VI:5's export-subsidy rule did not establish permission to offset the same subsidy twice. It also rejected the proposition that omission of the Tokyo Round's broader prohibition on concurrent duties disposed of the narrower double-remedy question. [DS379-04, ¶¶551–583](https://www.wto.org/english/tratop_e/dispu_e/379abr_e.pdf)

Yet the tribunal expressly declined China's claim that reversal of the panel's interpretation automatically established a violation. It found duplication likely under the methodology but not inevitable in every instance. The relevant facts included the subsidy's effect on export prices and any corrective adjustment. The decisive violation was Commerce's failure to investigate whether and to what extent the same subsidies were offset twice. The tribunal put an affirmative investigative obligation on the administering authority; it did not itself calculate a correct rate or require abandonment of every concurrent duty. [DS379-04, ¶¶591–606](https://www.wto.org/english/tratop_e/dispu_e/379abr_e.pdf)

The US adoption statement disputed both this interpretation and the tribunal's characterization of Commerce's consideration of the issue, stressing evidentiary and measurement difficulties. That disagreement should remain attributed. The adjudicated result was an obligation to examine duplication, while the losing party contested its legal foundation and practical operation. [DS379-05, PDF pp. 3–6](https://ustr.gov/sites/default/files/uploads/Mar25%20Stmt%20US%20AD-CVD%20fin.pdf)

Prusa and Vermulst's law-and-economics study supports the importance of the result while challenging its design. They argue that the specific US methodology justified a stronger finding about duplication, distinguishing this from the more general empirical difficulties of measuring subsidy pass-through under other methods. Their caveats concern subsidized surrogate prices, effects on world prices, and the surrogate profit margin. They also emphasize that the public-body ruling left room for a new determination supported by better evidence. This is analytical scholarship, not a witness account of either delegation's strategy. Its forecasts concerning the post-2016 regime are not treated here as subsequently realized facts. [DS379-10, §§4.2–4.3, pp. 214–228; conclusion, pp. 232–234](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1474745612000560)

A smaller issue provides particularly concrete evidence of technical engagement. The Appellate Body recorded that China had proposed adjusting an external loan benchmark for inflation and offered savings rates as an alternative to selecting comparator countries by gross national income. Commerce accepted the inflation adjustment but rejected other suggestions. These arguments arose in the earlier coated-free-sheet-paper investigation and were incorporated into the off-the-road-tyre record. The Appellate Body criticized the panel's treatment of them but could not complete the benchmark-legality analysis on the available findings. Technical engagement is demonstrable; a Chinese victory on the substantive legality of that benchmark is not. [DS379-04, ¶¶531–537](https://www.wto.org/english/tratop_e/dispu_e/379abr_e.pdf)

## The negotiated implementation procedure

The May 2012 agreement specifies rights and reciprocal procedural undertakings in detail. China could request a compliance panel without new consultations; if it chose consultations, the parties would meet within fifteen days. The US would accept establishment at the first DSB meeting considering China's request. Both would cooperate toward specified panel and appeal timetables. If a subsequent adopted compliance ruling found noncompliance, China could request authorization to suspend concessions, and the US would not invoke the ordinary thirty-day timing objection to bar that request. The US retained its right to arbitration over the level of suspension and relevant procedural principles. The agreement also addressed replacement adjudicators, remaining rights, and objections to the agreed steps. [DS379-07, ¶¶1–12, pp. 2–3](https://docs.wto.org/dol2fe/Pages/SS/directdoc.aspx?filename=Q:/WT/DS/379-14.pdf&Open=True)

These are agreed procedures, not authorization for China to retaliate immediately. Their negotiation demonstrates capacity to specify contingencies, deadlines, and legal safeguards while preserving substantive disagreement. Because no draft history is available, the final terms do not establish which side first proposed each clause or what China conceded to obtain US undertakings. They also do not establish an internal Chinese approval sequence. The published document names the ambassadors who signed; it does not expose their instructions.

## What implementation changed—and what this record cannot establish

The January 2012 US status report says Commerce had issued questionnaires to Chinese respondents and the government, granted requested extensions, and was analyzing or awaiting responses. As a party status report, it establishes the US account of the work underway; it does not independently establish that every request was necessary or every answer adequate. It does make a simple narrative of wholly inactive delay inappropriate without further examination. [DS379-06, entire one-page report](https://docs.wto.org/dol2fe/Pages/SS/directdoc.aspx?filename=Q:/WT/DS/379-12.pdf&Open=True)

The August implementation notice records new determinations addressing loan benchmarks, trading companies, land specificity, adverse facts available, public bodies, and double remedies. Its tables make clear that implementation did not mean withdrawal of every duty. The listed countervailing rate for Weifang East Steel Pipe rose from 29.62% in the investigation to 29.83%; Zibo Aifudi's rose from 29.54% to 83.34%. These are matched comparisons within the notice's CVD table. The full underlying redetermination memoranda were not successfully recovered in this pass, so these increases are not assigned a particular evidentiary or political cause. [DS379-08, pp. 52683–52685](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2012-08-30/pdf/2012-21322.pdf)

The adjacent anti-dumping tables require a different reading. For Zibo Aifudi, 64.28% is labeled the investigation's weighted-average dumping margin and 20.19% the revised cash-deposit rate. Those columns do not by themselves establish that the deposit actually charged immediately before implementation was 64.28%, or that exporters received a corresponding refund. The notice limits the new determinations to relevant unliquidated entries on or after implementation and expressly conditions deposit instructions on rates not already superseded by intervening administrative reviews. Estimating actual commercial relief would require the applicable prior deposit instructions, intervening reviews, entry dates and values, and eventual assessments. [DS379-08, pp. 52684, 52687–52688](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2012-08-30/pdf/2012-21322.pdf)

The October correction added a missing exporter–producer combination while leaving the published recalculated rates unchanged. Meanwhile, the WTO case history records the US claim of full compliance on 31 August and China's disagreement on 28 September. The examined docket lists no subsequent DS379 compliance-panel ruling or retaliation award. USTR's page labels the case concluded, but that administrative label is not an adjudication rejecting China's objection. These materials establish implementation actions and contested compliance; they do not independently resolve the WTO legality of the new determinations. Subsequent cases such as DS437 and DS449 require separate treatment rather than being silently counted as DS379 compliance proceedings. [DS379-09, pp. 65672–65673](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2012-10-30/pdf/2012-26668.pdf); [DS379-01, final procedural entries](https://www.wto.org/english/tratop_e/dispu_e/cases_e/ds379_e.htm); [DS379-11, status and implementation account](https://ustr.gov/issue-areas/enforcement/dispute-settlement-proceedings/wto-dispute-settlement/definitive-anti-dump-1)

## Implications for the Solomon update

For **S-C13 and S-P05**, the case narrows any attempted extension of Solomon's political-negotiation findings to all Chinese external bargaining. Detailed legal reasoning and a precise procedural agreement are directly observable. This does not contradict his findings within their original high-political setting, demonstrate abandonment of broad principles there, or date the arrival of technical competence to 2001. The accession cases already contain sophisticated technical bargaining.

For **Freeman F07**, the case is compatible with experienced, active institutional participation. China used administrative objections, litigation, appeal, and a negotiated implementation procedure. But one later case cannot establish the proposed learning sequence or distinguish learning from resources, specialist counsel, changed economic stakes, or selection of a favorable legal issue. The extent to which expertise was internal to the delegation, supplied by outside counsel, or distributed across firms and ministries remains unresolved.

For **S-P25**, implementation clearly remained a contested arena. That observation alone does not establish Solomon's fuller pattern of China demanding rigid adherence while seeking flexibility for itself. Here both parties agreed an extension and procedural safeguards, and the dispute principally concerned US implementation of an adverse ruling. The evidence supports a revised mechanism: institutional membership supplied legal instruments through which China could challenge a major power's administrative practices and negotiate the terms of further review. It does not identify a uniquely Chinese tactical disposition.

The most defensible addition to the book is therefore an account of changed instruments and venues, with a strict separation between observed competence and inferred learning. China's submissions reveal agency; the panel's jurisdictional exclusion, the appeal's mixed findings, and the unresolved implementation dispute reveal constraints. Public adjudication also makes some conduct much more visible than it was in the private diplomacy Solomon studied. Greater visibility of detailed argument must not itself be mistaken for the historical emergence of that behavior.

## Reading boundary and next discriminating evidence

The Appellate Body report was read selectively: ¶¶285–319, 343–359, and 531–606, with visual checks of printed pp. 123 and 223. The panel report was read at ¶¶1.1–1.7 and 14.11–14.42. China's entire first-submission executive summary, Annex A-1, pp. A-2–A-10, was read; the full Chinese brief and its exhibits were not acquired. The May agreement, January status report, six-page US adoption statement, implementation notice, and correction were read in full within their document boundaries. Federal Register tables and implementation qualifications were visually checked. Prusa and Vermulst were read at pp. 197–199, §§4.2–4.3 (pp. 214–228), and the concluding section on pp. 232–234; other sections were not fully read.

A stronger causal account needs China's case-selection and instruction records, counsel and official interviews tied to specific documents, successive versions of the May procedural agreement, the complete US redetermination files and Chinese responses, and the DSB minutes or Chinese statement explaining its September objection. A financial-outcome claim additionally needs entry-level implementation evidence. These gaps limit conclusions about tactics, attribution of expertise, compliance, and commercial benefit; they do not erase the documented legal arguments or signed procedural bargain.
