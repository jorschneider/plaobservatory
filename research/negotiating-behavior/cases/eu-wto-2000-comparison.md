# Europe’s accession bargain: fixed ceilings, negotiable compensation, 2000–2001

Research date: **15 September 2026**. This is a bounded historical comparison, not an account of subsequent EU–China negotiations or a completed implementation audit. The [source register](../sources/eu-wto-2000-register.json) records originals, preserved copies, hashes, reading scope and dependencies; the [move ledger](eu-wto-2000-moves.json) distinguishes proposals, reported authorization, agreement summaries and final schedules. Claim identifiers refer to the project’s actual Solomon/Freeman extraction, not newly invented paraphrases of the book.

On 19 May 2000, Premier Zhu Rongji confirmed that China would retain a 49 percent foreign ownership ceiling in mobile telecommunications. An agreement nevertheless followed that afternoon. The Chinese official documentary history describes European Trade Commissioner Pascal Lamy accepting the ceiling after consulting member states and industry, while seeking compensation elsewhere. Zhu accepted the principle of that compensation; its particulars went back to the negotiators. The revealing change was therefore not a last-minute abandonment of a Chinese ownership limit. It was the construction of an acceptable package around it—and authorization on both sides to accept that package. This detailed sequence comes from a Chinese collection published in 2013, not independently released European minutes. [EWT-01, printed pp 383–385/PDF 418–420](https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/dl/sms/zhj_1_1.pdf).

The comparison with the American agreement of November 1999 is unusually close: the same Chinese leadership, accession campaign and overlapping commercial sectors. But it is sequential, not experimental. Europe negotiated after Washington had obtained commitments from which other WTO members could benefit; European firms had different priorities; accession still required multilateral completion. Differences between the two bargains cannot simply be assigned to different national negotiating styles. Nor should the EU be treated as one more nation-state: its Commission negotiated amid the interests and authorization requirements of fifteen member states.

## The American baseline was an asset, not the end of the European negotiation

In the Chinese history’s account of 24–26 January 2000, EU chief negotiator Hans-Friedrich Beseler explained that Europe had reduced its tariff request list from more than 580 lines to 150. He invoked most-favored-nation treatment and proposed proceeding on the basis of the American agreement, while seeking comparable satisfaction of European priorities. Long Yongtu replied that some concessions to Washington already reflected European concerns, and that protecting sensitive sectors had required greater concessions elsewhere. The prior agreement thus did two things: it supplied a floor from which Europeans could demand improvements, and it supplied Chinese negotiators with an argument that particular limits had already been politically settled. Neither effect made the remaining bargain automatic. [EWT-01, p 370/PDF 405](https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/dl/sms/zhj_1_1.pdf).

This dated account also qualifies a later participant’s generalization. Banking negotiator Deng Hongguo recalled that Europe would not take the US agreement as the basis of its own. Beseler’s reported January language says the opposite. The accounts can partly be reconciled by distinguishing acceptance of a baseline from acceptance of its sufficiency, but the discrepancy should remain visible. Deng’s retrospective summary cannot replace the dated exchange. [EWT-13, p 295, with the earlier participant audit](../sources/wto-financial-participant-note.md).

The surviving February technical package makes this institutional bargaining concrete. Its goods page reserved tariff and procurement annexes; its services page distinguished items for chief negotiators from telecommunications and insurance issues left open. It proposed immediate opening of Zhuhai to RMB banking, dropping an EU request to advance Beijing, and a staged relaxation of chain-store limits. These are proposed terms, despite some sentences beginning “China confirms.” Handwritten marks have no identified author or agreed significance. The reproduced pages lack the referenced tariff and procurement annexes. Treating every checked line as a concluded concession would turn a negotiating document into an agreement it does not claim to be. [EWT-01, captioned 23 February, pp 374–375/PDF 409–410](https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/dl/sms/zhj_1_1.pdf).

The distinction between technical and political issues was itself negotiated. The Chinese history says Beseler insisted that the technical list be cleared before Lamy visited; China said it could not accept every item. The anticipated February visit did not occur. In January Lamy had already said he wanted a visit that would produce an agreement and that he needed to work with member-state ministers. This is evidence of the counterpart’s management of authority and exposure to failure, alongside China’s domestic constraints. [EWT-01, pp 371–373/PDF 406–408](https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/dl/sms/zhj_1_1.pdf).

## March is the necessary unsuccessful comparison

Lamy did visit in late March, and Zhu met him on 29 March. The Chinese record again separates highly sensitive ownership and state-trading questions from matters on which China could compensate European commercial interests. A surviving English page of China’s 30 March package retained a 50 percent auto joint-venture ceiling and a 25 percent tariff by 1 July 2006. It offered earlier elimination of oil quotas and licensing but explicitly left timing for further negotiation while retaining state trading. This is documentary evidence of conditional and incomplete offers, not an analyst’s reconstruction from eventual WTO commitments. The reproduced page ends during its tariff paragraph; the full Chinese package is not present there. [EWT-01, pp 376–378/PDF 411–413](https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/dl/sms/zhj_1_1.pdf).

Europe also moved. The Chinese collection’s transcription of the EU’s 30 March response records a revised wine request of 15 percent, between an earlier European demand of 10 and a then-current Chinese offer of 20. For mobile telecommunications it offered alternative combinations of ownership and time: one route reached 50 percent after five years; another accepted 49 percent after three years with 35 percent immediately. Life insurance alternatives traded an eventual 51 percent ceiling against retention of 50 percent combined with specified wholly owned branches. These were alternatives within a package, not a series of cumulative Chinese concessions. [EWT-01, Box 5-10, pp 379–380/PDF 414–415](https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/dl/sms/zhj_1_1.pdf).

The round failed despite detailed drafts, ministerial bargaining and premier-level attention. The Chinese history attributes to Lamy a need to obtain authority for accepting the life-insurance and automobile ceilings. An official British parliamentary account of the 10–11 April General Affairs Council subsequently reports support for his approach and a call for reciprocal Chinese flexibility; it does not disclose the private mandate. This negative case narrows any claim that senior Chinese involvement reliably signals imminent settlement. The senior meeting had occurred; the mutually acceptable package and authorization had not. [EWT-01, p 380/PDF 415](https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/dl/sms/zhj_1_1.pdf); [EWT-06, UK report, paragraph 7](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/written-answers/2000/apr/18/general-affairs-council-luxembourg).

## May: authority could travel in both directions

Beijing publicly wanted progress. At his 8 May meeting with Denmark’s prime minister, Zhu expressed hope for an early agreement and that Europe would not be the last counterpart to finish. This is contemporary evidence of Chinese interest in closure, not proof of a binding deadline or capitulation. Technical discussions resumed on 11–13 May; the ministerial round began on 15 May. Chinese reporting on 18 May described political and technical tracks operating in parallel, while acknowledging that reporters outside the venue could not establish whether agreement was imminent. Hotel reservations and optimistic rumors are weak substitutes for negotiating records. [EWT-07, event 8 May; webpage publication 7 November](https://www.fmprc.gov.cn/web/gjhdq_676201/gj_676203/oz_678770/1206_679062/xgxw_679068/200011/t20001107_9333636.shtml); [EWT-08](https://news.sina.com.cn/china/2000-05-15/89743.html); [EWT-09](https://news.sina.com.cn/society/2000-05-18/90471.html).

The Chinese history reports that Lamy arrived with authority to accept the 50 percent life-insurance ceiling if compensated, and to accept the auto ceiling if tariffs improved. Mobile telecommunications remained harder. A reproduced English outline, captioned May 2000, proposed 50 percent after five years and explicitly made concessions conditional on satisfying requests on its second page. That second page is not reproduced. The first page lists several prior European positions and alternatives, making it especially unsafe to collapse the negotiation into a single starting demand. It is also not precisely dated enough to assign it to a particular May meeting. [EWT-01, pp 381–382/PDF 416–417](https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/dl/sms/zhj_1_1.pdf).

On the Chinese account, Lamy said on 17 May that he had understood Zhu’s March remarks as allowing a 50 percent mobile ceiling. Acceptance of 49 required new authority. On 18 May he sought confirmation from a Chinese leader that he could take back to Brussels. That evening the sides still differed over immediate mobile equity, chain-store treatment and the number of insurance licenses. Shi Guangsheng promised further coordination but warned that room was limited; the next morning he reported that coordination had not improved the Chinese offer. This establishes an unsuccessful attempt to improve the offer through coordination; it does not identify the reason or demonstrate disagreement within the leadership. [EWT-01, pp 383–385/PDF 418–420](https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/dl/sms/zhj_1_1.pdf).

Lamy then accepted 25 percent mobile ownership initially and reduced other requests. At the 19 May meeting Zhu characterized 49 percent as a collective government decision. Lamy reported consultations with member states and industry and sought compensating terms; Zhu accepted the principle, leaving specifics to Shi. The meeting supplied confirmation Lamy had requested before accepting 49 percent. It may have helped him secure or exercise a conditional mandate, but the record does not establish exactly when Brussels authorized acceptance or whether the meeting caused that authorization. Internal EU instructions and Chinese decision papers are needed to distinguish these possibilities from each other and from a last-minute release of new Chinese concessions.

## Which terms actually changed?

The Commission’s 2001 explanatory memorandum describes the resulting accession package from a European standpoint. The WTO’s October 2001 services schedule supplies the eventual legal terms. Neither alone establishes every concession made specifically on 19 May. The following comparisons preserve that distinction.

Deng’s Zhuhai account is particularly useful because he identifies a decision level below the premier: he says he obtained PBOC leadership approval and accepted the Portuguese request, which he judged not substantive. That evaluation should not become ours without examining affected banks and the commercial value of earlier entry. Aaditya Mattoo’s contemporaneous analytical treatment explains why geographically staggered opening can have persistent effects: earlier entry may attract investment and establish positions that later nationwide opening does not fully erase. This is a mechanism to test, not evidence that Zhuhai actually experienced such an effect. [EWT-13, p 295](../sources/wto-financial-participant-note.md); [EWT-14, pp 18,22/PDF 21,25](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/231201468770437827/pdf/multi0page.pdf).

The same restraint applies to aggregate tariff claims. The Commission reports an average reduction from 18.6 to 10.9 percent on 150 EU-priority industrial products and describes further reductions relative to earlier offers. That selected basket is not the average of all Chinese tariffs and is not directly comparable with an American priority-product average. Without identical tariff lines, product weights, starting dates and phase-in dates, those figures cannot rank the two delegations’ negotiating effectiveness. [EWT-02, paragraph 21](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:52001PC0517).

## Agreement did not end the accession bargain

One European gain was explicitly outside the accession commitments: the Commission records seven new insurance licenses—five life and two non-life—and two additional sub-branches to be granted before accession. Its October 2000 memorandum sought dates for five *remaining* new licenses and two sub-branches. Those are different counts and categories, not contradictory accounts of a single seven-license commitment. The available records do not establish the final completion date of each authorization. Delayed confirmation cannot by itself prove breach, nor can a public promise establish that firms had begun operating. [EWT-02, paragraph 37](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:52001PC0517); [EWT-10, pp 1–2](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/api/files/document/print/en/memo_00_67/MEMO_00_67_EN.pdf).

That October memorandum also reports slow multilateral progress across several working sessions and identifies matters involving ministries beyond MOFTEC. The Commission reported China’s accusation that members sought commitments beyond normal accession requirements, while members described their work as clarification. The June 2001 USTR release likewise reports further US–China negotiations over agriculture, trading rights, insurance and retailing. These unilateral accounts establish continued bargaining and disagreements about interpretation. They do not establish, without the exchanged drafts, that every clarification was an attempt to withdraw a shared commitment. [EWT-10, pp 1–2](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/api/files/document/print/en/memo_00_67/MEMO_00_67_EN.pdf); [EWT-11, USTR release 01-38, archival mirror](https://usinfo.org/wf-archive/2001/010614/epf404.htm).

The final insurance provisions illustrate the legal detail at stake. A horizontal commitment protects the existing ownership, operation and scope granted in contracts or licenses at accession. A separate insurance note makes subsequently granted more favorable authorizations available to other foreign suppliers on request. These clauses address existing entitlements and future treatment differently. Mattoo identifies the politically sensitive asymmetry between previously authorized foreign insurers and new 50-percent ventures, but his discussion does not settle every dispute over whether a new branch inherits an old company’s privileged status. That requires the license, application and relevant domestic rule. [EWT-03, p 3 and p 30 note 8](https://docs.wto.org/dol2fe/Pages/SS/directdoc.aspx?filename=Q:/WT/ACC/CHN49A2.pdf&Open=True); [EWT-14, pp 12–13/PDF 15–16](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/231201468770437827/pdf/multi0page.pdf).

Europe also undertook obligations. The textile exchange of letters initialled on 19 May specified how European restrictions would enter WTO phase-out arrangements, and how the bilateral agreements would extend if accession occurred after 2000. Its administrative annex retained Chinese export-management duties alongside European procedures. Council Decision 2000/787 authorized signing and reciprocal provisional application; Parliament’s October 2001 accession resolution approved the Community position. These are distinct legal and institutional acts. They undermine a literal reading of rhetoric that the bargain required nothing of the existing members, without establishing equal concession values on each side. The textile annex’s tabular category schedule has not been independently inspected here; no product-level quota calculation is offered. [EWT-15, letters paragraphs 3–8 and annex prose](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:22000A1214(01)); [EWT-16](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32000D0787); [EWT-17](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/resource.html?format=PDF&uri=cellar%3A8d26f810-b893-4166-8f18-d95cce0de46f.0004.01%2FDOC_75).

## What this changes in the Solomon reassessment

The case supports **revising the mechanism** behind senior intervention, S-P24. The operative question is what authorization changed, for whom, and on which terms. March supplies contrary evidence to a simple forecast of imminent agreement from leader involvement; May identifies authoritative confirmation of a limit as something the counterpart requested before accepting it; its effect on the timing of European authorization remains a hypothesis. Solomon’s discussion of mandates and coordination, S-C09–12, remains useful. The record does not warrant diagnosing every unyielding position as Chinese leadership conflict under S-C19.

For S-P12, the record **retains within scope** the importance of partial understandings, reservations and wording, while adding unusually explicit technical evidence: “agreed” items coexist with unresolved annexes, alternatives and package conditions. For S-P05, it **narrows** any broad inference that Chinese negotiators prefer principles instead of operational precision. Detailed schedules and drafting were central, but commercial detail does not itself refute a proposition about the political use of broad principles. The appropriate comparison preserves that scope.

For S-P19, invoking the American bargain was observable, but it operated within MFN accession negotiations and was done by both parties. It is **unresolved** whether this demonstrates a distinct Chinese propensity to restate shared understandings favorably: each disputed prior commitment needs its own text. For S-P23, Chinese interest in timely closure is visible, yet the specific causal effect of the approaching American congressional vote remains **unresolved**. Later Chinese secondary literature describes a European strategy of borrowing American pressure, but partly depends on memoirs and press accounts not independently recovered here. No counterfactual shows which concessions would have disappeared without that vote. [EWT-18, selected WTO section, printedpp 10–13/PDF 11–14](https://www.sies.org.cn/uploadfile/file/european%20survey/202112020857569596_%E3%80%8A%E6%AC%A7%E6%B4%B2%E8%A7%82%E5%AF%9F%E3%80%8B2021%E5%B9%B4%E7%AC%AC4%E6%9C%9F%281%29.pdf.pdf).

For S-P25, the continued arena of bargaining is **retained within scope**, but a specifically asymmetric Chinese claim to revise obligations is **unresolved**. Members pressed for clarification too; regulatory interpretation, accession completion and later compliance are separate activities. Mattoo’s distinction between a binding commitment and actual liberalization is especially useful here: an opening bound in a schedule does not measure the competition that emerges, while weak regulatory institutions need not imply a deliberate negotiating deception. His working paper is an analytical interpretation, not independent evidence of the May meetings.

The remaining evidentiary limits materially constrain these verdicts. The Chinese collection supplies detailed sequences and valuable English facsimiles, but its narration and selected documents are not independent witnesses. Commission summaries advocate the agreement and contain apparent discrepancies: paragraph 42 gives three years for Chinese-enterprise RMB access, while the final schedule gives two; paragraph 35 gives one year for non-life business scope, while the schedule gives two. Lapres’s early practitioner comparison is valuable but also compresses and sometimes misstates terms. Neither the full signed EU bilateral package and annexes nor internal European and Chinese mandates have been acquired. Beseler’s participant chapter remains an acquisition lead, not a read source. No interviews were conducted.

What survives this audit is a more precise historical conclusion: the accession-era Chinese delegation combined defended ownership limits with substantial bargaining over timing, geography, product scope and administrative implementation. The European comparison supplies no evidence that Beijing possessed a timeless, uniform script or that the Europeans escaped it. It shows how negotiators on both sides used prior bargains, conditional packages, domestic constraints and higher authority to assemble an agreement—and why the exact document version determines what can responsibly be called a concession.
