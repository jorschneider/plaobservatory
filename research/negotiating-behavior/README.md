# Chinese negotiating behavior: reassessing Solomon through 2026

**Foundation dossier — 5 September 2026.** This project asks which conclusions in Richard H. Solomon's *Chinese Negotiating Behavior* remain useful, which need revision, and under what circumstances Chinese behavior toward major powers has changed. The United States is the main comparison; other major counterparts are necessary controls. This is original research and commentary, not a reproduction of the book.

The initial work has three foundations: a sequential audit of the supplied book, including Charles W. Freeman Jr.'s 1999 commentary; a documentary pilot on the 1999 US–China WTO accession negotiation; and a secondary-literature program that tests competing explanations and identifies additional sources. The aim is to reconstruct offers, instructions, disputes, and decisions before drawing conclusions from outcomes.

## What has already changed our approach?

**The baseline is earlier than the printing date.** Solomon's central study concerns 1967–1984. He explicitly leaves open whether the findings transfer to renewed confrontation, limits what negotiating tactics can accomplish, and recognizes bureaucratic constraints. Freeman already identifies institutional specialization, learning, and counterpart-specific practices. An update should not rediscover those points and call them new.

**There is a substantial Chinese documentary starting point.** The MOFCOM collection publishes selected archival documents, including dated proposals and the signed April 10, 1999 MOU body. Its editorial narrative, original facsimiles, translations, and later participant accounts need separate treatment. Access to the collection is not access to every internal decision.

**Draft status and authority are central.** The April MOU names unresolved issues; November offers distinguish ownership, management control, phase-ins, and safeguards. Chinese sector testimony describes approval chains; American letters impose deadlines and request senior Chinese intervention. These records complicate a story told solely through psychological tactics or the final result. Details and qualifications are in the [first synthesis](cases/wto-1999-synthesis.md).

## The next research step

Read the scholarly literature in parallel with further documentary work. Begin with Zhang Qingmin's continuity/change chapter, Shaffer and Gao on trade-law learning, and Johnston on measuring apparent change; obtain Pearson's accession chapter and the relevant chapters of Faure and Zartman's 2025 volume. Use their evidence and disagreements to refine the archive requests below. The [literature plan](literature/README.md) separates immediate public reading from the first full-text requests.

Recover the **April 10 MOU attachments**, the **NEC–NSC memorandum behind the April decision**, the **successive November offers and final bilateral schedules**, and the **Chinese authorization records identified by sector participants**. Build a dated comparison for telecom, life insurance, bank/auto-finance access, and safeguards. These records can distinguish an actual reversal from a conditional draft, a genuine mandate limit from bargaining tactics, and a material concession from retrospective compression.

Then extend the method to the EU accession talks and the 2018–2026 US trade negotiations, using Chinese white papers alongside underlying agreements, original reporting, counterpart records, and implementation evidence. Additional major-power and crisis cases will test whether findings travel beyond accession and commerce.

The 2026 cutoff is the intended endpoint of the study. **The update through 2026 is not yet complete.** Later white papers and readouts are initial source leads with selective reading, not a researched account of every intervening negotiation. No new interviews, archival requests, or correspondence have been conducted. Downloaded source books and full transcripts are kept outside the public repository.
